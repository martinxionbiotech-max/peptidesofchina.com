Epitalon is a synthetic four-amino-acid peptide — Ala-Glu-Asp-Gly — supplied as a lyophilized powder for cell-culture and preclinical research. Its small size makes it cheap to synthesize, which is exactly why catalogue listings are hard to compare: the sequence is trivial, the documentation is not. A 390 Da tetrapeptide has no aromatic residue, so the analytical methods used for larger peptides need adjusting, and the counterion can account for a large share of the fill weight. This page covers the research background, the batch records worth requesting, and the handling details that affect assay reproducibility.

## Why This Compound

Epitalon comes out of the peptide bioregulator programme led by Vladimir Khavinson at the St. Petersburg Institute of Bioregulation and Gerontology. The story starts with epithalamin, a polypeptide fraction prepared from bovine pineal glands, and narrows to a four-residue fragment — Ala-Glu-Asp-Gly, often shortened to AEDG — that appeared to carry part of the parent extract's activity. Almost every foundational paper on the synthetic tetrapeptide traces back to that laboratory or its close collaborators, which is the first thing a careful reader should notice. The literature is real, but it is concentrated.

The research themes are narrow and repeat across the papers. In cultured human fibroblasts and retinal pigment epithelial cells, treatment has been reported to increase hTERT expression, raise telomerase activity in TRAP assays, and extend the number of population doublings before replicative senescence. A 2025 study in *Biogerontology* complicates the standard summary: telomere lengthening in human cell lines was attributed either to telomerase upregulation or to alternative lengthening of telomeres, depending on the line. That distinction matters, because "telomerase activator" is a cleaner label than the data support.

A second line concerns the pineal axis. Because the parent extract came from pineal tissue, researchers examined melatonin synthesis and circadian endpoints in aged rodents, including AANAT activity in pinealocyte cultures. A third line looks at antioxidant enzymes — catalase, superoxide dismutase, lipid peroxidation markers — in hepatocyte, lymphocyte, and erythrocyte models. A fourth uses tumour-prone mouse strains and reports changes in spontaneous tumour incidence over long observation periods. Each of these is a preclinical observation with its own model limitations, not a property of the vial on your bench.

For a sourcing decision, the research history matters less than the chemistry. AEDG has two acidic residues and no tryptophan, tyrosine, or phenylalanine. It does not absorb meaningfully at 280 nm. It is highly hydrophilic and elutes early on a standard reversed-phase column unless an ion-pairing agent is used. It is small enough that residual salts and water form a large fraction of any fill. Those four facts drive nearly every documentation question on this page. Two practical consequences follow from the structure. The peptide has no chromophore worth using for detection, so purity is measured by UV absorbance at 214 nm, where the peptide bond absorbs, or by charged aerosol detection. And because the molecule is only 390 Da, a counterion such as acetate or trifluoroacetate is not a trace component; it is a stoichiometric part of the salt. A certificate that reports only HPLC area percent tells you about the peptide peak, not about how much peptide the vial contains.

## What the Batch Record Should Show

A useful Epitalon certificate answers four questions in order: what the molecule is, how pure the peptide peak is, what else is in the vial, and whether the document belongs to the lot that shipped.

**Identity by mass.** The free base has a monoisotopic mass near 390.14 Da and an average mass of 390.35 Da; the singly protonated ion appears near m/z 391.15. Small hydrophilic peptides ionise well in electrospray, and sodium adducts at +22 Da are common, so a spectrum should show the protonated species and its adducts rather than a single clean peak. A certificate that prints "molecular weight 390.35" without a spectrum has quoted the catalogue value. It has not confirmed the batch.

**Purity by HPLC with method detail.** Ask for the column, mobile phase, gradient, wavelength, and integration parameters. At 214 nm, area normalisation reports the peptide peak as a percentage of UV-absorbing material. It does not see acetate, trifluoroacetate, water, or inorganic salts. For a tetrapeptide, deletion sequences differ by one residue — roughly 71 to 129 Da — and many of them will be resolved only if the gradient is shallow enough. A 10-minute generic gradient can hide them under the main peak.

**Counterion and residual acid.** Epitalon is commonly supplied as the acetate salt. Trifluoroacetate can remain from reversed-phase purification, and it is both a mass contributor and a biologically active impurity in cell culture at high concentration. The certificate should name the counterion and, where relevant, report residual TFA. If the document is silent, the "10 mg" on the label probably refers to total vial content rather than peptide mass. The [counterion note](/blog/role-of-counterions-in-peptide-stability/) explains why the salt form changes both mass and solution behaviour.

**Water content and peptide content.** Karl Fischer titration gives water. Nitrogen analysis or amino acid analysis gives peptide content. Both are necessary to convert fill weight into molar amount. A lyophilized tetrapeptide is hygroscopic, so water content can drift after the vial is opened, and a certificate measured at release may not describe the vial six months later.

**Appearance and batch linkage.** The cake should be a white to off-white plug with no shrinkage, melting, or discoloration. The batch number on the certificate must match the vial label, and the document should carry a synthesis date and a retest date. A certificate that could describe any Epitalon lot is a template, not a record.

## Sourcing & Verification

Epitalon is one of the cheapest peptides in the catalogue to make, and that shapes the supplier landscape. A short sequence, standard solid-phase chemistry, and no difficult purification steps mean many trading companies can buy vials from a handful of factories and relabel them. The questions below separate a manufacturer from a relabeller.

- Does the certificate print the full AEDG sequence, or only the trade name? A supplier that cannot show four residues is not describing a batch.
- Is the reported mass the free base or an acetate salt? The two differ by the counterion, and the label may not say which was weighed.
- Was purity measured at 214 nm, and what was the gradient? If the method says 280 nm, the data are not measuring this peptide.
- What is the peptide content in mg per vial, and by which method?
- Is residual TFA reported? For cell-based assays, this is not a cosmetic detail.
- Does the chromatogram show the main peak at a plausible retention time for a hydrophilic tetrapeptide, or does it look like a generic printout?
- Can the supplier name the synthesis site and provide a batch-specific certificate rather than a product-specific one?
- Has the lot been tested for endotoxin if it will contact primary cells?

Names are another source of confusion. Epitalon, Epithalon, and Epithalone are spelling variants of the same synthetic tetrapeptide. Epithalamin is not the same substance: it is the bovine pineal extract from which the sequence was derived, and a listing that uses the two names interchangeably is either careless or hoping the buyer will not notice. The [CoA reading guide](/blog/how-to-read-a-peptide-coa-a-researchers-guide-to-hplc-and-ms-reports/) walks through the fields in more detail, and the [identity-verification note](/blog/peptide-molecular-weight-verification-steps/) explains why a catalogue mass is not a measurement.

One more check is worth doing before a first order: ask for the certificate of a lot that has already shipped, not a specimen document. Suppliers often keep a clean example for inquiries. Comparing that example with the certificate that arrives with your order tells you whether documentation is generated per batch or per product.

## Handling & Storage

Lyophilized Epitalon is stable for extended periods at −20°C when kept dry and protected from light. The handling risks are ordinary but consequential.

Bring the vial to room temperature before you open it. A cold vial attracts condensation, and the powder is hygroscopic; water taken up at the bench is water that will not appear in the release certificate. Wipe the outside, wait at least twenty minutes, and open in a low-humidity space.

For cell-culture work, dissolve in sterile water, phosphate-buffered saline, or the assay medium. The two acidic residues make the peptide readily water-soluble, so DMSO is usually unnecessary. Prepare a stock no more concentrated than the assay can tolerate, because small peptides can adsorb to plastic at low concentration; use low-binding tubes and avoid transferring stock through multiple containers. Add solvent gently down the wall and swirl rather than vortex. Foam at the air-liquid interface is a sign of denaturation or aggregation, and neither improves an assay.

Aliquot before freezing. Aqueous solutions kept at 2–8°C are short-term working stocks; repeated freeze-thaw cycles promote aggregation and adsorption. Divide into single-use portions, freeze at −80°C for long-term storage, and do not refreeze. If the assay requires sterile solution, filter through a 0.22 µm polyethersulfone membrane and check recovery, since small peptides can bind to some filter materials.

All handling described here is bench-scale laboratory work. ## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is Epitalon the same as Epithalon?**

Yes. Epitalon, Epithalon, and Epithalone are spelling variants used for the same synthetic tetrapeptide, Ala-Glu-Asp-Gly. The differences are transliteration and catalogue convention, not chemistry. A supplier should still print the sequence on the certificate, because a name alone does not identify a batch.

**Is Epitalon the same as epithalamin?**

No. Epithalamin is a polypeptide fraction prepared from bovine pineal glands. Epitalon is a synthetic four-residue fragment derived from that work. They differ in mass, composition, and documentation, and a listing that treats the names as synonyms is not precise enough for research procurement.

**What purity should a research-grade batch meet?**

The catalogue specification is ≥98% by HPLC at 214 nm. Purity by area normalisation is a starting point, not a complete specification. A useful release also reports peptide content, counterion, water content, and a batch-specific mass spectrum.

**Why is there no 280 nm purity figure?**

Because the sequence contains no tryptophan, tyrosine, or phenylalanine. There is no aromatic chromophore to absorb at 280 nm, so a purity value measured at that wavelength would be measuring noise or buffer components. Reports for this peptide should use 214 or 220 nm, or a universal detector.

**What counterion is typical for Epitalon?**

Acetate is the most common catalogue form. Trifluoroacetate can remain from purification, and the certificate should say which one applies. The counterion changes the mass of material in the vial and can matter in cell-based assays.

**How is Epitalon supplied?**

As a lyophilized powder in sealed vials, typically 10 mg, 40 mg, or 50 mg per vial in 10-vial packs. The catalogue lists current formats. The certificate should state peptide content rather than only fill weight.

**How should a stock solution be stored?**

Prepare a single-use stock, aliquot it, and freeze at −80°C. Avoid repeated freeze-thaw cycles and keep working solutions at 2–8°C for short periods. Use low-binding tubes, since small peptides can adsorb to plastic.

**Can I have a batch tested independently?**

Yes, and for a first order it is a reasonable check. Request the raw chromatogram and mass spectrum along with the certificate, then have a third-party laboratory confirm identity and peptide content. ## The Author's Take

**Position.** I would not choose an Epitalon supplier on price per vial. For a 390 Da salt, the fill weight is a poor proxy for peptide content, so the cheapest vial is often the one with the most counterion and the least documentation.

**Reasoning.** Epitalon is easy to synthesize, which means the market is full of product that is chemically plausible but analytically opaque. The failure modes are quiet: a certificate measured at a wavelength the peptide does not absorb, a "10 mg" label that describes total fill rather than peptide, a counterion that changes from lot to lot, and a chromatogram with a gradient too steep to resolve deletion sequences. None of those problems shows up in a purity number. They show up when an assay gives a different answer with a new vial. A supplier who can produce a mass spectrum, a method description, and a peptide-content value is demonstrating process control. A supplier who cannot is asking you to trust a template.
