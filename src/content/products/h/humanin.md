Humanin is a 24-amino-acid peptide encoded in the mitochondrial 16S rRNA region, which makes it an oddity in a catalogue organised around synthetic analogues of human hormones. It was discovered in 2001 in a screen for factors that protect neurons from death triggered by familial Alzheimer's disease genes, and the early papers gave it a narrative that has been hard to shake: a mitochondrial survival peptide with a possible role in ageing. The narrative is interesting. The evidence is mostly cell culture and rodent work, and the commercial literature often runs ahead of both.

This page treats humanin as a laboratory reagent. It covers the biology that makes it useful, the analytical records that establish identity, the handling issues created by a single free cysteine, and the comparisons with other mitochondrial-derived peptides. It is supplied for in vitro and preclinical research only, and nothing here is a claim about human health or longevity.

## Why This Compound

The discovery paper by Hashimoto and colleagues described a peptide that blocked neuronal death induced by multiple familial Alzheimer's disease genes and by amyloid-beta. That was an unusual result: a small mitochondrial-encoded peptide acting outside the mitochondrion, apparently through a receptor or an intracellular partner. The follow-up work gave two mechanistic threads. Guo and colleagues showed that humanin interferes with Bax activation, which connects it to the intrinsic apoptosis pathway. Ikonen and colleagues showed that humanin binds insulin-like growth factor-binding protein 3, which links it to the IGF-1/IGFBP system and to cell survival signalling.

Those two threads explain why humanin appears in several otherwise separate research literatures. In apoptosis work, it is a tool for probing Bax-dependent death and mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilisation. In IGF signalling work, it is a peptide that competes with IGF-1 for IGFBP-3 binding, changing the local availability of the growth factor. In metabolic work, it has been studied in rodent models of insulin sensitivity and inflammation, though the mechanisms are not settled. In ageing biology, it is one of a small family of mitochondrial-derived peptides whose circulating levels change with age, a correlation that is interesting but not the same as an intervention.

The family matters for interpretation. Humanin is not the only mitochondrial-derived peptide: MOTS-c, SHLP2, SHLP6 and others are encoded in different mitochondrial regions and have different sequences and activities. Papers sometimes use "humanin" and "mitochondrial-derived peptide" interchangeably, which is a mistake. A supplier listing that says "humanin" but describes MOTS-c biology is a warning sign, and a researcher citing a MOTS-c result as humanin evidence is making a category error.

There is also a practical reason humanin is worth a careful page. It is a short peptide with one cysteine, no disulfide partner and a hydrophobic stretch in the middle. That combination creates a specific set of analytical problems: dimerisation through the free thiol, aggregation at higher concentrations, and adsorption to surfaces. A certificate that reports only a mass and a purity peak will not tell you whether half the vial is dimer.

## What the Batch Record Should Show

Humanin is small enough that the entire sequence can be confirmed by mass spectrometry, and the certificate should be read for the things a generic peptide template omits.

**Identity and sequence coverage.** The monoisotopic mass of the reduced peptide is about 2,686 Da. MS/MS should cover the chain, including the N-terminal methionine and the C-terminal alanine. A certificate that reports only "MW 2687.2" has quoted the catalogue. The N-terminus is not acetylated in the standard synthetic form, and the C-terminus is free; both should be confirmed because modifications shift the mass and can change activity.

**Cysteine status and dimer content.** Humanin has a single cysteine and no intramolecular disulfide partner. In solution, the free thiol can oxidise to form a disulfide-linked dimer, and the dimer is a different reagent. The certificate should report monomer content by RP-HPLC or SE-HPLC and identify any dimer peak. If the supplier does not mention it, ask directly. This is the most common quality gap for humanin.

**Purity with method detail.** RP-HPLC at 214 nm is standard. Look for a clean main peak and for shoulders that could be oxidised or truncated forms. Area percent purity can hide a small dimer peak if the gradient is shallow or the integration is generous.

**Counterion and content.** Acetate or trifluoroacetate, stated on the certificate. Peptide content per vial by amino acid analysis or nitrogen determination. A 10 mg fill with 80 percent peptide content is an 8 mg vial, and the difference matters when preparing a stock.

**Endotoxin and sterility.** Humanin is used in primary neuronal and cell-line experiments, both of which are sensitive to endotoxin. LAL testing and a sterility result should be in the package. The [endotoxin explainer](/blog/what-are-endotoxins-why-they-matter/) covers the assay logic.

**Traceability.** Lot number, synthesis date, retest date, manufacturing site. The [CoA guide](/blog/how-to-read-a-peptide-coa-a-researchers-guide-to-hplc-and-ms-reports/) explains how to check that the document belongs to the vial.

## Sourcing & Verification

Humanin is not an expensive peptide to synthesise, but it is a peptide that attracts a particular kind of catalogue copy: mitochondrial, longevity, neuroprotection. The science is interesting enough without the marketing, and the buyer's job is to separate the two.

Questions worth asking:

- Is the sequence written out, and does the observed mass match the expected 24-residue peptide?
- Is the N-terminus free, and is the C-terminus free or modified?
- What percentage of the batch is monomer, and is dimer reported?
- Is the counterion stated, and is residual TFA measured?
- What is the peptide content per vial, and how was it measured?
- Was endotoxin tested, and by which method?
- Does the supplier manufacture the peptide, or buy bulk powder and aliquot it?

A supplier who can show a chromatogram with a resolved dimer peak and a mass spectrum with sequence coverage is telling you something. A supplier who sends a one-page certificate with "purity 99%" and no dimer data is asking you to assume the hard part was done. The [peptide testing guide](/blog/peptide-testing-and-analysis-a-professional-guide-to-laboratory-verification/) and the [quality pillar](/peptide-quality-testing/) cover the orthogonal methods.

One naming check: humanin is sometimes listed alongside "humanin analogue" or "HNG" constructs. Those are engineered variants with different sequences and, in some cases, different receptor pharmacology. A catalogue that uses the names interchangeably is not a reliable source for a defined reagent.

## Handling & Storage

Lyophilised humanin is stored at −20°C, dry and protected from light. Let the vial warm to room temperature before opening. Reconstitute with water or buffer down the vial wall and swirl gently; do not vortex, because the hydrophobic stretch promotes aggregation at the air-liquid interface.

The free cysteine is the handling issue. Avoid buffers containing oxidising agents, and avoid prolonged exposure to air in an open vessel. If the experiment requires a reducing environment, note that DTT or TCEP will keep the peptide monomeric but may interfere with the assay; a fresh preparation is usually the better answer. Do not store a reconstituted solution for long periods at neutral pH, where thiol oxidation is fastest.

Humanin adsorbs to plastic and to some filter membranes. Use low-binding tubes, and check recovery after filtration. For cell culture, filter through a 0.22 µm membrane if sterility is required, and confirm that the peptide does not stick to the membrane. If the solution becomes cloudy, it may be aggregation rather than contamination; dilute or adjust the buffer and centrifuge before assuming the worst.

Short-term solutions are held at 4°C. For longer storage, aliquot and freeze at −20°C, avoiding repeated freeze-thaw. The [reconstitution guide](/blog/peptide-lyophilization-reconstitution-guide-for-researchers/) and the [handling pillar](/peptide-handling-protocols/) cover the general workflow. The points above describe laboratory handling only, not any clinical use.

## How It Compares

**SHLP2 and SHLP6.** Small humanin-like peptides encoded in the same mitochondrial region, with different sequences and different reported activities. They are not interchangeable with humanin, and a study that uses one should not be cited as evidence for another. The [mitochondrial peptide literature](/blog/therapeutic-peptide-classification-a-2026-research-guide/) is still working out the family relationships.

**MOTS-c.** A 16-residue mitochondrial-derived peptide encoded in the 12S rRNA region, studied mainly in metabolic and exercise biology. It is the most common alternative to humanin in the same research conversations, and the mechanisms are different. The [MOTS-c listing](/catalog/mots-c/) gives the catalogue format.

**SS-31 (elamipretide).** A synthetic tetrapeptide that targets cardiolipin and the inner mitochondrial membrane. It is not a mitochondrial-encoded peptide and does not share humanin's sequence or proposed receptor. The comparison is useful mainly to show how broad the "mitochondrial peptide" category has become, and the [SS-31 page](/catalog/ss-31/) is the catalogue entry.

**IGF-1 LR3.** A growth factor analogue that acts on the IGF-1 receptor. Humanin binds IGFBP-3, which modulates IGF availability, so the two appear in the same pathway conversations, but they are not substitutes. The [IGF-1 LR3 page](/catalog/igf-1-lr3/) covers the receptor side.

**BPC-157 and other repair peptides.** Frequently listed together in catalogue navigation, with no mechanistic overlap. Humanin's literature is about apoptosis and mitochondrial stress, not tissue repair. The [BPC-157 page](/catalog/bpc-157/) is a different research area entirely.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is humanin?**

A 24-amino-acid peptide encoded in the mitochondrial 16S rRNA region. It was identified as a factor that protects cells from apoptosis in certain disease-gene models, and it has since been studied in mitochondrial stress, IGFBP-3 binding and metabolic research.

**Is humanin a peptide or a protein?**

A peptide. It is short enough to be made by solid-phase synthesis and is supplied as a lyophilised powder. The "mitochondrial-derived" part describes its genetic origin, not its size.

**Why does the certificate need to report dimer content?**

Because humanin has one free cysteine and no intramolecular disulfide partner, so it can form disulfide-linked dimers in solution. A dimer is a different reagent with different assay behaviour. A certificate that reports only a mass and a purity peak may be hiding the dimer.

**What purity is supplied?**

The catalogue specification is ≥98 percent by RP-HPLC. For humanin, monomer content and peptide content are the more informative numbers, and the certificate should report both.

**How should it be stored and reconstituted?**

Store the powder at −20°C, dry and protected from light. Reconstitute gently with water or buffer, avoid vortexing, and keep the solution at 4°C for short-term use. Avoid oxidising conditions and repeated freeze-thaw.

**Can humanin be used in cell culture?**

Yes, in neuronal, fibroblast and other cell systems, provided the preparation is sterile-filtered and endotoxin-tested. It is not sterile by default.

**Is humanin an anti-ageing compound?**

No. Ageing biology is a research area in which humanin has been studied, and circulating levels change with age in some datasets. That is a correlation, not evidence of a clinical effect, and this page makes no claim about human use.
