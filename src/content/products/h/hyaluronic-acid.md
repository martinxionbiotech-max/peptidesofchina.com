Hyaluronic acid is not a peptide, and a catalogue that files it under "Skin, Hair & Nails" is already telling you that the marketing taxonomy and the chemistry do not match. It is a glycosaminoglycan: an unbranched polysaccharide of repeating glucuronic acid and N-acetylglucosamine units, with no protein backbone and no fixed molecular weight. That last point is the one buyers miss. "Hyaluronic acid" describes a polymer family, and the specification that matters is the molecular weight distribution, not the name on the label.

For a laboratory, hyaluronic acid is an extracellular matrix reagent. It is used to coat surfaces, to make viscous solutions, to build pericellular matrices, and to study receptor signalling through CD44 and related proteins. The biology is size-dependent, which means a batch with the wrong molecular weight can give the opposite result to the one the paper describes. This page covers that problem, the records that define a batch, and the handling of a hygroscopic polymer that dissolves slowly and degrades under shear.

## Why This Compound

Hyaluronic acid is a major component of the extracellular matrix and of synovial fluid, vitreous humour and skin. It is synthesised at the plasma membrane by hyaluronan synthases, which extrude the growing chain directly into the extracellular space, and it is turned over by hyaluronidases and by receptor-mediated uptake. Laurent and Fraser's classic review remains the clearest account of its physical chemistry: a highly hydrated random coil that occupies a large volume at low concentration, with viscoelastic properties that depend on chain length and concentration.

The receptor biology is where the size dependence becomes experimentally important. CD44 is the principal cell-surface receptor for hyaluronan, identified by Aruffo and colleagues in 1990. Binding can promote cell migration, adhesion and signalling, and the response depends on how the polymer is presented: a high-molecular-weight chain can act as a space-filling, anti-adhesive molecule, while low-molecular-weight fragments can be pro-inflammatory and pro-angiogenic. Toole's review describes the shift from "extracellular glue" to "pericellular cue", and Tavianatou and colleagues catalogue the size-dependent signalling pathways in inflammation and cancer.

Three practical research uses follow. In 3D culture, high-molecular-weight hyaluronan is used to create a hydrated matrix with defined rheology. In migration and wound assays, coated surfaces or soluble polymer are used to test CD44-dependent adhesion. In inflammation research, low-molecular-weight fragments are used as agonists, and the preparation must be free of endotoxin because endotoxin is itself a potent inflammatory stimulus. The same reagent can be a control in one experiment and a variable in another, depending on its size and purity.

There is also a formulation reason the polymer appears in peptide catalogues. Hyaluronic acid is used as an excipient and viscosity agent in research formulations, and it is often bought alongside peptides for that purpose. The quality requirements for an excipient are different from those for a signalling reagent: for an excipient, consistency and sterility matter most; for a signalling reagent, molecular weight and endotoxin matter most. ## What the Batch Record Should Show

A hyaluronic acid certificate should define the polymer, not just the substance. The fields below are the ones that predict whether a batch will behave like the reference material in an assay.

**Molecular weight and distribution.** Weight-average molecular weight (Mw) and polydispersity (Mw/Mn) by size-exclusion chromatography with multi-angle light scattering, or by viscometry calibrated against a standard. A single "high molecular weight" claim is not a specification. The distribution matters because a blend of very high and very low chains can produce a biological response dominated by the low end.

**Concentration and purity.** The content of hyaluronan in the vial, usually by carbazole assay or HPLC of the disaccharide digest, and the protein content by a colorimetric assay. A "≥98%" purity figure for a polymer usually means the polysaccharide fraction, not a single molecular species.

**Endotoxin.** LAL testing is essential for any preparation used in cell culture or in inflammation research. Hyaluronic acid from fermentation can carry endotoxin if the downstream purification is incomplete, and the polymer's viscosity can interfere with some assay formats.

**Residual contaminants.** Protein, nucleic acid, heavy metals and residual solvent, depending on the source and the purification route. For animal-derived material, the tissue source and viral-inactivation steps should be stated; for fermentation-derived material, the host organism and the removal of fermentation residues.

**Physical form.** Appearance, colour, odour, moisture content and pH of a reconstituted solution. Hyaluronic acid is hygroscopic, and a powder that has absorbed water will weigh heavy and dissolve differently.

**Sterility.** A sterile solution requires a sterility test; a powder is not sterile by default. If the product is supplied as a sterile liquid, the fill volume and container should be stated. The [sterility protocol guide](/blog/sterility-testing-for-research-peptides-a-protocol-guide/) explains the test and its limits.

## Sourcing & Verification

The hyaluronic acid market is large, and most of it is cosmetic. Research-grade material is a smaller segment with different documentation. The questions below separate the two.

- What is the weight-average molecular weight, and how was it measured?
- What is the polydispersity, and is a distribution curve available?
- Is the source fermentation or animal tissue, and what is the host organism or tissue?
- Is endotoxin reported by LAL, and at what limit?
- What is the protein content, and how was it measured?
- Is the material sterile, or is sterility a separate test?
- Is the concentration stated in mg/mL or as a percentage, and is the density given for a liquid?
- Does the lot number match the vial and the shipping documents?

A supplier who answers "high molecular weight" without a number is not selling a characterised reagent. A supplier who provides Mw, polydispersity and endotoxin is. Ask which form is in the vial.

## Handling & Storage

The powder is hygroscopic. Store it dry at room temperature, in a sealed container with a desiccant, and let it equilibrate before weighing. Opening a cold container in a humid room causes condensation, and the powder will cake. Weigh quickly, reseal, and avoid repeated opening.

Dissolving is slow. Hyaluronic acid hydrates from the outside in, and a lump of powder dropped into water forms a gel skin that takes hours to disperse. Add the powder slowly to stirred solvent, or wet it first with a small volume of solvent and then dilute. Gentle stirring or rolling is better than high-shear mixing, which degrades the polymer and reduces viscosity. Do not use a blender or a high-pressure homogeniser unless the protocol specifically calls for it.

Solutions are usually stored at 2–8°C for short-term use. They are susceptible to microbial growth, so sterile handling is important, and a preservative-free solution should be used fresh. Freeze-thaw cycles can change the solution structure and are best avoided; if freezing is necessary, aliquot and thaw gently at 4°C.

For cell culture, sterile-filter through a 0.22 µm membrane. High-molecular-weight solutions are viscous and filter slowly; pre-dilute if necessary, and use a filter with a low protein-binding membrane. ## How It Compares

**Sodium hyaluronate.** The salt form, and the form most catalogues actually supply. It is more soluble and more stable in solution than the acid form. The difference is small but real for a formulation that specifies one or the other.

**Crosslinked hyaluronic acid.** Chemically crosslinked gels with much longer residence times, used in 3D culture and in device research. They are not the same as linear hyaluronan and should not be substituted in a CD44-binding assay without revalidation. The crosslinker and its residues are additional variables.

**Low-molecular-weight hyaluronan and oligosaccharides.** Fragments below about 200 kDa behave differently from high-molecular-weight chains, and the low end can be pro-inflammatory. If a study specifies "HA oligosaccharides", a high-molecular-weight batch is not a substitute.

**Other glycosaminoglycans.** Chondroitin sulfate, dermatan sulfate and heparin share the GAG architecture but differ in disaccharide composition, sulfation and receptor interactions. Heparin in particular has anticoagulant activity that hyaluronan does not.

**Collagen and fibronectin.** ECM proteins used in similar 3D-culture contexts. They provide adhesion ligands that hyaluronan does not, and the two are often combined rather than compared. The [GHK-Cu page](/catalog/ghk-cu/) is a useful contrast for the peptide-side of matrix research.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is hyaluronic acid a peptide?**

No. It is a polysaccharide, a glycosaminoglycan made of repeating disaccharide units. It contains no amino acids and is not made by solid-phase peptide synthesis.

**Why does molecular weight matter?**

Because the biological response depends on chain length. High-molecular-weight hyaluronan is space-filling and can be anti-adhesive; low-molecular-weight fragments can be pro-inflammatory and pro-angiogenic. A batch without a stated molecular weight is an undefined reagent.

**How is molecular weight measured?**

Size-exclusion chromatography with multi-angle light scattering is the standard absolute method; viscometry with a calibrated standard is a common alternative. A supplier should state the method, not just the number.

**What purity is supplied?**

The catalogue specification is ≥98 percent hyaluronan content. For research use, endotoxin, protein content and molecular weight are more informative than the headline purity.

**How should the powder be dissolved?**

Slowly. Add the powder to stirred solvent or wet it first, then dilute. High-shear mixing degrades the polymer. Expect several hours for a high-molecular-weight solution to become homogeneous.

**Can it be sterile-filtered?**

Yes, through a 0.22 µm membrane, but high-molecular-weight solutions are viscous and may need dilution first. Check recovery, because some membranes adsorb the polymer.

**Is it stable at room temperature?**

The dry powder is stable when kept dry and protected from moisture. Solutions are less stable and should be refrigerated, used fresh, and protected from microbial contamination.

## The Author's Take

**Position.** I would not buy hyaluronic acid without a molecular weight and an endotoxin result. The name is a family, not a specification, and the family behaves differently at each size.

**Reasoning.** The literature is unusually clear that hyaluronan's effects are size-dependent, and the commercial market is unusually vague about size. A cosmetic-grade "high molecular weight" powder can be a mixture that behaves like a low-molecular-weight preparation in a cell assay, and an endotoxin-contaminated batch can produce an inflammatory signal that has nothing to do with the polymer. The two fields that predict reproducibility are Mw distribution and endotoxin, and they are the two fields most often missing from a one-page certificate. For a 3D culture or a CD44 assay, that is not a detail; it is the experiment.
