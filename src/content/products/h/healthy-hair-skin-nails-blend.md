This is the one product in the catalogue whose name describes a marketing category rather than a molecule. "Healthy Hair Skin Nails" is a retail shelf label, and the formulation behind it is a defined mixture of water-soluble vitamins and related cofactors: thiamine, riboflavin, niacin, pantothenic acid, biotin, folic acid, choline and inositol. There is no peptide in the vial and no single active ingredient to characterise. What a laboratory buys is a specified concentration of eight small molecules in a sterile liquid, and the quality question is whether the measured concentrations match the label.

That framing matters because the category invites claims that the analytical record cannot support. A mixture of B vitamins is not evidence that hair grows faster or that skin repairs itself. The research value is different and more modest: a defined cofactor mixture can be used in depletion-repletion designs, in keratinocyte or fibroblast culture media, and as a controlled source of coenzymes for metabolic assays. This page is about that use, and about the analytical work needed to trust the vial.

## Why This Compound

The eight components are connected by one theme: they are cofactors or precursors for reactions that cells cannot run without. Thiamine pyrophosphate is the cofactor for pyruvate dehydrogenase, alpha-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase, branched-chain ketoacid dehydrogenase and transketolase. Riboflavin is the precursor of FAD and FMN, which carry electrons in oxidoreductases. Niacin is the precursor of NAD and NADP, the redox carriers for glycolysis, the TCA cycle and the pentose phosphate pathway. Pantothenic acid is the backbone of coenzyme A, which activates acyl groups for the TCA cycle and fatty acid metabolism. Biotin is covalently attached to carboxylases involved in gluconeogenesis, fatty acid synthesis and amino acid catabolism. Folic acid carries one-carbon units into nucleotide synthesis and methylation. Choline is a precursor for phosphatidylcholine and acetylcholine; inositol is a precursor for phosphoinositides and inositol phosphates.

In skin biology, several of these have specific experimental hooks. Biotin deficiency in animals produces dermatitis, alopecia and nail changes, which is the historical reason biotin became a "hair and nail" ingredient; the deficiency model is a useful tool for studying keratinocyte differentiation, but it does not follow that supraphysiological biotin improves normal hair growth. Pantothenic acid, in the form of dexpanthenol, has been studied in keratinocyte migration and barrier repair assays. Niacin, through NAD, is required for PARP-dependent DNA repair in UV-irradiated keratinocytes. Choline and inositol feed into phospholipid signalling, which is relevant to any cell-culture system where membrane turnover is part of the readout.

The blend's laboratory value is convenience and definition. Instead of weighing eight compounds and sterilising eight stock solutions, a laboratory can dilute one liquid into medium and know the nominal concentrations. The risk is equally obvious: a single liquid with eight light- and heat-sensitive components is a formulation challenge, and a supplier who does not assay each component is selling a label, not a reagent.

There is also a nomenclature issue. The retail category groups these vitamins with collagen, keratin and other proteins, but the vial contains none of those. A researcher who orders this product expecting a peptide or protein will be disappointed. The catalogue entry is a vitamin mixture, and the related products are other vitamin/lipotropic liquids, not peptides.

## What the Batch Record Should Show

A multi-component vitamin liquid needs a certificate that assays each component, not a certificate that reports the formula. The difference is the whole quality story.

**Concentration per component.** The label states thiamine HCl 50 mg, riboflavin 100 µg, niacin 5 mg, pantothenic acid 25 mg, biotin 100 µg, folic acid 100 µg, choline 10 mg and inositol 10 mg per millilitre. The certificate should report measured values for each, with the assay method. HPLC with UV detection works for thiamine, riboflavin, niacin and folic acid; pantothenic acid is often measured microbiologically or by LC-MS; biotin is usually measured by LC-MS/MS or a binding assay. A certificate that reports only total "B-complex" is not a specification.

**Stability-sensitive components.** Riboflavin is photolabile and degrades under light. Thiamine degrades at neutral or alkaline pH and under heat. Folic acid is sensitive to light and oxidation. The certificate should state the pH, the container and the shelf life, and the laboratory should check for precipitate or colour change before use. A yellow-orange colour is normal for riboflavin; a brown colour is not.

**Sterility and endotoxin.** The product is described as a sterile liquid, so the certificate should include a sterility test by membrane filtration and an endotoxin result by LAL. A liquid preparation that will be added to cell culture must be sterile; a "research-grade" label is not a substitute for the test.

**Preservative and vehicle.** If the formulation contains benzyl alcohol or another preservative, it should be declared, because preservatives interfere with some cell assays. The vehicle matters as much as the actives: pH, buffer, osmolality and any solubiliser should be stated.

**Physical parameters.** pH, osmolality, fill volume and appearance. A vitamin liquid can precipitate if the concentration exceeds solubility at low temperature, and a precipitate that redissolves on warming is a handling note rather than a defect.

**Traceability.** Lot number, fill date, retest date and manufacturing site. ## Sourcing & Verification

This is a product where the gap between a label and an assay is wide enough to drive a truck through. The label is a formulation; the certificate is the evidence. Ask for both.

- Are all eight components assayed, with methods and results, or only the "typical" formula?
- Is the riboflavin content measured after filling, or calculated from the input weight?
- Is the pH stated, and is it compatible with the assay medium?
- Is sterility tested by membrane filtration, and is endotoxin reported by LAL?
- Is any preservative or solubiliser declared?
- Is the fill volume stated, and is there overfill?
- Does the lot number match the vial and the shipping documents?
- Is the product stored and shipped protected from light?

A supplier who sends a formula sheet with no assay values is selling a compounded recipe. A supplier who sends a certificate with per-component results is selling a reagent. The [supplier verification protocol](/blog/china-peptide-manufacturer-verification-a-2026-b2b-sourcing-protocol/) and the [GMP assessment guide](/blog/how-to-assess-peptide-supplier-gmp-compliance-in-2026/) cover the facility questions.

One category-specific caution: biotin interferes with streptavidin-biotin immunoassays and with some thyroid hormone assays. A laboratory using this blend in culture should know that carryover can distort a biotin-based detection step. That is a technical note, not a safety claim, and it is the kind of detail a formulator should be able to discuss.

## Handling & Storage

Store the liquid at 2–8°C, protected from light, and do not freeze. Freezing a multi-component aqueous formulation can cause precipitation and phase separation, and the vitamins may not redissolve evenly. Keep the vial in its carton or wrap it in foil; riboflavin degradation is fast under bench lighting.

Bring the vial to room temperature before opening, and wipe the septum. Swirl gently; do not shake. If a precipitate is visible, warm the vial in a water bath at 37°C and swirl; if it does not redissolve, do not use the vial. A change in colour from yellow to brown is a sign of degradation, not a concentration effect.

Prepare dilutions fresh. The vitamins are more stable in the concentrated formulation than in dilute cell-culture medium, where pH and light exposure accelerate loss. If the experiment requires a defined exposure window, add the blend to medium shortly before use rather than storing supplemented medium for days.

Use aseptic technique. The liquid is sterile as supplied, but repeated puncture of the septum introduces contamination. Aliquot into smaller sterile vials if the product will be opened repeatedly. **What vitamins are in the blend?**

Per millilitre: thiamine HCl 50 mg, riboflavin 100 µg, niacin 5 mg, pantothenic acid 25 mg, biotin 100 µg, folic acid 100 µg, plus choline 10 mg and inositol 10 mg. It does not contain vitamin B12.

**Is this a peptide product?**

No. It is a vitamin and micronutrient liquid. The catalogue category is "Skin, Hair & Nails", which is a retail taxonomy, not a chemical description. There is no peptide or protein in the formulation.

**Can the blend be used in cell culture?**

It can be diluted into medium for research use, provided the preparation is sterile and the preservative, if any, is compatible with the cells. The certificate should state sterility and preservative content. It is not a cell-culture medium on its own.

**Does the blend affect hair or nail biology?**

No claim of that kind is made here. Biotin deficiency produces hair and skin changes in animal models, which is why biotin appears in the category, but the evidence does not show that a vitamin mixture changes hair or nail biology in normal subjects. This is a laboratory reagent for research on vitamin cofactor biology.

**How should it be stored?**

At 2–8°C, protected from light, and never frozen. Riboflavin is photolabile and thiamine is heat-sensitive. Bring the vial to room temperature before opening and use aseptic technique.

**What should the certificate include?**

Assayed values for each component, pH, osmolality, fill volume, sterility, endotoxin and any preservative. A formula sheet without assay values is not a batch record.

**Why does the colour matter?**

Riboflavin gives the solution a yellow-orange colour that is normal. A brown colour or a precipitate indicates degradation or a formulation problem. The certificate should state the expected appearance.

## The Author's Take

**Position.** I would buy this product only with a per-component assay. The name is a category, the formula is a recipe, and the certificate is the only thing that turns the recipe into a reagent.

**Reasoning.** Eight vitamins in one liquid is a stability problem before it is a biology problem.
