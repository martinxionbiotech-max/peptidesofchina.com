## Why This Compound

The design logic of Matrixyl is easy to state and hard to execute. KTTKS is a fragment of the procollagen I N-terminal propeptide, and the palmitoyl chain is there to increase lipophilicity so that the peptide associates with cell membranes and skin lipids. In a plastic dish, that lipophilicity changes everything: the peptide partitions into the membrane, aggregates in aqueous buffer, and adsorbs to surfaces. In an analytical lab, it means the peptide does not behave like a normal water-soluble pentapeptide.

That is why Matrixyl is a useful compound for a research catalogue rather than just a cosmetic ingredient. It forces the experimenter to think about formulation. A study that simply dissolves the powder in water and adds it to cells may be measuring precipitate, not peptide. A study that uses dimethyl sulfoxide or ethanol as a primary solvent and then dilutes into medium may be measuring solvent effects. The published in-vitro work on palmitoyl pentapeptide-4 is more careful than the marketing, but it is not immune to these problems.

The other reason to work with Matrixyl is comparative. It is one of the best-known members of a large family of palmitoylated cosmetic peptides, and having a well-characterised reference makes it easier to interpret the rest. If your assay can distinguish Pal-KTTKS from a scrambled palmitoylated peptide, it can probably distinguish the other members of the family too. That is a more valuable outcome than a single “collagen boost” number.

## What the Batch Record Should Show

Identity for a lipopeptide requires more than a mass. The intact mass should match the palmitoylated sequence, and the fragmentation pattern should cover the palmitoyl attachment and the peptide backbone. A batch that has the right mass but the wrong acylation site is a different molecule. The certificate should also state the counterion, because lipopeptides are often supplied as trifluoroacetate salts and the residual acid can affect solubility and cell tolerance.

Purity should be measured by reversed-phase HPLC with a gradient that resolves the lipopeptide from free palmitic acid and from non-palmitoylated peptide. This is the most common failure mode in the category: a supplier purifies the peptide but does not remove the fatty acid, and the resulting chromatogram shows a large early-eluting peak that is not the product. The certificate should show the palmitic-acid region, not just the main peak. A method that runs at 214 nm will see the peptide bond; a method that runs at 280 nm will see almost nothing unless the sequence contains an aromatic residue, which KTTKS does not.

Water content and residual solvent are next. Lyophilized lipopeptides can retain solvent from the purification step, and residual trifluoroacetic acid or acetonitrile can affect the assay. Karl Fischer water content and a residual-solvent screen are appropriate. If the product is intended for cell culture, endotoxin and bioburden should also be reported.

Finally, the physical form. Palmitoylated peptides often produce a waxy or off-white powder rather than a crisp cake. That is normal, but it makes visual inspection less informative. The useful check is solubility: a clear solution in the specified solvent at the specified concentration, with no undissolved film on the vial wall. ## Sourcing & Verification

Matrixyl is a cosmetic ingredient with a large Chinese supply base, and the quality distribution is wide. The cheap material is often a crude palmitoylated peptide with a single HPLC peak and no statement of free fatty acid, counterion or residual solvent. The better material comes with a full analytical package and a batch-specific certificate. The price difference is usually smaller than the cost of repeating an experiment.

Start by asking for the sequence and the acylation pattern. “Palmitoyl pentapeptide-4” is a trade name, not a structural specification; the supplier should be able to state Pal-KTTKS and show the mass. Then ask for the purity method and a chromatogram that includes the free-fatty-acid region. Then ask for counterion, water content and residual solvent. A supplier who can answer those four questions is selling a characterised material; a supplier who cannot is selling a label.

The second check is the solvent recommendation. A supplier who says “dissolve in water” for a palmitoylated peptide has not worked with the molecule. The practical route is a small volume of dimethyl sulfoxide, ethanol or a compatible organic solvent, followed by dilution into buffer or medium with vigorous but controlled mixing. Ask for the supplier's recommended protocol and check that it matches the literature. The [solubility troubleshooting guide](/blog/peptide-solubility-troubleshooting/) explains why lipopeptides behave differently from hydrophilic peptides.

The third check is the supplier's understanding of the cosmetic regulatory boundary. Matrixyl is sold for research use, not as a finished cosmetic ingredient. A supplier who markets it with efficacy claims is creating regulatory risk for the buyer. We keep the listing in research-use language and expect the same from the manufacturer. The [compliance hub](/peptide-compliance-risk/) discusses the boundary in more detail.

Finally, compare the cost per milligram of peptide, not the cost per vial. A 10 mg vial that contains 60% peptide and 40% counterion and water is not cheaper than a 10 mg vial that contains 90% peptide. The certificate should let you calculate the actual peptide content. If it does not, the price comparison is meaningless.

## Handling & Storage

Store the lyophilized powder at −20 °C, protected from moisture. Let the vial warm to room temperature before opening. Because the material is waxy and static-prone, weigh it carefully and use a solvent that wets the powder rather than a jet of water, which can leave a film on the vial wall.

For a stock solution, dissolve in dimethyl sulfoxide or ethanol at a concentration that keeps the peptide soluble, then dilute into the assay buffer. Add the organic stock slowly, with mixing, and watch for a transient haze. If a precipitate forms, the final organic concentration is probably too high or the buffer ionic strength is too low. Do not filter unless you have checked recovery; lipopeptides adsorb to membranes.

For cell-based work, keep the final organic solvent concentration constant across all conditions, including the vehicle control. The solvent itself can affect fibroblast behaviour at concentrations above about 0.5% (v/v), and the effect is not always linear. Prepare dilutions fresh and avoid repeated freeze-thaw of the aqueous working solution.

Chemical compatibility is straightforward: avoid strong oxidants and extreme pH. The peptide has no cysteine, so disulfide chemistry is not a concern, but the palmitoyl tail can be hydrolysed under strongly basic conditions. Store the powder dry, the stock solution cold, and the working dilution for the day. ## How It Compares

Matrixyl 3000 is a blend of palmitoyl tripeptide-1 and palmitoyl tetrapeptide-7, not a single peptide. It is often compared with Matrixyl because the names are similar, but the analytical and biological questions are different. If you need a single defined sequence, Matrixyl is the simpler reference; if you need the blend, buy the blend and characterise both components.

Against [GHK-Cu](/catalog/ghk-cu/), the comparison is about mechanism and handling. GHK-Cu is a copper-binding tripeptide that is water-soluble and blue-green in solution; Matrixyl is a lipophilic lipopeptide that needs organic solvent. Against [AHK-Cu](/catalog/ahk-cu/), the same contrast applies. Against acetyl hexapeptide-8 (Argireline), the difference is the acylation and the target assay: Argireline is water-soluble and is studied in neurotransmitter-release models, while Matrixyl is studied in fibroblast and matrix models.

Within the catalogue, [KPV](/catalog/kpv/) is a small anti-inflammatory tripeptide with no lipophilic tail, and [BPC-157](/catalog/bpc-157/) is a repair-biology peptide with a very different solubility profile. Neither is a substitute. The useful comparison is methodological: if your lab can handle a lipopeptide cleanly, it can handle the rest of the cosmetic peptide panel with fewer surprises.

I would treat Matrixyl as a formulation problem first and a biology problem second. The molecule is not difficult to synthesise; it is difficult to keep in solution at a defined concentration, and most of the disappointing in-vitro results I have seen trace back to that. ## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is Matrixyl the same as palmitoyl pentapeptide-4?**

Yes. Matrixyl is a trade name for palmitoyl pentapeptide-4, the palmitoylated KTTKS sequence. The INCI name is the more useful identifier when comparing suppliers, because trade names are not standardised.

**Why is Matrixyl supplied as a powder rather than a solution?**

The lipopeptide is more stable as a dry powder. Aqueous solutions are prone to aggregation and adsorption, and the organic co-solvent needed to dissolve the peptide is not compatible with long-term storage for many assays. Reconstitute fresh for each experiment.

**What solvent should I use?**

A small volume of dimethyl sulfoxide or ethanol is the usual starting point, followed by dilution into buffer or medium. Water alone often gives a hazy or incomplete solution. Keep the final organic concentration constant across conditions and vehicle controls.

**How do I check for free palmitic acid?**

Run the HPLC method with a gradient that retains the fatty-acid region and compare the peak area with a palmitic-acid standard. A certificate that shows only the main peptide peak does not rule out free fatty acid.

**Is Matrixyl a cosmetic ingredient or a research chemical?**

This listing is a research chemical. It is not sold as a finished cosmetic ingredient and no cosmetic or therapeutic outcome is claimed. If you are formulating a product, you need a cosmetic-grade supplier and a regulatory review, not a research vial.

**What purity does the catalogue specify?**

≥98% by HPLC for the 10 mg vial. The lot certificate states the measured purity and the method. Ask for the chromatogram if you need to compare batches.

**Can I use Matrixyl in cell culture?**

Only with a lot that has endotoxin and bioburden data and a solvent protocol that your cells tolerate. Run a solvent-only control and a scrambled-peptide control if the experiment is mechanistic.

**Why does the powder look waxy?**

Palmitoylated peptides often do. The appearance is not a purity indicator. Solubility in the specified solvent and the chromatogram are more useful checks than the visual texture of the powder.
