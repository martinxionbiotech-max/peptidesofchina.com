MOTS-c is a 16-amino-acid peptide encoded within the mitochondrial 12S rRNA gene, supplied as a lyophilized powder for metabolic and mitochondrial research. It is one of a small group of peptides translated inside mitochondria rather than in the cytosol, and that origin is the reason it appears in so many research catalogues. The sourcing problem is less exotic: a 16-mer with two methionines oxidises predictably, and many certificates report a purity figure without showing whether the oxidised forms were resolved. This page covers the biology, the batch data that matter, and the handling steps that keep an assay comparable across vials.

## Why This Compound

MOTS-c was described in 2015 by Lee and colleagues at the University of Southern California as a peptide encoded by a short open reading frame in mitochondrial DNA. The name is an acronym for Mitochondrial Open Reading Frame of the 12S rRNA type-c. The sequence, MRWQEMGYIFYPRKLR, is 16 residues long and has no counterpart in the nuclear genome. In the original paper, systemic treatment of diet-induced obese mice activated AMPK, altered folate and methionine cycle metabolites, improved insulin sensitivity, and reduced adiposity. That combination — a mitochondrial gene product acting on whole-body metabolism — made the peptide a useful tool for researchers interested in retrograde mitochondrial signalling.

Later work added an exercise connection. In mouse studies, MOTS-c levels rose in skeletal muscle after exercise, and treatment in older animals was associated with better physical capacity and muscle homeostasis. The paper by Reynolds and colleagues in *Nature Communications* is the usual citation for that line. A separate study found that MOTS-c modulated plasma metabolites and enhanced insulin sensitivity in mice, while human observational work reported associations between circulating MOTS-c and insulin sensitivity in lean individuals but not in obese ones. That last detail is the kind of qualifier that tends to disappear from supplier copy.

The field is still young, and the literature has contradictions worth knowing. A 2026 study in *Inflammation and Regeneration* found that MOTS-c activated metabolic signalling in human mesenchymal stromal cells while blunting their reparative function — a result that does not fit a simple "mitochondrial support" narrative. Reviews from 2022 onward summarise the area without resolving the direction of some effects. For a research buyer, the practical takeaway is that MOTS-c is an active preclinical tool, not a settled mechanism. The [categories and mechanisms hub](/peptide-categories-mechanisms/) places it alongside other mitochondrial and metabolic research peptides.

There is also a naming issue. MOTS-c is sometimes grouped with "mitochondrial-derived peptides" alongside humanin and the small humanin-like peptides. Those are different molecules with different sequences and different assays. A catalogue that lists them as interchangeable variants is not describing chemistry; it is describing a marketing category.

The sequence contains two methionines and one tryptophan. That is the analytical headline. Methionine oxidises to methionine sulfoxide under ordinary handling, adding 16 Da per residue; tryptophan oxidation adds another 16 Da and can open the indole ring under stronger conditions. A batch that was fine at release can acquire a shoulder in front of the main peak after months on a shelf, and an assay that uses UV absorbance alone may not notice.

## What the Batch Record Should Show

**Identity by mass and sequence coverage.** The reported molecular weight should be consistent with the 16-residue sequence, and the mass spectrum should show the multiply charged envelope expected for a peptide of this size. A single "MW 2174" line is a catalogue value, not a measurement. Ask for the deconvoluted mass and, where available, MS/MS coverage of the N-terminal and C-terminal regions. Coverage matters because a truncated sequence can have a mass close to the intended product if the truncation is balanced by a modification.

**Oxidation profile.** The certificate should show whether the main peak was resolved from the +16 and +32 Da species. On a shallow gradient, methionine sulfoxide often appears as a leading shoulder rather than a separate peak. If the chromatogram has no integration events before the main peak, ask whether the method was capable of separating them. This is not a hypothetical impurity: it is the most likely change between the vial you receive and the assay you run.

**Purity by HPLC, with the method stated.** The catalogue floor is ≥98% by peak area at 214 nm. Area normalisation does not see counterions, water, or inorganic salts. For a basic peptide, trifluoroacetate is a common residual from purification, and acetate is a common counterion. The certificate should name which one applies and report residual TFA if the assay is sensitive to it.

**Peptide content and fill.** A 10 mg vial contains peptide plus counterion plus water. Nitrogen analysis or amino acid analysis converts fill mass into peptide mass. Without a peptide-content value, a nominal concentration can be off by a margin that matters in cell-based work. This is the same issue that affects every catalogue peptide, but it is more visible here because the molecule is large enough that a 10 mg fill can look reassuring while containing less peptide than expected.

**Endotoxin and bioburden.** MOTS-c is often used in cell-culture and primary-cell experiments. Endotoxin testing by LAL is relevant whenever the preparation contacts cells, and sterility is not automatic for a lyophilized powder. A certificate that omits both is not necessarily deficient, but the omission should be known before the assay is designed.

**Batch linkage.** The certificate should carry a batch number matching the vial label, a synthesis date, a retest date, and the manufacturing site. A document that could apply to any MOTS-c lot is a template.

## Sourcing & Verification

MOTS-c sits in a part of the catalogue where marketing language runs ahead of the data. The questions below are the ones that produce a useful answer.

- Does the certificate print the full 16-residue sequence, or only "MOTS-c"? A supplier who cannot show the sequence cannot demonstrate that the batch is the peptide and not a fragment.
- Is the reported mass the free base or a salt? The counterion changes the mass and the fill.
- Was the chromatogram run on a gradient capable of resolving methionine sulfoxide? Ask for the method, not just the purity number.
- Is the peptide content reported in mg per vial, and by which method?
- Is residual TFA reported? For cell-based assays, this is a real variable.
- Was the lot screened for endotoxin before cell-based work?
- Does the supplier describe the product as a mitochondrial extract or a synthetic peptide? MOTS-c is produced by synthesis; an "extract" description is a category error.
- Can the supplier provide a batch-specific certificate for a lot that has already shipped?

One useful comparison is between the certificate and the label. If the label says 10 mg and the certificate reports 8.2 mg peptide content, the difference is the salt and water, and the label is describing fill. That is acceptable if it is disclosed. It is a problem if the buyer discovers it only after preparing a stock.

## Handling & Storage

Lyophilized MOTS-c should be stored at −20°C, protected from light and moisture. The powder is not especially fragile, but the sequence is.

Let the vial reach room temperature before opening. Condensation on a cold vial is a direct route to moisture uptake, and moisture accelerates methionine oxidation and aggregation. Open in a low-humidity space and reseal promptly if the vial is not used in one session.

For assay work, dissolve in sterile water or buffer and check the pH. The peptide carries a net positive charge at neutral pH because of its arginine and lysine residues, and it can adsorb to glass and some plastics at low concentration. Use low-binding tubes, avoid unnecessary transfers, and prepare stocks at a concentration that leaves room for dilution without approaching the adsorption-sensitive range. Add solvent gently; vortexing creates an air-liquid interface that promotes aggregation.

Aliquot before freezing. Aqueous stocks kept at 2–8°C are short-term working solutions; repeated freeze-thaw cycles increase oxidation and aggregation. Freeze single-use portions at −80°C and do not refreeze. Protect from light, since tryptophan is photo-oxidisable.

If the assay involves cell culture, filter the stock through a 0.22 µm membrane and run a vehicle control with the same buffer and filter. Small differences in salt content between batches can show up as differences in cell response, and the vehicle control is the only way to separate them.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is MOTS-c?**

MOTS-c is a 16-amino-acid peptide encoded by a short open reading frame in the mitochondrial 12S rRNA gene. It is studied as a mitochondrial-derived peptide involved in metabolic signalling, including AMPK activation and folate-methionine cycle modulation in preclinical models.

**Is MOTS-c a synthetic peptide or a tissue extract?**

Catalogue MOTS-c is produced by solid-phase synthesis. It is not extracted from mitochondria or tissue. A supplier that describes an extract is using the wrong category, and the certificate should make the synthetic origin clear.

**What purity does a MOTS-c batch need?**

≥98% by HPLC at 214 nm. That number is a starting point. A useful release also reports identity by mass, oxidation profile, counterion, water content, and peptide content.

**Why does oxidation matter for this peptide?**

The sequence contains two methionines and one tryptophan. Methionine sulfoxide adds 16 Da and commonly appears as a leading shoulder on HPLC. If the method does not resolve it, a purity figure can look stable while the active peptide content is falling.

**How should MOTS-c be stored?**

Lyophilized powder at −20°C, protected from light and moisture. Reconstituted stocks should be aliquoted and frozen at −80°C for long-term storage, with working solutions kept at 2–8°C for short periods. Avoid repeated freeze-thaw cycles.

**Can MOTS-c be used in cell culture?**

It is supplied for laboratory research, and cell-culture use is a common research application. Endotoxin testing and sterility are separate release tests; confirm whether they were performed before planning a primary-cell experiment.

**How is MOTS-c supplied?**

As a lyophilized powder in sealed vials, typically 10 mg or 40 mg per vial in 10-vial packs. The catalogue lists current formats and availability.

**Is MOTS-c the same as humanin?**

No. Humanin is a different mitochondrial-derived peptide with a different sequence and a different literature. They are sometimes grouped under the same category, but they are not interchangeable in an assay.

## The Author's Take

**Position.** I would treat MOTS-c as an early-stage research reagent, not as a settled metabolic tool. The supplier question that matters is whether the batch record shows the oxidation profile, not whether the page uses the word "mitochondrial" often enough.

**Reasoning.** The biology is interesting and the foundational papers are real, but the literature is small and contains at least one result that cuts against the usual narrative. The chemistry is more concrete: two methionines, one tryptophan, a basic sequence, and a counterion that can be a large fraction of the fill. Those features produce predictable failure modes — oxidised peptide that co-elutes, adsorption to plastic at low concentration, and a "10 mg" label that describes fill rather than peptide. A supplier who reports the sequence, the mass, the oxidation profile, and peptide content is giving you something you can work with. A supplier who reports only a purity percentage is giving you a number.
