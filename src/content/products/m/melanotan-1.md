## Why This Compound

Melanotan-1 is a synthetic version of α-MSH, a tridecapeptide cleaved from pro-opiomelanocortin. Native α-MSH is short-lived in plasma, and the two substitutions in Melanotan-1 were designed to change that: norleucine replaces methionine at position 4, removing the most easily oxidised residue, and D-phenylalanine replaces L-phenylalanine at position 7, which slows proteolysis and increases receptor affinity. The N-terminal acetyl group and C-terminal amide complete the stabilised structure. The result is a linear peptide that is much more resistant to degradation than the parent hormone.

The main research context is melanocortin receptor pharmacology. MC1R is expressed on melanocytes, where activation raises intracellular cAMP, drives MITF transcription, increases tyrosinase activity, and shifts pigment production toward eumelanin. That pathway is studied in cultured melanocytes and reconstructed skin models, usually with concentration-response curves for cAMP, tyrosinase, and pigment content. Melanotan-1 serves as a reference agonist in those experiments because its affinity and stability are well characterised. The readout is a cellular response, not a cosmetic outcome.

A second context is the wider biology of MC1R. The receptor is not confined to pigment cells; it appears on macrophages, endothelial cells, and some neurons, where melanocortin signalling has been studied in inflammation and stress-response models. Herraiz and colleagues reviewed the α-MSH/MC1R interaction as a driver of effects beyond pigmentation, including DNA repair and inflammatory modulation (Herraiz et al., 2021). That broader literature is why Melanotan-1 appears in immunology and photobiology labs, and not exclusively in dermatology research.

A third context is genetic variation. Common MC1R variants change receptor function and the balance of eumelanin and pheomelanin, and those variants are used to study differences in UV response (Scott et al., 2002; Kadekaro et al., 2003). A laboratory may use Melanotan-1 to compare signalling across variant receptors, or to rescue a weak cAMP response in a variant cell line. Those experiments depend on the ligand being the correct stereoisomer, which is why chiral purity belongs in the batch record rather than in a footnote.

The clinical formulation of afamelanotide exists, and the literature around it provides pharmacokinetic and safety data that a research peptide usually lacks. That literature is not a licence for human use, and this page does not cover it. The catalogue material is a laboratory reagent, and the questions that matter are analytical: identity, stereochemistry, counterion, and stability under light.

## What the Batch Record Should Show

Identity begins with mass. The average mass of the free peptide is 1,646.9 Da, and the singly protonated ion appears near m/z 1,647.9. Because the molecule is large enough to form multiple charge states, the certificate should show a deconvoluted spectrum rather than a single peak label. The observed mass should be consistent with the acetylated, amidated sequence, and the isotope envelope should look like a measurement rather than a copied number.

The harder question is chirality. D-Phe at position 7 is central to the molecule's stability and receptor behaviour, and a batch that contains a substantial L-Phe fraction is not the same reagent. Standard amino acid analysis hydrolyses the peptide and reports total phenylalanine without distinguishing D from L. Chiral analysis requires a separate method, such as derivatisation with Marfey's reagent followed by reversed-phase separation, or chiral LC-MS. Most catalogue certificates do not include it. Ask whether the batch was tested, and if not, ask how the manufacturer controls the stereochemistry of the coupling step.

Norleucine is the second non-natural feature. It is isobaric with leucine and isoleucine under low-resolution mass spectrometry, so a deconvoluted mass alone does not prove its position. A peptide map or MS/MS fragmentation that covers residues 3-5 is stronger evidence. For a 13-mer, this is practical and should not add much cost.

The terminal modifications should be stated explicitly. N-terminal acetylation adds about 42 Da, and C-terminal amidation removes about 1 Da relative to the free acid. A certificate that reports the mass but not the modifications leaves the buyer to infer them. The sequence printed on the certificate should include Ac- at the N-terminus and -NH₂ at the C-terminus, not just the one-letter code.

Purity by reversed-phase HPLC is usually measured at 214 nm, with a second channel at 280 nm to highlight the tyrosine and tryptophan chromophores. The certificate should state column, mobile phase, gradient, flow, and integration threshold. A 280 nm chromatogram is useful for this peptide because oxidation products of tryptophan and tyrosine absorb differently from the parent, and a small satellite peak may be visible at one wavelength but not the other.

Oxidation is a real degradation route even with norleucine in place. Tryptophan at position 9 and tyrosine at position 2 can oxidise, adding 16 or 32 Da, and light accelerates the process. A certificate should report the main peak and any characterised oxidation products. If the chromatogram shows a shoulder that is not identified, ask what it is; an unidentified impurity in a light-sensitive peptide is worth resolving before an assay.

Counterion, water, and peptide content follow the same logic as for any lyophilised peptide. Acetate is common; TFA can remain from purification. Karl Fischer water data and a peptide-content assay are what convert fill weight into peptide weight. On a 1,647 Da peptide, a few percent water and a counterion can shift the amount of peptide in a 10 mg vial enough to matter for a concentration-response curve. Endotoxin testing is relevant for macrophage and primary cell work.

Traceability closes the record. The lot number on the certificate should match the vial label, and the document should show synthesis and retest dates and the manufacturing site. A certificate that could apply to any Melanotan-1 lot is a template. The questions below are specific to Melanotan-1. The first two are the ones most suppliers cannot answer.

## Handling & Storage

Melanotan-1 is light-sensitive. Store the powder at −20°C in the dark, preferably in the amber vial supplied, with a desiccant. Bring the vial to ambient temperature before opening; condensation on a cold cake invites oxidation. A cold vial exposed to humid air will collect water on the cake, and moisture accelerates oxidation and aggregation.

Reconstitution should be gentle. Add water or assay buffer slowly down the vial wall and swirl; vortexing is counterproductive. Foaming increases the air-liquid interface, and both tryptophan and tyrosine are vulnerable to oxidation at that interface. Cloudiness can indicate incomplete dissolution or a buffer mismatch; let it settle before topping up. The [solubility troubleshooting note](/blog/peptide-solubility-troubleshooting/) covers the common causes. For cell work, filter through a 0.22 µm membrane and check recovery by UV absorbance at 280 nm.

Protect the solution from light throughout the experiment. Amber tubes or foil wrapping are adequate for short handling, and a dark cupboard is better than a bench top for anything longer than a few minutes. The catalogue's two-week reconstituted figure assumes refrigerated, dark storage; it is a planning figure, not a stability study. A laboratory should define its own working window with a fresh preparation and an appropriate analytical check.

Use polypropylene tubes for dilute stocks. The peptide is moderately hydrophobic and can adsorb to polystyrene and to some filter materials. Keep stocks concentrated and dilute immediately before use. If the assay tolerates a carrier protein, 0.1% BSA can reduce losses, but it adds a background that may interfere with fluorescence or mass spectrometry; test it before adopting it.

Aliquot before freezing. Repeated freeze-thaw cycles promote aggregation and oxidation, and a 13-mer with a hydrophobic C-terminus is more prone to self-association than a short cationic peptide. For long-term storage, use a freezer without a frost-free defrost cycle and keep the vial desiccated. The [handling protocols](/peptide-handling-protocols/) cover the general laboratory routine.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How does Melanotan-1 differ from Melanotan-2?**

Melanotan-1 is linear and more MC1R-selective. Melanotan-2 is cyclic, more potent at MC3R and MC4R, and has a different research profile. The sequences, receptor selectivity, and typical assay contexts differ. A laboratory working on melanocyte biology usually chooses Melanotan-1; a laboratory working on central melanocortin signalling may choose Melanotan-2.

**Why does the D-Phe residue matter?**

D-phenylalanine at position 7 slows proteolysis and contributes to receptor affinity. A batch containing the L-form is a different molecule with different stability. Standard amino acid analysis cannot distinguish the two, so chiral analysis is needed. Ask whether the batch was tested and by which method.

**Is Melanotan-1 acetylated and amidated?**

Yes. The catalogue sequence is Ac-Ser at the N-terminus and Val-NH₂ at the C-terminus. Acetylation adds about 42 Da, and amidation removes about 1 Da relative to the free acid. The certificate should state both modifications rather than leaving them to inference.

**What mass should the certificate show?**

The average mass of the free peptide is 1,646.9 Da; the singly protonated ion appears near m/z 1,647.9. Because the molecule forms multiple charge states, a deconvoluted spectrum is more useful than a single m/z label. A certificate that shows only "MW 1646.9" has quoted the catalogue.

**What is the correct storage routine?**

At −20°C, desiccated, protected from light. Bring it to room temperature before opening. The amber vial supplied by the manufacturer is adequate for short-term handling; for long-term storage, a light-tight container with desiccant is better.

**How stable is the reconstituted solution?**

The catalogue suggests 2-8°C and use within two weeks, protected from light. That is a planning figure. Oxidation of tryptophan and tyrosine, adsorption to plastic, and freeze-thaw cycling all reduce the active concentration over time. A laboratory should establish its own working window.

**Can Melanotan-1 be used in cell culture?**

Yes, with a sterile, endotoxin-tested preparation. Pass the preparation through a 0.22 µm membrane and check recovery by UV absorbance. Keep exposure times consistent across wells, because the peptide adsorbs to plastic and the effective concentration can fall during a long incubation.

**Why is the solution yellow after reconstitution?**

A pale yellow tint can be normal for a peptide containing tryptophan, but a strong yellow or brown colour suggests oxidation or contamination. Check the UV spectrum and, if possible, run a fresh vial alongside the older one before using the material in a quantitative assay.
