## Why This Compound

The six components sit in three research neighbourhoods. L-carnitine is the carrier that moves long-chain fatty acids into the mitochondrial matrix as acylcarnitines; the carnitine pool also buffers excess acyl-CoA, which is why it appears in studies of mitochondrial fuel handling rather than in studies of a single enzyme. Methionine is the substrate for S-adenosylmethionine, the methyl donor used in dozens of transmethylation reactions, and its metabolism intersects with folate and B12 through the methionine cycle. Choline is a precursor to acetylcholine and to phosphatidylcholine, and its oxidation product betaine is another methyl donor. Inositol is a sugar alcohol that feeds the phosphatidylinositol cycle, producing the second messengers IP3 and DAG. Vitamin B6 is the cofactor for transamination and decarboxylation reactions, and vitamin B12 is required for methionine synthase and methylmalonyl-CoA mutase.

Putting them in one ampoule is a formulation decision, not a pharmacological one. The research rationale is that the components are metabolically adjacent: methyl transfer, one-carbon metabolism, phospholipid synthesis, and mitochondrial acyl handling all draw on the same small set of substrates and cofactors. A study that varies choline availability, for example, may want to hold methionine and B12 constant because they share the methylation cycle. A study on carnitine-dependent oxidation may want inositol and choline present because membrane phospholipid composition affects mitochondrial membrane behaviour.

That adjacency is also the analytical problem. Six water-soluble compounds with different UV absorbance maxima, different ionization behaviour, and different stability profiles do not fit one generic HPLC method. A supplier that reports a single "purity" figure for the blend is either measuring one marker and extrapolating or reporting a number that has no defined meaning. The useful release package reports each component separately, with a method and a specification for each.

The catalogue concentration of 350.25 mg/mL is a total dissolved solids figure, not a sum of active ingredients with independent release limits. Buyers should ask for the component table and the mass balance. If the sum of declared components accounts for only part of the 350.25 mg/mL, the difference is excipient, buffer, or water — and that difference should be identified.

## What the Batch Record Should Show

The first page of the batch record should identify the lot, the fill date, the ampoule type, and the fill line. For a liquid product, the container is part of the specification: amber glass versus clear glass, Type I versus Type II borosilicate, and the closure system all affect photostability and extractables. A certificate that lists only the solution composition is incomplete.

The analytical package should start with a component assay for each declared analyte. L-carnitine is typically measured by HPLC with UV detection after derivatization, or by LC-MS/MS without it. Methionine, choline, and inositol require separate chromatographic conditions; choline and inositol are often measured by LC-MS/MS or by enzymatic assays. B6 and B12 are usually measured by HPLC or microbiological assay, and B12 at low concentration may need immunoaffinity cleanup. The certificate should name the method for each analyte and give a result as a percentage of label claim, not just "present."

Uniformity is the next issue. An ampoule is a single unit, so content uniformity is about fill-to-fill variation across the batch, not tablet-to-tablet variation within a bottle. Ask for fill volume by weight, density, and the acceptance range. A 10 mL ampoule that contains 9.6 mL is a five percent shortfall before any analytical variation is considered.

The physical and microbiological tests matter as much as the assays. pH should be recorded with the buffer system identified; a liquid blend at the wrong pH can precipitate or hydrolyse. Osmolality should be stated if the material is intended for cell-culture dilution, because a hypertonic stock can distort a medium. Sterility by membrane filtration and endotoxin by LAL are minimum release tests for a sterile liquid. Particulate matter should be checked by the light-obscuration method, and visible particles by inspection against a black-and-white background.

Preservative status should be explicit. A multi-dose ampoule that contains no preservative has a different handling window from one that does. If a preservative is present, the certificate should identify it and give its concentration. If the label says "sterile" but not "preservative-free," ask which is true.

Finally, a stability-indicating assay is worth requesting. The blend contains vitamins that are light-sensitive and a choline salt that is hygroscopic. A short accelerated study — four weeks at 25°C/60% RH and at 40°C/75% RH — will show whether the label claim survives the shipping lane. A supplier that cannot provide any stability data is selling a batch, not a product.

## Sourcing & Verification

Liquid blends are the easiest category to fake and the hardest to verify. A trader can buy six food-grade ingredients, dissolve them in water, filter the solution, and fill ampoules under a laminar hood. The result may look identical to a properly manufactured batch, and a certificate of analysis generated in-house will not reveal the difference. The buyer's defence is documentation depth and independent testing.

Start with the manufacturer's scope. Does the site hold a production licence that covers liquid fill and sterilization? Does it run its own HPLC, or does it outsource every assay? A factory that sends all testing to a contract lab is not automatically bad, but the chain of custody needs to be documented. Ask for the name and address of the testing laboratory, not just a report with a logo.

Ask for the component specification sheets. Each ingredient should have an identity test, an assay, and an impurity profile. L-carnitine, for example, can be supplied as the tartrate or the free base; the two have different mass fractions and different counterions. Choline can be choline chloride, choline bitartrate, or choline dihydrogen citrate. Inositol can be myo-inositol or D-chiro-inositol. These are not interchangeable in a formulation, and a label that says "inositol" without the isomer is underspecified.

The next question is the water. Liquid formulations are mostly water, and the water quality determines the bioburden and the endotoxin load. Ask for the water system's conductivity, TOC, and microbial monitoring data. A batch made with purified water that is out of specification can fail sterility even after filtration, because filtration removes organisms but not endotoxin.

Independent verification should focus on the analytes most likely to be wrong. L-carnitine is the largest component by mass and the easiest to overstate. B12 is the smallest and the easiest to understate because of adsorption losses during filtration. Choline and inositol are the most likely to be omitted or substituted. A third-party assay panel that covers those four, plus pH and sterility, gives a reasonable picture of whether the batch matches the label.

## Handling & Storage

Store the ampoules at 2–8°C, protected from light. Do not freeze. A liquid formulation that freezes can crack the ampoule or phase-separate; the vitamins and choline salts may precipitate on thawing and not fully redissolve. If an ampoule has been accidentally frozen, inspect it for cracks and for precipitate before use, and discard it if either is present.

Bring the ampoule to room temperature before opening, but do not warm it above 30°C. Warm the solution by rolling the ampoule between gloved palms rather than by immersion in a water bath, which can introduce contamination at the neck. Wipe the neck with 70% isopropanol and use a fresh needle or a filter needle if the solution is being transferred. For cell-culture work, filter the solution through a 0.22 µm PVDF or PES filter into a sterile container; do not assume the ampoule is particle-free just because it is sterile.

Dilute into the assay buffer at the final ratio, and record the dilution. The stock is concentrated enough that a small pipetting error becomes a large concentration error. Use positive-displacement pipettes for viscous solutions, and check the density if the protocol calls for a gravimetric dilution. The [handling guide](/peptide-handling-protocols/) covers the general liquid-handling workflow.

Light protection is not optional. B6 is photolabile, and B12 is sensitive to light and to ascorbate. Amber ampoules help, but they do not eliminate the need to keep the solution out of direct light during preparation. Wrap the tube in foil if the assay requires a long equilibration.

Do not store a diluted working solution for more than the same day unless a stability study supports it. The blend has no preservative in most formats, and a dilute solution in a bench container is an excellent growth medium for environmental organisms. If a working solution must be held, keep it at 2–8°C, protected from light, and record the hold time.

## The Author's Take

**Position.** Multi-component liquid blends are the category I trust least, and SHRED is a good example of why. The product can be analytically correct and still be a poor research material if the component table is vague.

**Reasoning.** A single peptide has one identity, one purity, and one impurity profile. A six-component blend has six of each, plus a solvent, plus a container, plus a fill volume. Every one of those is a place for a supplier to substitute, omit, or overstate. The certificate that matters is not the one with the highest number; it is the one that lists each analyte, its method, its specification, and its result. I would rather buy a batch with a 96% carnitine assay that is fully documented than a batch with a 102% label claim and no component table. The second number is often a calculation, not a measurement.

**Why is there no CAS number or molecular formula?**

CAS numbers and molecular formulas identify single chemical entities. SHRED is a mixture of six compounds in aqueous solution, so no single CAS number applies. The certificate should identify each component and its salt or isomer instead.

**How is a multi-component blend verified?**

Each declared component needs its own assay method, specification, and result. The batch record should also include pH, osmolality, fill volume, sterility, endotoxin, and particulate matter. A single "purity" figure for the blend is not a meaningful release test.

**What is the difference between SHRED and Super Shred?**

No. The label specifies 2–8°C and says do not freeze. Freezing can crack the glass or cause the dissolved components to precipitate on thawing. Keep the ampoules upright, protected from light, and within the labelled temperature range.

**Can SHRED be used in cell culture?**
