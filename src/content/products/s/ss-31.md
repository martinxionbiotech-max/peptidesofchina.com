## Why This Compound

Cardiolipin is a dimeric phospholipid found almost exclusively in the inner mitochondrial membrane. It is not a passive structural lipid. It stabilises the cristae curvature, anchors respiratory supercomplexes, and participates in the conformational changes that couple electron transfer to proton pumping. When cardiolipin is oxidised or redistributed, cytochrome c can dissociate from the membrane, the permeability transition pore becomes easier to open, and electron leak rises. That sequence is central to mitochondrial injury models in cardiac, renal, and neuronal tissue.

SS-31 was designed to bind cardiolipin electrostatically. The arginine and lysine side chains carry positive charge, and the dimethyltyrosine ring contributes an aromatic surface that inserts into the membrane interface. The result is a peptide that partitions into the inner membrane at concentrations far below its bulk concentration in the cytoplasm. Published subcellular fractionation work with tritiated peptide reported roughly a thousand-fold accumulation relative to the cytoplasm, which is why the compound can be active at low nanomolar concentrations in isolated mitochondrial assays.

The research value is mechanistic. Most mitochondrial antioxidants scavenge radicals after they are formed. SS-31 acts upstream of that step by preserving the cardiolipin–supercomplex organisation that keeps electron transfer efficient. In an isolated mitochondrion, that difference is measurable: oxygen consumption rate, membrane potential, calcium retention capacity, and cytochrome c release all respond to cardiolipin stabilisation. In a cell model, the readouts are mitochondrial membrane potential, ROS-sensitive dyes, and Seahorse respiration. In a tissue model, the readouts are infarct size, tubular injury score, or cristae morphology by electron microscopy.

Those are preclinical readouts. This page uses only preclinical data, and nothing here should be read as evidence of benefit in any human condition.

## What the Batch Record Should Show

Start with the sequence and the stereochemistry. The certificate should state the intended sequence, the coupling strategy, and the scale. For a tetrapeptide, the synthesis is short enough that every coupling can be monitored, and a competent manufacturer will have in-process checks for each step. Ask for the chiral HPLC result for D-arginine incorporation, or an NMR method that distinguishes the diastereomer. A standard RP-HPLC method cannot separate D-Arg-SS-31 from the intended peptide in most cases; the two co-elute.

The Dmt residue is the second analytical problem. Dimethyltyrosine is sensitive to oxidation, and an oxidised species adds 16 Da. The certificate should report the Dmt-related impurity by LC-MS, with a limit that is appropriate for a research reagent. If the supplier reports only "purity 98%" by UV, the oxidised species may be hidden in the peak.

Counterion content is the third control. SS-31 is typically supplied as the acetate salt. Acetate content by ion chromatography should be stated, along with residual trifluoroacetic acid if the purification used TFA. The peptide is water-soluble, so reconstitution is not usually limited by solubility, but the counterion affects pH and ionic strength in mitochondrial assays. A batch with high residual TFA can acidify a small-volume assay.

Residual solvents should be reported for the synthesis and purification steps. Class 2 solvents such as dichloromethane, dimethylformamide, and acetonitrile are common in solid-phase synthesis and HPLC, and each has a limit. Water content by Karl Fischer should be below the lyophilization specification. For cell work, endotoxin and bioburden results are required; a tetrapeptide can be pure and still carry enough endotoxin to activate macrophages in a co-culture.

A mass balance calculation is useful here too. The peptide content by amino acid analysis, plus acetate, water, and residual solvents, should account for the vial mass. For a 10 mg fill, a 10% discrepancy is a meaningful difference in the amount of active peptide.

## Sourcing & Verification

SS-31 is made by solid-phase synthesis, purified by preparative HPLC, and lyophilized. The sequence is short, but the non-natural residues make it more expensive than a typical tetrapeptide, and that cost creates an incentive to substitute. D-arginine and dimethyltyrosine are not commodity building blocks; a supplier that quotes a price far below the market is either buying poorly characterised raw materials or using the wrong residues.

The first sourcing question is whether the manufacturer synthesises the peptide or buys it. A trading company can sell a vial with a certificate from an anonymous factory. Ask for the production site, the synthesis batch number, and the name of the QC laboratory. If the answer is a P.O. box and a template certificate, the documentation is not traceable.

The second question is how the manufacturer controls stereochemistry. A supplier that cannot explain the chiral HPLC method, or that reports only "HPLC purity," has not demonstrated control of the one impurity that matters most for this compound. The [molecular weight verification guide](/blog/peptide-molecular-weight-verification-steps/) explains why mass spectrometry is necessary but not sufficient for identity. The [COA reading guide](/blog/how-to-read-a-peptide-coa-a-researchers-guide-to-hplc-and-ms-reports/) shows where a certificate can hide a stereochemical problem.

The third question is the counterion and the residual solvent package. SS-31 is often used in isolated mitochondria and in cell culture, where TFA and residual solvents can confound the assay. Ask for the ion chromatography result and the residual solvent panel. If the supplier cannot provide them, budget for independent testing. A small aliquot sent to a contract lab for chiral HPLC, LC-MS, and ion chromatography costs less than a repeat of a mitochondrial assay.

A fourth question is fill accuracy. The catalogue lists 10 mg and 50 mg vials. A 10 mg fill of a 663.8 Da peptide is a small amount of powder, and static and lyophilization losses can shift the fill. Ask whether the fill is expressed as peptide content or as total mass. The difference is not trivial when the assay is calibrated against a stock prepared from the vial.

## Handling & Storage

Store lyophilized SS-31 at −20°C, dry, and protected from light. Equilibrate the vial to room temperature before opening. The peptide is hygroscopic enough that repeated opening of a cold vial can pull moisture into the cake, and moisture accelerates oxidation of the Dmt residue.

SS-31 is water-soluble. Reconstitute in sterile water or phosphate-buffered saline at pH 7.2–7.4. A stock of 1–10 mM is convenient for cell work; the exact concentration depends on the assay volume and the dilution scheme. DMSO is not required and is best avoided for mitochondrial assays, where solvent effects on membrane potential are a real confounder. If a stock must be prepared in DMSO for a specific protocol, keep the final DMSO concentration at or below 0.1% and run a solvent control.

Add the buffer gently down the vial wall and swirl; do not vortex. The peptide is small and does not aggregate like a long-chain peptide, but foaming at the air–water interface can still cause loss. Filter the buffer before adding it to the vial rather than filtering the peptide solution, which can adsorb to the membrane. If a sterile solution is required, filter through a low-protein-binding PVDF membrane and record the loss.

Aliquot before freezing. The reconstituted stability figure of up to 14 days at 4°C is a planning limit for a sterile, buffered stock. For longer storage, aliquot and freeze at −20°C or −80°C, and avoid repeated freeze-thaw. The Dmt residue is the oxidation-sensitive site; a solution left on the bench in light will oxidise faster than a frozen aliquot.

For cell assays, prepare the working dilution in medium and equilibrate it to assay temperature before adding it to the cells. A cold bolus can transiently depolarise the mitochondrial membrane and confound the first minutes of a Seahorse or JC-1 readout. ## How It Compares

SS-31 belongs to the Szeto-Schiller peptide family, a series of small cell-permeable peptides designed to target the inner mitochondrial membrane. The distinguishing feature of SS-31 is cardiolipin affinity rather than membrane potential. MitoQ and other triphenylphosphonium compounds accumulate on the matrix side of the inner membrane, driven by the membrane potential; they are positively charged lipophilic cations that partition across the membrane. SS-31's accumulation is driven by binding to cardiolipin, which is why it remains associated with the membrane even when the potential collapses.

The SLU-PP-915 comparison is a useful reminder that "mitochondrial research" covers two different levels. SS-31 acts on the membrane and the electron transport chain; SLU-PP-915 acts on the nuclear transcription programme that builds the mitochondrion. A study that uses one as a substitute for the other is comparing a membrane effect with a gene-expression effect. The [SLU-PP-915 page](/catalog/slu-pp-915/) explains the transcriptional side.

Within the SS family, SS-31 is the most characterised member. Other SS peptides have different aromatic residues and different half-lives, and some have been used in preclinical injury models. They are not interchangeable, and a catalogue that lists "SS peptide" without the sequence is underspecified.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How does SS-31 accumulate in mitochondria?**

SS-31 binds cardiolipin, the anionic phospholipid enriched in the inner mitochondrial membrane. The interaction is electrostatic and structural: the arginine and lysine residues engage the phosphate head groups, while the dimethyltyrosine ring inserts at the membrane interface. This binding concentrates the peptide at the inner membrane without requiring the membrane potential that drives triphenylphosphonium compounds. The mechanism has been studied by subcellular fractionation and by cardiolipin-binding assays.

**What makes elamipretide different from other antioxidant peptides?**

Most antioxidant peptides scavenge radicals after they are produced. SS-31 acts upstream by stabilising the cardiolipin–supercomplex organisation that keeps electron transfer efficient. The Dmt residue also has direct radical-scavenging activity, but the primary mechanism is structural: preserving the inner membrane environment reduces electron leak rather than quenching the radicals that have already formed.

**How should SS-31 be reconstituted for cell-based mitochondrial assays?**

Reconstitute in sterile water or phosphate-buffered saline at pH 7.2–7.4 to a stock of 1–10 mM. DMSO is not required. For cell assays, dilute the stock into medium so that the final vehicle concentration is negligible, and equilibrate the dilution to assay temperature before adding it. Filter the buffer, not the peptide solution, and use low-binding tubes for dilute stocks.

**Why does chiral purity matter for SS-31?**

The sequence contains D-arginine at position 1. If the coupling or deprotection steps are not controlled, the D-arginine can epimerise to L-arginine, producing a diastereomer with the same mass and a different biological profile. Standard RP-HPLC often cannot separate the two. Chiral HPLC or NMR is needed to demonstrate that the correct stereoisomer is present.

**What counterion is SS-31 supplied as?**

The catalogue material is supplied as the acetate salt. Acetate content should be stated on the certificate and controlled by ion chromatography. Residual TFA from HPLC purification should also be reported if the material is intended for cell culture, because TFA can acidify small-volume assays and is cytotoxic at sufficient concentration.

**How stable is reconstituted SS-31?**

The catalogue figure is up to 14 days at 4°C for a sterile, buffered stock. The main degradation route is oxidation of the Dmt residue, which is accelerated by light, oxygen, and trace metals. Aliquot and freeze what will not be used within the working window, and avoid repeated freeze-thaw.

**Can SS-31 be used in isolated mitochondria?**

Yes. It is commonly used in isolated mitochondrial preparations, where the readouts are oxygen consumption, membrane potential, calcium retention capacity, and cytochrome c release. The assay buffer matters: a high-phosphate buffer can compete with cardiolipin binding, and a high-detergent buffer can strip the membrane. Run a vehicle control and a cardiolipin-binding control if the question is mechanistic.
