## Why This Compound

The nine components fall into four connected groups. The first is the arginine–ornithine–citrulline axis. Arginine is the substrate for nitric oxide synthases and for arginase; ornithine is the product of arginase and a precursor for polyamines and proline; citrulline is the product of NOS and a precursor for arginine recycling in the kidney. Providing all three together lets a study examine substrate competition rather than a single isolated reaction. In an endothelial cell model, the relevant readout is nitric oxide production, usually measured by nitrite accumulation or by a fluorescent probe. In a hepatocyte model, the relevant readout is urea production. The same three substrates feed both pathways, and the balance between them is the research question.

The second group is the collagen-related amino acids. Lysine and proline are incorporated into collagen as hydroxylysine and hydroxyproline after post-translational hydroxylation. Glutamine supplies nitrogen for nucleotide synthesis and is a major fuel for rapidly dividing cells, including fibroblasts. A study on extracellular matrix production may use these three as a substrate panel, with hydroxyproline as the readout. The formulation does not include vitamin C, which is a required cofactor for prolyl hydroxylase; researchers who need collagen synthesis to proceed must add it separately.

The third group is the redox pair. N-acetylcysteine is a cysteine donor and the rate-limiting precursor for glutathione synthesis. Taurine is a sulfonic acid with antioxidant and osmolyte functions; it is not a peptide and it is not incorporated into proteins. Together they address two different redox mechanisms: glutathione-dependent thiol reduction and taurine-dependent chloramine detoxification. In a cell-culture model of oxidative stress, the relevant readouts are GSH/GSSG ratio, protein carbonyls, and cell viability.

The fourth group is L-carnitine, which carries long-chain fatty acids into the mitochondrial matrix as acylcarnitines and buffers the acyl-CoA pool. It is the only component in the blend that directly touches mitochondrial fuel handling. A study on fatty acid oxidation may use carnitine as a supplement, but the blend also contains amino acids that can enter the TCA cycle, so the interpretation is not clean unless the design includes single-component controls.

## What the Batch Record Should Show

The first check is the arithmetic. The declared amounts sum to 865 mg per millilitre if the per-mL basis is literal. Several of these components have solubility limits below that combined concentration at neutral pH, and arginine, ornithine, and carnitine are all highly soluble individually but compete for water in a mixture. A formulator can reach a high total strength by using a concentrated stock, a low pH, or a co-solvent, but the certificate should state the basis and the final volume. Ask whether the values are per mL of finished solution or per vial. If the answer is per vial, the label is ambiguous; if the answer is per mL, ask for the density and the dissolution procedure.

The component assay is the core of the record. Amino acids are usually measured by ion-exchange chromatography with ninhydrin detection, by reversed-phase HPLC after derivatization, or by LC-MS/MS. The method should be able to resolve arginine from ornithine and citrulline, which are structurally similar and elute close together on some columns. Taurine is not an amino acid in the standard ninhydrin sense and may need a separate method. N-acetylcysteine is measured by HPLC with UV detection or by LC-MS/MS; it oxidises to cystine and to other disulfides, so the sample preparation needs a reducing agent if the total content is the claim. Carnitine is measured by HPLC after derivatization or by an enzymatic assay.

The certificate should report each component as a percentage of label claim with an acceptance range. A single "amino acid assay" result is not enough. If the supplier reports only total nitrogen, the result cannot distinguish the declared blend from a cheaper mixture with the same nitrogen content.

Physical tests matter for a concentrated solution. pH should be recorded with the buffer system identified; a pH outside the physiological range can precipitate glutamine or cause Maillard reactions between lysine and any residual sugar. Osmolality should be stated, especially if the material is diluted into cell-culture medium. Colour and clarity should be inspected against a reference; a yellow tint can indicate oxidation of NAC or contamination. Particulate matter should be tested by light obscuration, and visible particles by inspection.

Sterility and endotoxin are release tests for a sterile liquid. Sterility by membrane filtration and endotoxin by LAL should be reported for each lot. Water quality is the upstream control: conductivity, TOC, and microbial monitoring of the water system are worth asking about, because filtration removes organisms but not endotoxin.

Finally, the container closure system should be described. A 10 mL Type I glass vial with a bromobutyl stopper and an aluminium crimp is standard. If the vial is not amber, the product should be stored in the dark. Extractables from the stopper are a low-probability but real issue for a long-stored liquid.

## Sourcing & Verification

A nine-component sterile liquid is a formulation project, not a catalogue item. The manufacturer needs a validated dissolution sequence, a filtration step, a fill line, and a sterility assurance programme. Many peptide suppliers do not have that capability; they buy the solution from a fill-finish contractor and label it. That is not automatically a problem, but the chain of custody must be documented.

Ask who compounds the solution. If the answer is a contract manufacturer, ask for the site's licence and the fill record. Ask who tests it. If the answer is the same contract manufacturer, ask whether the laboratory is accredited and whether the methods are validated. A certificate issued by the sales office is not a release document.

Ingredient identity is the next layer. L-arginine can be supplied as the free base or the hydrochloride; the mass fraction differs. L-lysine can be the monohydrochloride. L-carnitine can be the tartrate or the free base. N-acetylcysteine can be the free acid or a salt. The certificate should state the form and the assay for each raw material, not just the name. The [ingredient quality guide](/blog/peptide-ingredient-quality-testing-products-2026-guide/) covers this in more detail for multi-component products.

The water and the filter are the two most common sources of batch failure. A 0.22 µm filter is not an endotoxin barrier unless it is validated for endotoxin retention. A water system that is out of specification can seed a batch with Gram-negative bacteria that release endotoxin after filtration. Ask for the water system's monitoring record and the filter's validation summary.

Independent testing should focus on the components that are easiest to misstate. Arginine is the largest by mass and the easiest to overstate. NAC is the most oxidation-prone and the easiest to understate. Carnitine is the most expensive and the most likely to be substituted with a cheaper grade. A third-party panel covering those three, plus pH, osmolality, and sterility, gives a reasonable picture.

The [quality testing pillar](/peptide-quality-testing/) explains the document hierarchy, and the [sourcing guide](/china-peptide-sourcing/) covers supplier qualification. For a blend like this, the certificate is only as good as the sampling plan; a homogeneous solution is easier to sample than a suspension, but a concentrated solution can stratify if it is not mixed before filling.

## Handling & Storage

Store at 2–8°C, protected from light. Do not freeze. A concentrated amino acid solution can crystallise or phase-separate on freezing, and the crystals may not redissolve completely. If an accidental freeze occurs, inspect for crystals and for a change in colour; discard the vial if either is present.

Bring the vial to room temperature before opening. Warm it by rolling between gloved palms; do not immerse it in a water bath, which can contaminate the neck. Wipe the stopper with 70% isopropanol and use a fresh needle. For cell work, filter the solution through a 0.22 µm PVDF or PES filter into a sterile container. The solution is concentrated, so a small volume goes a long way; use a positive-displacement pipette for accuracy.

Dilute into the assay buffer at the final ratio and record the dilution. The high osmolality of the stock can stress cells if it is added directly to a small volume of medium. Add the stock slowly with mixing, and check the final osmolality if the assay is sensitive to osmotic changes. A vehicle control at the same dilution is essential.

Light protection matters. NAC and several amino acids are photolabile, and the solution should be kept in its original carton or in an amber container. Do not store a diluted working solution overnight unless a stability study supports it. The blend has no preservative in most formats, and a dilute solution is a good growth medium for environmental organisms.

For long-term storage, keep the vials upright and do not stack them on their sides. A liquid vial that has been lying down can wick solution into the stopper and increase the risk of leakage. Record the date of first opening and discard the vial after the labelled in-use period.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What are the key components of Super Human Blend?**

The formulation contains L-arginine, L-ornithine, L-citrulline, L-lysine, L-glutamine, L-proline, L-taurine, L-carnitine, and N-acetylcysteine. The declared amounts are listed per millilitre on the specification. The components span nitric oxide and urea cycle substrates, collagen-related amino acids, redox precursors, and a mitochondrial fatty acid carrier.

**Why combine arginine, ornithine, and citrulline?**

The three are connected through the urea cycle and nitric oxide synthesis. Citrulline can be converted to arginine, arginine is the NOS substrate, and arginase converts arginine to ornithine and urea. Providing all three lets a study examine substrate competition between the NOS and arginase routes rather than a single isolated reaction.

**What are the storage requirements?**

Store at 2–8°C, protected from light. Do not freeze. Bring the vial to room temperature before opening, and keep it upright. A diluted working solution should be used the same day unless a stability study supports a longer hold.

**Is the per-mL concentration verified analytically?**

It should be. Ask for a component assay for each declared analyte, not a single total-nitrogen result. The certificate should state whether the values are measured or calculated and whether they refer to the finished solution or the fill volume.

**Can Super Human Blend be used in cell culture?**

It can be diluted into medium if the formulation and the vehicle are compatible with the assay. The stock is concentrated and has a high osmolality, so add it slowly with mixing and run a vehicle control at the same dilution. Filter the solution before use.

**Does the blend contain vitamin C for collagen synthesis?**

No. Vitamin C is a required cofactor for prolyl and lysyl hydroxylase, and it is not listed as a component. A study that measures collagen synthesis or hydroxyproline production must add ascorbate separately.

**How should I verify the identity of the amino acids?**

Use a method that resolves arginine, ornithine, and citrulline, such as ion-exchange chromatography with ninhydrin detection or LC-MS/MS. Taurine and NAC may need separate methods. A certificate that reports only total amino nitrogen cannot confirm the declared composition.

**What documentation should I request before ordering?**
