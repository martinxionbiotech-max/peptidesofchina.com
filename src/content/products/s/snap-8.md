## Compound Profile

SNAP-8 (Acetyl Octapeptide-3) is a synthetic octapeptide functionally related to the N-terminal peptide region of SNAP-25 (synaptosomal-associated protein 25), a key component of the SNARE complex responsible for neurotransmitter vesicle fusion at synaptic terminals. The SNARE complex also mediates catecholamine release from cutaneous neuronal junctions, which drives contraction of expression muscles. By providing a competitive analogue of the SNAP-25 binding site, SNAP-8 is studied as a potential inhibitor of SNARE complex assembly at the neuro-muscular junction — a mechanism proposed to reduce muscle contraction amplitude at the site of application. Research into SNAP-8 examines its ability to inhibit catecholamine exocytosis from neuronal terminals in skin — specifically, the noradrenaline release that triggers receptor-mediated muscle fiber contraction. In contrast to botulinum toxin, which cleaves SNAP-25 irreversibly, SNAP-8 provides a reversible competitive peptide that can be used to study SNARE-mediated exocytosis in dermal research models without permanent enzymatic destruction of the SNARE machinery. Research applications include studies of neuro-muscular junction pharmacology in skin, SNARE complex assembly kinetics, and catecholamine exocytosis modulation in cutaneous tissues. ## Why This Compound

The case for SNAP-8 rests on a sequence analogy. SNAP-25 is one of three proteins that form the SNARE complex, the machinery that pulls a synaptic vesicle into contact with the plasma membrane and triggers release of its contents. The N-terminal region of SNAP-25 participates in the initial assembly of the complex. SNAP-8 copies eight amino acids from that region, with an acetyl group on the N-terminus and an amide on the C-terminus. The proposed mechanism is competitive: the peptide occupies a binding site that the native protein would use, slowing assembly and reducing exocytosis. Because the interaction is reversible, the effect can be titrated, unlike botulinum toxin, which cleaves SNAP-25 and leaves the machinery permanently damaged.

That is a coherent hypothesis. It is not the same as a demonstrated effect in a human tissue, and the published record for SNAP-8 itself is small. Most of the peer-reviewed work on SNAP-25-derived peptides concerns acetyl hexapeptide-8, a shorter sequence with a similar proposed mechanism, or synthetic fragments used as laboratory probes. The clinical literature on SNAP-8 is sparse; the studies that exist tend to be formulation studies in which it is one of several actives, which makes it impossible to attribute an outcome to this peptide. A 2020 study of a hyaluronic-acid microneedle patch, for example, included acetyl octapeptide-3 alongside arginine/lysine polypeptide, palmitoyl tripeptide-5, and adenosine. The patch was the intervention; the peptide was an ingredient. That is a real distinction, and a supplier who cites the study as proof of SNAP-8 efficacy is overreading it.

The mechanistic literature on SNARE proteins is much stronger than the literature on the cosmetic peptide. SNAP-25's role in vesicle fusion is well established, and the interaction between SNAP-25 and syntaxin is a standard topic in neurobiology. The open question is whether an eight-amino-acid fragment, applied to a cell culture or a skin model, reaches the relevant compartment at a concentration that changes assembly. Peptides are large and polar; crossing the stratum corneum or even a cell membrane is not automatic. Some formulation research uses penetration enhancers or microneedles for exactly that reason. A study that ignores delivery is testing the peptide's binding in a tube, not its behaviour in tissue.

The acetyl group matters for stability. N-terminal acetylation blocks aminopeptidase attack and is common in peptide drugs and cosmetic peptides. The C-terminal amide does something similar at the other end. These modifications are part of the intended molecule, not optional decorations. A batch that lacks the acetyl group is a different peptide with a different mass and, likely, a shorter half-life in culture. The same logic applies to the amide: the free acid is close in mass but different in charge.

The research value of SNAP-8 is therefore best framed as a probe. It can be used to test whether competitive inhibition of SNARE assembly changes a measured endpoint in a defined system. It is not a validated active with a known clinical dose-response. That distinction should shape both the study design and the purchasing decision.

## What the Batch Record Should Show

The first field is the intact mass. The calculated molecular weight of the acetylated, C-amidated octapeptide is about 1075 Da, so the protonated ion should appear near m/z 1076 in positive-ion mode. The certificate should show the spectrum and the observed mass. An observed mass 42 Da lower than expected is consistent with a missing acetyl group; a mass 1 Da higher may indicate the free acid instead of the amide. Those are small differences on a large molecule, but they change the identity of the product.

The second field is purity by reverse-phase HPLC. The certificate should give the column, mobile phase, gradient, wavelength, and integration method. It should also state whether the method resolves the acetylated peptide from the unacetylated precursor and from truncated sequences. SNAP-8 is short enough that synthesis-related impurities can be close in hydrophobicity, and a shallow gradient may not separate them. A peak-area purity of 98% is a useful number only when the method has been shown to detect the relevant impurities.

The third field is counterion. SNAP-8 is commonly supplied as the acetate salt, though trifluoroacetate appears in some research-grade material. The counterion affects solubility, pH, and mass balance. The certificate should state which form is present and, if the product is intended for sensitive assays, give the residual content. A peptide content figure that ignores counterion and water overstates the amount of active material.

The fourth field is water content by Karl Fischer titration. A lyophilised octapeptide can hold several percent water. The certificate should report the result and the fill mass, and it should distinguish peptide mass from total powder mass. A 10 mg vial is a fill weight, not a guarantee of 10 mg of peptide.

The fifth field is appearance and solubility. SNAP-8 is typically a white to off-white powder. It should dissolve in water or aqueous buffer, but the acetate salt may dissolve more readily than the free base. The certificate can record appearance as an observation, but appearance is not an identity test. A white powder can be the wrong peptide.

The sixth field is the batch traceability block: batch number, manufacture date, retest date, and site of manufacture. If the certificate does not link to the vial label, the data is unanchored. For a peptide sold mainly through cosmetic supply channels, that link is the difference between a research material and an unverified sample.

## Sourcing & Verification

The first question is whether the supplier can show a mass spectrum for the finished lot. A calculated mass is not a measurement. Ask for the observed m/z and the ion mode. The observed value should be consistent with the acetylated, amidated sequence, not with the unacetylated precursor.

The second question is how the method handles the acetylated versus unacetylated forms. Ask whether the HPLC gradient was developed to resolve them and whether the certificate reports any unacetylated peptide as a related substance. A supplier who has never considered the acetyl group is unlikely to have a method that detects its loss. A supplier who has considered it will have an answer about resolution and forced-degradation data.

The third question is what "98% purity" describes. Is it peak-area purity by HPLC, peptide content by nitrogen analysis, or a value copied from a synthesis report? Ask for the chromatogram and the integration method. A certificate that reports only a headline number is not a specification.

The fourth question is the counterion and water content. These fields are boring, which is why they are informative. A supplier who includes them is likely to have a real QC release process. A supplier who omits them is likely to be using a template. The same logic applies to the fill mass: ask whether the label weight is peptide mass, salt mass, or total powder mass.

The fifth question is the evidence claim. If a supplier says SNAP-8 is "clinically proven," ask which trial, in which population, with what endpoint, and whether SNAP-8 was the only active. In the published formulation studies, it usually is not. A supplier who cannot answer is repeating marketing copy, and that tells you something about the rest of the certificate.

## Handling & Storage

Store lyophilised SNAP-8 at −20°C long-term and 2–8°C short-term, protected from light and moisture. The powder is hygroscopic; let the vial equilibrate before opening. Keep working stock in a desiccator and avoid repeated warming.

Reconstitution should use a buffer that keeps the peptide soluble and the pH near neutral. The acetylated peptide is stable in mild conditions, but extreme pH can hydrolyse the C-terminal amide or promote aggregation. Prepare the solution gently; avoid vigorous vortexing, which can create foam and cause surface adsorption. If the assay requires a particular buffer, check compatibility before dissolving the whole vial.

Because the molecule is small and polar, adsorption to plastic can be a source of loss at low concentrations. Use low-binding tubes for dilute solutions, and include a carrier protein if the assay permits it. Aliquot into single-use volumes and store at −20°C or below. Avoid repeated freeze-thaw cycles.

Solution stability is assay-dependent. The published storage guidance is a starting point, not a guarantee for every buffer. If an experiment runs for several days, establish a time course and check for hydrolysis or aggregation rather than assuming the peptide is intact. A clear solution can still contain degraded peptide.

This is laboratory material, supplied for in vitro and preclinical research. It is not for human or veterinary use, and nothing here is a dosing instruction. Reconstitution volumes and exposure conditions belong in an approved study protocol.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is SNAP-8?**

SNAP-8 is an acetylated octapeptide modelled on the N-terminal region of SNAP-25. It is supplied as a lyophilised powder for laboratory research on SNARE complex assembly and catecholamine exocytosis. It is not a botulinum toxin and does not cleave SNAP-25.

**Is SNAP-8 the same as botulinum toxin?**

No. Botulinum toxin is an enzyme that cleaves SNAP-25 irreversibly. SNAP-8 is a short peptide proposed to compete with SNAP-25 for binding in the SNARE complex. The mechanism is reversible and titratable, which is why it is studied as a laboratory tool rather than a toxin substitute.

**Does SNAP-8 have published clinical evidence?**

The direct clinical literature is limited. Some formulation studies include acetyl octapeptide-3 as one of several actives, which makes it impossible to attribute an outcome to the peptide alone. The mechanistic literature on SNAP-25 and SNARE assembly is much stronger than the literature on this specific octapeptide.

**What does the acetyl group do?**

N-terminal acetylation blocks aminopeptidase attack and is part of the intended molecule. A batch without the acetyl group has a mass about 42 Da lower and may behave differently in culture. The certificate should confirm the acetylated mass, not just the amino-acid sequence.

**How is purity verified?**

By reverse-phase HPLC with UV detection, supported by mass spectrometry for identity. The certificate should give the method, the chromatogram, and the observed mass. A purity percentage without a method is not a specification.

**How should SNAP-8 be stored?**

Store lyophilised powder at −20°C long-term and 2–8°C short-term, protected from light and moisture. Equilibrate before opening. Reconstitute in a compatible buffer, avoid extreme pH, aliquot into single-use volumes, and avoid repeated freeze-thaw cycles.

**Is SNAP-8 soluble in water?**

The acetate salt is generally water-soluble, but solubility depends on pH, ionic strength, and concentration. If a solution is cloudy, check the buffer and the order of addition before assuming the peptide is degraded. Use low-binding tubes for dilute solutions.
