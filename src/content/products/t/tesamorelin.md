## Why This Compound

GHRH is a 44-residue peptide released from the hypothalamus into the hypophyseal portal circulation. It binds GHRHR on anterior pituitary somatotrophs and raises intracellular cAMP, which drives GH synthesis and release. The full-length sequence is rapidly cleaved by DPP-IV at the Tyr¹-Ala² bond, and that cleavage is the main reason native GHRH has a short plasma half-life.

Tesamorelin's design addresses that problem at the N-terminus. The trans-3-hexenoic acid group is a fatty acid, but it is not the C16 or C18 fatty acid used in albumin-binding analogues. It caps the N-terminus and blocks DPP-IV access without creating a strong albumin-binding moiety. The published pharmacology describes a half-life in the range of roughly 26–30 minutes, which is longer than sermorelin and much shorter than CJC-1295 with DAC. That intermediate position is the research value: it lets a study vary exposure duration without changing the receptor or the full-length sequence.

The 44-residue length is the other variable. Sermorelin and CJC-1295 without DAC are 29-mers; they contain the receptor-binding domain but not the C-terminal region. Tesamorelin retains the full sequence, including the residues that contribute to plasma binding and to the conformational stability of the peptide. A study that compares tesamorelin with a 29-mer is comparing a full-length analogue with a truncated one, not just two half-lives.

Those differences are measurable in pituitary cell models. The readouts are cAMP accumulation, GH release by ELISA or radioimmunoassay, and receptor internalisation. In an animal model, the readouts are GH pulse amplitude and frequency, IGF-1 levels, and pituitary gene expression. Those are preclinical endpoints. Later development of tesamorelin is outside the scope of this page, and nothing here should be read as evidence of benefit in any human condition.

## What the Batch Record Should Show

The first item is the N-terminal modification. The trans-3-hexenoic acid group is not a standard amino acid, and its attachment is the defining feature of the molecule. The certificate should state the expected intact mass, the observed mass, and the method used to confirm the modification. A mass that matches the unmodified 44-mer is a failed batch, even if the HPLC purity is high. A mass that matches the modified peptide but with a mass error close to the method's limit should be re-run with a higher-resolution instrument.

The second item is purity and the impurity profile. A 44-mer synthesized by solid-phase methods will contain deletion sequences, truncated fragments, and oxidised species. RP-HPLC should be run with a disclosed method, and the impurity table should list the main related substances. The N-terminal modification can also produce a mixture of modified and unmodified peptide, and the two may co-elute on a shallow gradient. Ask for the ratio of modified to unmodified species by LC-MS, not just the total purity.

The third item is aggregation. Tesamorelin is a 5.1 kDa peptide with a hydrophobic N-terminus, and it can self-associate in aqueous solution. Size-exclusion chromatography on the reconstituted solution should be part of the release package. A batch with 98% monomer by SEC is a different material from a batch with 90% monomer and 8% aggregate, even if both pass RP-HPLC at 98%.

The fourth item is peptide content and counterion. A 2 mg vial is a small fill, and the peptide content by amino acid analysis or nitrogen determination should be stated separately from the salt weight. The counterion is usually acetate; the certificate should give the acetate content by ion chromatography. Water content by Karl Fischer should be below the lyophilization specification. Residual TFA should be reported if the material is for cell culture.

For cell work, endotoxin and bioburden results are required. A 44-mer can be pure by HPLC and still carry enough endotoxin to activate a pituitary culture. Sterility is a separate test and should not be inferred from filtration during fill.

## Sourcing & Verification

Tesamorelin is a difficult synthesis. The 44-residue chain is long enough that coupling efficiency drops in the middle of the sequence, and the N-terminal fatty acid adds a hydrophobic handle that complicates purification. A manufacturer that can make a 29-mer may not be able to make this molecule reproducibly. That is the first sourcing question: has the supplier made this specific sequence before, and can they show a batch history?

The second question is the modification chemistry. Ask which fatty acid is used, how it is activated, and how the coupling is monitored. A supplier that cannot describe the chemistry is unlikely to control the ratio of modified to unmodified peptide. The third question is the analytical package. Ask for the intact mass, the modification ratio, the SEC aggregate result, the peptide content, and the counterion. A certificate that reports only "purity ≥98%" is not sufficient for a modified peptide. The [COA reading guide](/blog/how-to-read-a-peptide-coa-a-researchers-guide-to-hplc-and-ms-reports/) shows how to spot a report that has been assembled from a template.

The fourth question is the fill size. The catalogue lists 2, 5, 10, 20, and 30 mg vials. A 2 mg fill is convenient for a small study, but the relative fill error is larger and the peptide content may be a smaller fraction of the total mass. Ask whether the fill is expressed as peptide content or as total mass. For a 44-mer, the counterion and water can account for a meaningful fraction of the vial.

Independent verification should focus on the intact mass and the modification ratio. Those are the two properties that a standard purity test can miss. A contract lab with LC-MS and SEC can confirm both from a single vial. The cost is small relative to a repeat of a chronic study.

## Handling & Storage

The powder keeps at −20°C if moisture is excluded; warm the sealed vial to room temperature before the first opening. Opening a cold vial in a humid room deposits water on the cake, and that water drives hydrolysis and aggregation.

Reconstitute in sterile water or phosphate-buffered saline at pH 7.2–7.4. The peptide is soluble at moderate concentrations, but the N-terminal fatty acid can promote aggregation at high concentration. Start at 1–2 mg/mL and dilute from there. If the solution is cloudy, do not filter it and assume the problem is solved; a cloudy solution may indicate aggregation, and the concentration in the filtrate will not match the label. Try a small amount of 10–20 mM acetate buffer at pH 5.5–6.0, then dilute into the assay buffer.

Use low-binding polypropylene tubes. The fatty acid can adsorb to polystyrene and to some filter membranes. Sterilise the buffer first; if the peptide solution itself must be filtered, use a low-binding PVDF disc and note the volume lost. Avoid vigorous vortexing; swirl gently and let the cake dissolve.

Aliquot before freezing. The catalogue says reconstituted solutions are stable for up to 14 days at 4°C, which is a planning limit for a sterile, buffered stock. For longer storage, aliquot and freeze at −20°C or −80°C, and avoid repeated freeze-thaw. A freeze-thaw cycle can change the aggregate profile.

Dilute into medium immediately before the assay and let the dilution reach room temperature. Run a vehicle control at the same buffer and salt concentration. General laboratory technique is covered in the [handling pillar](/peptide-handling-protocols/), and the [stability note](/blog/peptide-stability-why-lyophilized-peptides-are-far-more-stable-than-you-think/) compares dry and solution storage.

A study that uses tesamorelin as a "short-acting version" of CJC-1295 with DAC is comparing a pulsatile signal with a sustained one; the receptor is the same, but the physiology is not.

Sermorelin and CJC-1295 without DAC do not. If the research question is about the receptor-binding domain alone, the 29-mers are the cleaner tools. ## Frequently Asked Questions

**How does tesamorelin differ from CJC-1295 With DAC?**

Both are GHRH receptor agonists with extended half-life relative to sermorelin, but the mechanisms differ. CJC-1295 with DAC binds albumin through a maleimide-linked DAC group, extending the half-life to days and producing a sustained exposure. Tesamorelin uses a trans-3-hexenoic acid N-terminal modification that slows DPP-IV cleavage without albumin binding, producing a moderate half-life and a more pulsatile profile. The two are not interchangeable in a study design.
