TB-500 is the catalogue name for synthetic Thymosin Beta-4 Acetate, a 43-amino-acid polypeptide supplied as a lyophilized powder for laboratory research. The name is the first sourcing problem: some suppliers use "TB-500" for the full protein, others for a short actin-binding fragment, and a certificate that does not print the sequence cannot tell you which one you have. This page covers the distinction, the identity checks that separate a full-length batch from a truncated one, and the handling conditions that keep a 43-mer from oxidising before the assay starts.

## Why This Compound

Thymosin Beta-4 is one of the most abundant actin-sequestering proteins in mammalian cells. It binds G-actin in a 1:1 complex and keeps a pool of monomer available for cytoskeletal remodelling. That housekeeping function is the basis of most research interest: when a cell changes shape, migrates, or divides, it needs actin monomers, and thymosin beta-4 is one of the proteins that supplies them. The peptide also has a short N-terminal tetrapeptide, Ac-SDKP, that is cleaved in vivo and studied separately for its own effects on inflammation and fibrosis.

The synthetic catalogue material is usually the full 43-residue sequence with an acetylated N-terminus, supplied as the acetate salt. The acetylation is not a purification artefact. Native thymosin beta-4 is N-terminally acetylated, and the acetyl group changes the peptide's charge and its interaction with actin. A batch that is missing the acetyl group is a different molecule with a mass 42 Da lower, even though the amino acid sequence is identical.

The naming confusion has a documented origin. Regulatory bodies have used "TB-500" to refer to a heptapeptide fragment corresponding to residues 17-23 of thymosin beta-4, while the research supply market has used the same name for the full 43-mer. Both usages persist. The practical consequence is that a buyer can order "TB-500" and receive either material, and both may be described as research-grade. If your study design depends on the full protein's actin-binding behaviour, the fragment is not a substitute; if it depends on the actin-binding motif alone, the full protein adds mass and complexity you may not need.

Preclinical research on the full sequence spans cardiac ischemia-reperfusion, dermal wound models, corneal repair, and skeletal muscle injury. The cardiac work is among the most cited: transgenic and peptide-administration studies reported improved cardiomyocyte survival and neovascularisation after injury, with integrin-linked kinase and Akt signalling proposed as intermediates. The muscle and skin literature is smaller and more variable. As with most peptides in this catalogue, the evidence is rodent and in vitro, and the step from those models to a general claim about tissue repair is not supported by the data.

What makes TB-500 interesting analytically is its size. A 43-mer is long enough that solid-phase synthesis produces a mixture of deletion sequences, and long enough that purification is harder than for a 15-mer. The impurity profile is therefore more informative than the purity number. A batch that is 97% pure by HPLC but contains 3% of a des-acetyl or oxidised species may behave differently from a 97% batch with a different impurity distribution.

## What the Batch Record Should Show

A full-length TB-500 batch record should answer four questions: is the sequence complete, is the N-terminus acetylated, is the methionine reduced, and how much of the vial is peptide?

**Mass confirmation.** The average molecular weight is about 4963 Da. Because the molecule is large and multiply charged, a simple low-resolution mass spectrum is less useful than a deconvoluted spectrum. Ask for the deconvoluted mass and the raw charge-state series. The observed mass should sit within a few daltons of 4963 for the acetylated, reduced form. A value near 4921 suggests loss of the acetyl group; a value near 4979 suggests oxidation. Both are common enough that the certificate should state which species the main peak represents.

**N-terminal acetylation.** This is the check most often missing. Acetylation adds 42.04 Da and blocks the N-terminus. An enzymatic or chemical acetylation step is part of the synthesis, and incomplete acetylation produces a mixed population. Mass spectrometry can distinguish the two forms if the spectrum is deconvoluted; a peptide map can locate the N-terminal peptide. If the supplier cannot state whether the material is acetylated, the "TB-500" label is ambiguous.

**Purity and impurity profile.** HPLC at 214 or 220 nm with a gradient that resolves the parent from des-acetyl, oxidised, and truncated species. A single "≥98%" figure is not enough. Ask for the chromatogram and, if available, the relative retention times of the main impurities. For a 43-mer, a purity figure obtained on a short column with a steep gradient can hide closely related sequences that a longer method would separate.

**Counterion and peptide content.** The product is supplied as the acetate salt. The certificate should give acetate content, water content by Karl Fischer, and peptide content by a suitable assay. A 10 mg vial may contain less than 10 mg of peptide once counterion and water are accounted for. This matters when preparing a nominal concentration for a concentration-response experiment.

**Endotoxin and sterility.** Endotoxin is relevant for cell-based assays and for any material that contacts primary cells. Sterility is a separate claim; a lyophilized powder is not sterile unless the process and container closure support it. Confirm which tests were run instead of reading a generic statement on the product page.

**Batch linkage.** Batch number on the vial must match the certificate. The record should include synthesis date, purification method, fill date, and retest date. For a peptide this size, lot-to-lot variation in acetylation and oxidation is more likely than in a short peptide, so a current certificate matters more than a historical one.

## Sourcing & Verification

The first question for any TB-500 quote is not price. It is "which sequence?" Ask for the certificate before the quotation. A supplier who sends a certificate with the full 43-residue sequence, an acetylated N-terminus, and a deconvoluted mass is selling a defined product. A supplier who sends a certificate with only "TB-500, 98%" is selling a name.

The second question is whether the fragment is being sold as the full protein. The [TB-500 fragment page](/catalog/tb-500-fragment-17-24/) exists because the two materials are legitimately different products. The fragment corresponds to the actin-binding region and is easier to synthesise and purify. It is not a cheaper version of the full protein; it is a different research tool. The [comparison of TB-500 and Thymosin Beta-4](/blog/tb-500-vs-thymosin-beta-4-what-you-re-actually-getting/) explains the naming history in more detail.

The third question is process control. A 43-mer requires a synthesis route with enough coupling efficiency to avoid a high proportion of deletion sequences. Ask about the synthesis scale, the purification method, and whether the batch was purified by preparative HPLC or only by precipitation. A supplier who cannot describe the purification step is probably a trader.

The fourth question is stability data. Methionine oxidation is the main degradation pathway, and it accelerates in solution at neutral pH. Ask how the batch was stored before shipment, whether it was exposed to light, and whether the certificate reports oxidised species. A batch that sat in a warm warehouse for six months may still be 98% pure by a method that does not resolve the sulphoxide.

## Handling & Storage

Lyophilized TB-500 should be stored at −20°C, protected from light and moisture. The vial should be allowed to reach room temperature before opening; condensation on a cold cake is a real source of moisture uptake. Once opened, keep the vial in a desiccator and avoid repeated warming events.

Reconstitution needs more care than for a short peptide. Add the solvent slowly against the vial wall and swirl rather than vortex. Foaming exposes the peptide to an air-liquid interface, and long chains are more prone to interfacial aggregation. If the solution is hazy, centrifuge at low speed and inspect before filtering; a 0.22 µm filter can remove aggregates but may also adsorb a fraction of the peptide, so check recovery if the assay is quantitative.

Aqueous solutions are short-lived. The catalogue storage guidance is 2-8°C for up to two weeks, but that is an outer limit, not a target. For a study that runs longer, aliquot and freeze at −20°C or below, then thaw once. Avoid repeated freeze-thaw. If the assay is sensitive to oxidation, consider adding a reducing agent or working under inert gas, but validate that the additive does not interfere with the readout.

The methionine also makes light protection worthwhile. Amber vials or foil wrap cost little and remove one variable. For cell culture, prepare the working solution fresh and discard the remainder rather than storing it in the fridge for the length of the study.

All of this is laboratory handling guidance. TB-500 is supplied for research use only and is not for human or veterinary administration.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is TB-500 the same as Thymosin Beta-4?**

The catalogue material is the full 43-amino-acid Thymosin Beta-4 sequence with an acetylated N-terminus, supplied as the acetate salt. Some regulatory and older sources use "TB-500" for a short fragment corresponding to residues 17-23. The name alone does not settle which material you have; the certificate's sequence does.

**What sizes are available?**

The catalogue lists 2 mg, 5 mg, and 10 mg vials in 10-vial packs. Not every size is in stock at all times, and the purity column can differ between sizes. Confirm the format and the batch certificate before ordering.

**How is TB-500 different from the TB-500 fragment?**

The full protein is 43 amino acids and includes the N-terminal Ac-SDKP region and the methionine at position 6. The fragment is a short peptide from the actin-binding region. The fragment is not a substitute for the full sequence in assays that depend on the whole protein.

**What purity does the catalogue material have?**

The standard catalogue specification is ≥98% by HPLC, but the useful question is how that figure was obtained and what impurities it excludes. Ask for the chromatogram and the deconvoluted mass. A purity number without a method is not a specification.

**How should I store the lyophilized powder?**

At −20°C, desiccated, protected from light. Let the vial warm to room temperature before opening. Aqueous solutions should be kept at 2-8°C and used within two weeks, or aliquoted and frozen for single use.

**How can I tell whether the N-terminus is acetylated?**

The deconvoluted mass should be about 4963 Da for the acetylated, reduced form. Loss of the acetyl group gives a mass near 4921 Da. A peptide map can confirm the N-terminal peptide directly. Ask the supplier which form the certificate describes.

**Why does the mass spectrum show a peak at 4979 Da?**

A peak 16 Da above the parent is consistent with methionine oxidation. It may be a storage artefact or a purification impurity. The certificate should state the oxidation level and the HPLC method used to separate the oxidised species.

**Is TB-500 supplied as acetate or as free base?**

The catalogue name is Thymosin Beta-4 Acetate, and the material is normally supplied as the acetate salt. Confirm the counterion on the batch certificate, because the acetate content affects peptide mass per vial.

## The Author's Take

**Position.** I would rather buy a well-documented fragment than a poorly documented full-length TB-500. The name is not the product; the sequence, the acetylation state, and the mass spectrum are the product.

**Reasoning.** The full 43-mer is a harder synthesis, and the market has responded by stretching the name to cover shorter, cheaper material. That is not necessarily fraud — the fragment is a legitimate research tool — but it becomes a problem when the buyer thinks the two are interchangeable. The analytical tells are simple: a deconvoluted mass near 4963, an N-terminal acetylation check, and an oxidation figure. If a supplier cannot provide those three, the price per vial is not comparable to a supplier who can. I have seen certificates that report 98% purity on a peak that was visibly split, and certificates that quote the catalogue molecular weight without a spectrum. Both are reasons to ask for raw data.
