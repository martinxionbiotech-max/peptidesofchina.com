## Why This Compound

KEDG belongs to a family of short peptides studied under the bioregulator heading, most of them three to four residues, most of them acidic, and most of them associated with a tissue of origin in the naming convention. The framework behind the family was developed by Vladimir Khavinson and colleagues in St. Petersburg, and it proposes that very short peptides can enter cells and nuclei and influence gene expression in a sequence-specific way. The claim is unusual, and the primary literature is concentrated in a small number of laboratories.

The testicular label should be read in that context. In the bioregulator tradition, a peptide is named for the tissue whose protein fraction it was derived from, then produced synthetically for research supply. The sequence is the product; the tissue is the provenance story. A synthetic tetrapeptide of this size has no memory of its source. What it has is a molecular mass, a charge profile at a given pH, and a set of possible degradation products.

KEDG's sequence contains an Asp-Gly pair. That detail matters more than the marketing category. Aspartyl residues followed by glycine are prone to succinimide formation, which opens two paths: hydrolysis back to aspartate, or rearrangement to isoaspartate. The isoform has the same mass and a different backbone, so a mass spectrometer will not separate them unless the method is built to do so. Over time, and faster at neutral to slightly alkaline pH, a KEDG preparation can accumulate isoaspartate without any change in the label. This is the quiet failure mode for the compound, and it is the reason the analytical section below spends more space on chromatography than on biology.

What can a researcher actually do with KEDG? The honest answer is narrower than the catalogue category suggests. It is a defined small peptide that can be used as a control in peptide-uptake work, as one member of a panel comparing short bioregulators, or as a tool in cell-culture experiments that measure transcriptional endpoints. Public, peer-reviewed data specific to KEDG is sparse; most of the surrounding evidence comes from related peptides such as AEDG (epitalon) and from review-level summaries of the family. A study designed around KEDG alone will not inherit the evidence base of its better-studied relatives.

That is not a reason to avoid the compound. It is a reason to design around the gap. Pair KEDG with a sequence-scrambled control of the same amino-acid composition, run the same assay with a related tetrapeptide, and treat any difference between them as the interesting result. The [peptide categories overview](/peptide-categories-mechanisms/) places this family alongside the mitochondrial and cosmetic peptide groups, and the [selectivity explainer](/blog/peptide-selectivity-explained-for-researchers/) is useful background for why short sequences are studied at all.

## What the Batch Record Should Show

A batch record for a tetrapeptide should be short enough to read in full and specific enough that another laboratory could repeat the release test. Six items do most of the work.

**Identity by mass and by fragmentation.** Electrospray ionisation of a tetrapeptide gives a clean singly or doubly charged ion, and the observed mass should match the calculated free-base or salt mass within a few ppm. Mass alone is weak evidence for a sequence this small, because isomers and near-isobars exist. Tandem MS or MS/MS coverage of the backbone is stronger. For KEDG, the b- and y-ion series should account for the Lys-Glu-Asp-Gly order rather than merely the composition.

**Purity by a method that resolves the analogues.** Reversed-phase HPLC at 214 nm is standard. The method details matter: column dimensions, particle size, mobile-phase modifiers, gradient slope, flow rate, injection volume, and integration threshold. A generic 10-minute gradient will resolve KEDG from a deletion peptide missing glycine, but it may not resolve KEDG from KEDG with an isoaspartate at the second position. Ask whether the method has been challenged with a stressed sample — heat, pH, or peroxide — and whether the resulting degradants were shown to elute separately. The [COA reading guide](/blog/how-to-read-a-peptide-coa-a-researchers-guide-to-hplc-and-ms-reports/) walks through the fields that should be present.

**Counterion and residual acid.** KEDG is typically supplied as an acetate salt, and trifluoroacetate can carry over from purification. Both change the mass of the fill. A certificate that reports "purity 98.5%" but not the counterion leaves the buyer unable to convert vial content into moles. The [counterion note](/blog/role-of-counterions-in-peptide-stability/) explains the arithmetic and why residual TFA matters in cell culture.

**Peptide content versus fill weight.** The label says 20 mg. That number may refer to the peptide, the peptide salt, or the total powder including water and buffer salts. Nitrogen analysis, amino acid analysis, or a validated UV method at 214 nm with a standard curve can establish peptide content. Karl Fischer titration establishes water. Without both, a 20 mg vial can contain 14 mg of peptide and still be within a loose specification.

**Endotoxin and bioburden.** For anything that will touch cultured cells, endotoxin should be reported in EU/mg or EU/vial, with the assay method named. Bioburden or sterility applies only if the product is sold as sterile; a lyophilized non-sterile powder should not carry a sterility claim it cannot support. The [endotoxin primer](/blog/what-are-endotoxins-why-they-matter/) covers why a low HPLC impurity profile says nothing about pyrogen content.

**Lot traceability.** The lot number on the certificate should match the lot on the vial, and the certificate should name the manufacturing site rather than the trading company. A document that lists only a brand is a brochure with a table attached.

## Sourcing & Verification

Testagen is a niche item, and niche items are usually made to order rather than stocked in depth. That changes the commercial conversation. A supplier quoting from a price list may be quoting a batch that does not exist yet, and the lead time may depend on a synthesis queue rather than a warehouse. Ask when the lot was manufactured, how many vials remain, and whether the certificate predates the order or will be issued with it.

There are three supplier shapes in this market. A synthesis facility makes the peptide and can show the batch record. A trader buys from a facility and resells, sometimes with the original certificate and sometimes with a reissued one. A repackager buys bulk powder, divides it into vials, and prints a label. The third shape is the most common for small peptides and the hardest to audit, because the repackaging step introduces moisture, static, and fill-weight error without changing the chemical certificate.

Three checks separate the shapes. First, ask for a lot-specific chromatogram, not a representative one. The file name, injection date, and detector settings should be visible. Second, ask what the peptide content is, not just the purity. A supplier who understands the difference will have the number. Third, order a small quantity and send an aliquot to an independent laboratory for identity and purity before committing to a larger lot. For a tetrapeptide, the retest cost is modest relative to the order, and it converts a claim into a measurement.

Price is a coarse filter. Four residues of solid-phase synthesis are inexpensive, so a very high price is usually markup rather than quality. A very low price can mean a smaller fill, a crude preparation, or a peptide that was never purified beyond a generic gradient. The [manufacturer verification protocol](/blog/china-peptide-manufacturer-verification-a-2026-b2b-sourcing-protocol/) sets out the documents to request before a first order, and the [purity versus concentration note](/blog/why-purity-and-concentration-tests-are-not-enough/) explains why a single percentage cannot carry a specification.

Customs paperwork is routine for a lyophilized peptide, but the description on the invoice should match the product. A parcel described only as "peptide sample" invites a hold, and a research-use statement should travel with the commercial documents. ## Handling & Storage

Lyophilized KEDG is hygroscopic. Let the vial equilibrate to room temperature inside a sealed bag before opening it; a cold vial opened in a humid room will condense water onto the cake, and a damp cake weighs more and dissolves unevenly. Work quickly, reseal with a dry stopper, and keep the desiccant in the outer container.

Reconstitute gently. Add solvent down the wall of the vial rather than onto the pellet, then roll or rock until the solution is clear. Vortexing a small peptide is unnecessary and creates foam that denatures material at the air-liquid interface. If the powder resists dissolution, check the pH and ionic strength before adding more solvent; KEDG carries two acidic residues and a basic lysine, so its solubility is pH-dependent.

Buffer choice should follow the assay. Phosphate-buffered saline is a reasonable default for cell work, but phosphate can complicate downstream mass spectrometry, and a volatile buffer such as ammonium acetate is easier to remove if the sample will be analysed. Avoid strong alkali; the Asp-Gly motif is most vulnerable to succinimide formation at neutral to alkaline pH, and a stock left at pH 8 for a week is not the same material as a fresh one.

Divide the reconstituted solution into single-use aliquots and freeze at −20°C or −80°C. Repeated freeze-thaw cycles concentrate the peptide at the ice interface and promote aggregation, and each cycle adds another opportunity for isoaspartate formation. For short-term work, 2-8°C is acceptable for days, not weeks. Protect from light as a routine measure; there is no aromatic chromophore to photodegrade, but the amide and carboxyl side chains are not the only things in the vial.

If the assay is sensitive to adsorption, use low-binding polypropylene tubes and add a carrier protein where the buffer permits. A dilute tetrapeptide solution in a plain plastic tube can lose a meaningful fraction to the wall, and the loss is worst at the low concentrations where the effect matters. The [reconstitution guide](/blog/peptide-lyophilization-reconstitution-guide-for-researchers/) covers technique, and the [solubility troubleshooting note](/blog/peptide-solubility-troubleshooting/) is the place to start when a cake will not dissolve.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is testagen the same as KEDG?**

Yes. Testagen is the catalogue name used for the synthetic tetrapeptide Lys-Glu-Asp-Gly, commonly abbreviated KEDG. The name does not imply a tissue extract, and a certificate should print the sequence rather than rely on the trade name.

**Is testagen a natural peptide or a synthetic one?**

The sequence motif is associated with testicular tissue in the bioregulator literature, but the research material is produced by solid-phase synthesis. The vial contains a synthetic tetrapeptide salt, not a tissue extract. Buyers should ask for the synthesis route and the counterion if those distinctions matter to the assay.

**Why does the catalogue not list a CAS number or molecular weight?**

Because the listing has not been populated with those fields. For a defined tetrapeptide, the manufacturer should be able to supply the free-base mass, the salt form, and a lot-specific mass spectrum. Their absence from the page is a documentation gap, not evidence that the compound is undefined.

**What is the main degradation risk for KEDG?**

Isoaspartate formation at the Asp-Gly sequence. The reaction proceeds through a succinimide intermediate and produces a peptide with the same mass but a different backbone. It is invisible to a simple mass check and can co-elute with the parent peak on a shallow gradient.

**How should the powder be stored before reconstitution?**

At −20°C, dry, protected from light, and sealed with desiccant. Allow the vial to reach room temperature in a sealed bag before opening to avoid condensation. Avoid repeated warming and cooling of the stock container.

**Can I use a generic HPLC gradient to release this peptide?**

You can, but the result will be weaker than the certificate implies. A generic gradient may not resolve isoaspartate or a one-residue deletion. Ask for the method and for a stressed-sample chromatogram showing that the degradants were separated from the main peak.

**What should I do if the reconstituted solution turns cloudy?**

Do not filter and proceed. Cloudiness usually means aggregation or incomplete dissolution of the salt, and filtering removes material while leaving the assay with an unknown concentration. Check the buffer pH and ionic strength, then prepare a fresh aliquot.
