## Compound Profile

The two components sit on different branches of the GH-axis receptor map. Tesamorelin is a stabilized GHRH analogue: a 44-residue peptide with an N-terminal modification that slows DPP-IV cleavage, developed as a longer-acting GHRHR agonist. Ipamorelin is a pentapeptide GHSR-1a agonist with two non-natural residues and no disulfide bond. Their sizes differ by roughly a factor of seven, which is useful analytically, because a mass spectrometer separates them easily even when a single chromatographic gradient does not.

## Why This Compound

The pair is studied because GHRHR and GHSR-1a sit on the same somatotroph and use different second messengers. Tesamorelin activates GHRHR, raising cAMP and driving the protein kinase A arm. Ipamorelin activates GHSR-1a, raising intracellular calcium through the Gq arm. When both are engaged, the secretory response in rodent pituitary models is larger than the sum of the individual responses, which is the mechanistic case for a combination design.

What distinguishes this blend from the CJC-1295 + Ipamorelin product is the GHRH component. Mod GRF 1-29 is a 29-residue fragment that clears in about half an hour. Tesamorelin is a 44-residue analogue with a longer residence time, so the GHRH input persists while the ipamorelin input rises and falls. That produces a different exposure shape: an extended GHRHR signal with a shorter GHSR-1a pulse layered onto it. Whether that shape is appropriate depends on the question. For pulse-architecture work, a short GHRH component is easier to interpret. For work that needs a sustained GHRH background, tesamorelin is the more suitable partner.

The 11:6 ratio is another variable. It is not the ratio used in the 5 + 5 mg CJC blend, and it is not a molar ratio. A milligram label on two peptides of very different molecular weight translates into a molar proportion weighted toward the smaller molecule. A study that reports concentrations in molar terms has to do that conversion explicitly; a study that reports nominal mass concentrations can use the label directly. Mixing the two conventions is a common source of confusion in blend work.

The limited-production note on this listing is not marketing language. Custom fills are scheduled in batches, and a repeat run is not guaranteed. A lab that standardises on this exact ratio should either reserve stock or accept that the next lot may require a bridging study. That is an operational risk, separate from the analytical risk, and it belongs in the procurement plan.

## What the Batch Record Should Show

Identity has to be established for both components. Ipamorelin's intact mass is 711.85 Da and is straightforward to confirm. Tesamorelin is a 44-residue peptide, and its intact mass should be reported with a spectrum rather than a nominal figure. A peptide of that length can carry deamidated, oxidized or truncated variants that differ by small mass shifts, so the identity section should show the main peak and the adjacent impurity peaks, not just a single number.

The ratio is the defining specification. For an 11 + 6 mg fill, the certificate should state the measured content of tesamorelin and ipamorelin separately, the method used, and the acceptance range. A label ratio without a measured assay is a fill instruction. Ask whether the assay was run on the reconstituted fill solution before lyophilisation or on the finished cake after. The second is the one that describes the product.

Content uniformity deserves more attention here than on a single-peptide page. A custom ratio has less process history behind it than a standard fill, and the two components differ in size, hydrophobicity and solubility. The record should show assays from multiple vials across the fill, with a mean and a relative standard deviation. One composite result cannot distinguish a homogeneous batch from one that separated during freezing.

Purity should be reported per component. Tesamorelin, as a 44-mer, has more potential deamidation and oxidation sites than ipamorelin, and its related-substances profile is correspondingly richer. Ipamorelin's degradants are mostly hydrolysis products and aggregates. A combined "≥98%" figure can hide a tesamorelin impurity that co-elutes with the ipamorelin peak, or the reverse. Ask for the two profiles separately.

The rest of the package is standard: water content by Karl Fischer, acetate content, residual TFA, fill mass, cake appearance, pH of the reconstituted solution, endotoxin by LAL, and bioburden or sterility where claimed. For a 17 mg fill, cake appearance is worth photographing at receipt. A cake that has shrunk, cracked or changed color has experienced moisture or thermal stress, and the assay may not capture that.

## Sourcing & Verification

Ask why the ratio is 11:6. A supplier who formulated it for a specific research protocol should be able to say so. A supplier who cannot explain the ratio is likely filling to a customer's specification without owning the process, which is acceptable if the documentation is good and risky if it is not.

Ask for the per-component assay method and the uniformity data. Ask whether the tesamorelin raw material was tested for identity and purity before filling, and whether the ipamorelin lot has its own certificate. A blend certificate that does not reference the two parent lots is incomplete, because it cannot be traced back to the materials that were mixed.

Ask about the fill sequence. Were the two peptides dissolved together and lyophilized as one cake, or filled as separate cakes in the same vial? The first depends on solution homogeneity; the second depends on fill accuracy for each component. Both can work, but the failure modes differ, and the uniformity data should match the process.

Ask about the regulatory status of tesamorelin as an API. Because the molecule is the active ingredient in an approved product, some suppliers will not ship it, and others will ship it only with research-use documentation. ## Handling & Storage

A -20°C freezer with desiccant, away from light, is the correct home for the vial. Let it reach room temperature before opening. The 44-residue component is the more sensitive of the two: longer peptides have more opportunities for aggregation and for slow conformational change at interfaces, and a cake that has absorbed moisture will degrade faster than the certificate's storage condition assumes.

Reconstitute gently. Add solvent down the wall, swirl, and wait. Tesamorelin carries a hydrophobic N-terminal modification, and ipamorelin carries a naphthylalanine residue; neither is instantly soluble at high concentration. A cloudy solution or a persistent film suggests incomplete dissolution, and the ratio in the liquid phase may not match the label. Do not vortex, and do not heat the vial to speed things up.

For cell work, prepare a concentrated stock and dilute into buffer containing a carrier protein. Both peptides adsorb to plastic and glass at low concentration. If the assay uses serum, note that serum contains DPP-IV activity; tesamorelin's N-terminal modification slows cleavage, but it does not make the peptide invisible to every protease in a complex medium. Report the medium composition and the incubation time.

Aliquot before freezing. Use the reconstituted blend within two weeks at 2–8°C; longer campaigns should use single-use aliquots at -80°C. Repeated freeze-thaw cycles change the aggregation profile of the larger component first, and the change may not be visible.
