## Why This Compound

Thymosin β4 is an abundant actin-sequestering protein. It binds globular actin in a 1:1 complex and helps maintain the monomer pool that cells draw on when they change shape, move or divide. The actin-binding site was mapped by mutational analysis to a region in the middle of the sequence, and residues 17-24 sit inside that region. The fragment LKKTETQE — acetylated at the N-terminus in some synthetic batches — is short enough to be made by solid-phase synthesis and small enough to be studied by biophysical methods that would be difficult with the full protein.

The sequence of the mature protein begins Ac-Ser-Asp-Lys-Pro..., and the first four residues, Ac-SDKP, are cleaved in vivo and studied as a separate peptide. The 17-24 fragment does not contain that N-terminal tetrapeptide, the N-terminal acetylation, or the rest of the protein. It contains the actin-binding motif and little else. That is the point of buying it: if the experiment is about the motif, the fragment removes the confounding presence of the full protein. If the experiment is about the full protein's behaviour in a tissue model, the fragment is not a substitute.

The naming history adds confusion. "TB-500" is used in the research market for the full 43-residue thymosin β4 sequence, and it has also been used in regulatory contexts for a short fragment. Catalogue products add a residue range to disambiguate: 17-23 and 17-24 are both sold, and they differ by a single glutamate. That difference changes the mass, the charge and the C-terminal context. A certificate that says "TB-500 fragment" without the range has not told you which peptide is in the vial.

The research literature on the fragment is mostly biophysical and cell-biological. It includes actin-binding assays, competition experiments, and studies of how the motif interacts with the cytoskeleton. The literature on the full protein is larger and includes cardiac, corneal and dermal models that the fragment has not been tested in. A reader who transfers those results to the fragment is making an assumption that the data do not support. The [comparison of TB-500 and thymosin β4](/blog/tb-500-vs-thymosin-beta-4-what-you-re-actually-getting/) covers the naming history; the [TB-500 product page](/catalog/tb-500/) describes the full sequence.

Analytically, the fragment is small and polar. It has two lysines and two glutamates, no tryptophan, tyrosine or phenylalanine, and no cysteine or methionine. The absence of aromatic residues means no useful absorbance at 280 nm; the absence of cysteine and methionine removes the oxidation and disulfide problems that dominate larger peptides. What remains is a molecule whose fill weight is heavily influenced by counterion and water, and whose purity method has to resolve a one-residue truncation. Those are the questions the batch record should answer.

## What the Batch Record Should Show

For an eight-residue peptide, the certificate can be short, but it has to answer the identity question precisely. The difference between a correct batch and a plausible one is often one amino acid.

**Mass and sequence.** The fragment has a defined sequence, LKKTETQE, and a corresponding calculated mass. Electrospray ionisation in positive mode gives singly and doubly charged ions for a peptide this size; the certificate should show the deconvoluted mass and compare it with the calculated value, rather than printing a single peak. It should also state whether the N-terminus is free or acetylated; acetylation adds about 42 Da and is a common synthetic choice. A mass that is 42 Da above the calculated free peptide is not necessarily an error, but it is a different molecule and the certificate should say so.

**The residue window.** The 17-23 variant lacks the C-terminal glutamate and is about 129 Da lighter. A one-residue truncation can arise from incomplete coupling or from a supplier deliberately selling the shorter fragment under a similar name. The certificate should print the sequence, not just the range, and the chromatogram should show whether a truncated species is present. This is the single most useful check for this product.

**Purity at 214 nm.** The peptide bond absorbs at 214 nm; there is no aromatic chromophore. The certificate should describe the column, mobile phase, gradient and integration settings. Because the molecule is small and hydrophilic, it elutes early on a conventional reversed-phase column, and a short generic gradient may not resolve it from the solvent front or from deletion sequences. An area-percent number presented without the chromatogram describes a peak, not a method.

**Counterion, water and peptide content.** The fragment is usually supplied as the acetate salt, and residual trifluoroacetate can remain from purification. Both contribute mass. Karl Fischer titration gives water; nitrogen analysis or amino acid analysis gives peptide content. For a 5 mg vial, the difference between fill weight and peptide content can be a large fraction of the label. The [COA reading guide](/blog/how-to-read-a-peptide-coa-a-researchers-guide-to-hplc-and-ms-reports/) explains how to compare the stated fill with the measured content.

**Endotoxin, appearance and batch linkage.** Endotoxin matters for cell-based assays. A normal cake is white to off-white and sits as a solid plug; shrinkage or discoloration is a warning. The lot number on the vial should match the certificate, and the certificate should carry a synthesis date and a retest date. Because the peptide has no chromophore and no unstable side chain, a batch that is properly characterised at release is likely to remain stable for a long time; the weak point is the documentation, not the chemistry.

## Handling & Storage

Hold the lyophilized powder at −20°C, protected from light and moisture. Let the vial warm to room temperature before opening; condensation on a cold cake is a source of water uptake. Keep the vial in a desiccator between uses, and avoid repeated warming events.

Reconstitution is straightforward. The peptide is freely soluble in water, and the solution should be clear after gentle swirling. Add the solvent against the vial wall; do not vortex. Foaming exposes the peptide to an air-liquid interface, and although this fragment has no cysteine or methionine, surface aggregation can still remove material from solution. Use low-bind tubes for dilute stocks, and avoid leaving a dilute solution on the bench for longer than the experiment requires.

Prepare solutions on the day of use and keep them cold only while the assay is running. For storage beyond a day, freeze small single-use aliquots at −20°C or lower and thaw only what the experiment needs. Repeated freeze-thaw can concentrate the peptide at the ice interface and promote aggregation. The fragment has no chromophore, so light protection is less critical than for a tryptophan-containing peptide, but standard dark storage costs nothing.

Filtration is a minor risk for this peptide. A 0.22 µm low-protein-binding filter is suitable, but check recovery if the assay is quantitative; small hydrophilic peptides can pass through most membranes, while some cellulose-based filters adsorb a measurable fraction. The [handling and protocols pillar](/peptide-handling-protocols/) covers the general workflow.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is TB-500 Fragment (17-24) the same as TB-500?**

No. TB-500 usually refers to the full 43-residue thymosin β4 sequence. The fragment is an eight-residue peptide from the actin-binding region. The two have different masses, different handling requirements and different literatures.

**Which residues are in the fragment?**

It corresponds to residues 17-24 of mature thymosin β4, with the sequence LKKTETQE. The certificate should state the sequence and the observed mass, not just the residue range.

**Why does the difference between 17-23 and 17-24 matter?**

The 17-23 variant lacks the C-terminal glutamate and is about 129 Da lighter. A one-residue truncation changes the mass and the C-terminal charge, and it can arise from incomplete synthesis. The certificate should resolve the two.

**What purity does the catalogue list?**

The page says ≥98% by HPLC. The useful follow-up is whether the gradient resolves the 17-23 truncation, so ask for the chromatogram and the method.

**Is the fragment acetylated?**

It depends on the batch. Native thymosin β4 is N-acetylated at its first residue, but the 17-24 fragment starts at Leu17, so the native acetylation is not present. Some synthetic batches may be acetylated for stability. The certificate should state which form is in the vial.

**How should the lyophilized powder be stored?**

Store the vial at −20°C in a desiccator, away from light. Let it reach room temperature before opening. Make solutions for the day and keep them cold during the assay.

**What sizes are available?**

The catalogue lists 5 mg and 10 mg vials in 10-vial packs. Confirm the peptide content per vial, not just the fill weight.

**Can I use full-protein data to interpret a fragment experiment?**

Only with caution. The fragment contains the actin-binding motif but not the rest of the protein. Results from full-length thymosin β4 models should not be assumed to apply to the fragment without fragment-specific evidence.
