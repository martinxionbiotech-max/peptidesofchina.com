Thymalin is the most awkward kind of product in a peptide catalogue: a polypeptide fraction rather than a defined molecule. The listing describes short thymic peptides in the 1-6 kDa range, a thymus tissue origin, and a ≥98% purity specification. Those three statements do not sit comfortably together. A mixture of peptides has no single sequence to confirm, no single molecular weight to report, and no meaningful way to express purity as one HPLC area percentage unless the method and the reference standard are defined.

That does not make the product illegitimate. Tissue-derived peptide fractions have a long research history, and a laboratory may have a legitimate reason to use one. It does mean the buyer has to evaluate a different document set from the one used for a synthetic peptide. This page is about that document set: what a mixture can prove, what it cannot, and where the sourcing risk actually sits.

The framing throughout is laboratory research; nothing here supports a human or veterinary application.

## Why This Compound

Thymalin comes out of the peptide bioregulator programme associated with the St. Petersburg school, where tissue-specific extracts were fractionated and the active short-peptide components were studied for effects on gene expression in the corresponding tissue. The thymus version sits alongside epithalamin from the pineal gland and similar preparations from other organs. The historical literature reports changes in thymus morphology, T-cell subset composition, and cytokine profiles in aged animals. Those are the endpoints the studies measured; they are not properties a buyer can assume for an uncharacterised lot.

The biological rationale for using a thymus-derived fraction is straightforward to state and hard to test. The thymus involutes with age, the cortex thins, thymic epithelial cells are lost, and naive T-cell output falls. A preparation that contains short thymic peptides might, in principle, supply sequences that influence that tissue. The word "might" is doing a lot of work. A mixture of dozens of peptides at unknown relative abundance has no single mechanism, and the literature rarely reports which component was responsible for an observed change.

For a research design, the practical value of thymalin is as a historical reference material. If a laboratory is comparing an old fraction-based preparation with a defined synthetic peptide such as thymosin alpha-1, thymalin provides the "complex mixture" arm of the comparison. That is a legitimate use. It becomes a weak use when the fraction is treated as a defined drug with a known active ingredient, because the composition of commercial lots is not standardised against a public reference.

The 1-6 kDa range is the most useful piece of information on the page. It tells you the material is not a single 28-mer, and it tells you that size-exclusion chromatography is a more informative identity test than reversed-phase HPLC alone. It also tells you that the fraction will behave differently from lot to lot unless the manufacturer controls the extraction and the cut-off. A supplier who reports only "≥98% by HPLC" for this material has applied a single-peptide specification to a mixture and hoped the buyer would not notice.

## What the Batch Record Should Show

A useful record for a fraction starts with the tissue, not the peptide. Species of origin, tissue, collection conditions, and the inactivation step are the first lines. Bovine and porcine thymus carry different regulatory implications, and a supplier that cannot name the species has not documented the raw material.

**Molecular-weight distribution.** Size-exclusion chromatography or SDS-PAGE with a calibrated marker set should show the fraction's mass range and the proportion of material outside the stated 1-6 kDa window. A large high-molecular-weight shoulder suggests incomplete digestion or aggregation. A large low-molecular-weight tail suggests over-digestion and a loss of the components the preparation is supposed to contain. The distribution is the closest thing a mixture has to an identity.

**Peptide content.** Total peptide should be measured by a method that does not depend on a single sequence: nitrogen determination, amino acid analysis after hydrolysis, or a colorimetric protein assay calibrated against a known standard. Each method has a different bias, so the certificate should name the method. A BCA result and a Lowry result on the same lot can differ by a factor that matters when the vial is quantified by mass.

**Composition fingerprint.** Reversed-phase HPLC of the fraction should produce a reproducible pattern of peaks, not a single sharp peak. The pattern is the fingerprint. Two lots can be compared by overlay, and a lot that produces a flat or wildly different chromatogram is not the same material even if both pass a total-peptide assay.

**Residual tissue components.** DNA, host protein, and lipids can carry through an extraction. The certificate should report residual DNA where the method allows, and the manufacturing description should state whether a precipitation or ultrafiltration step removes high-molecular-weight material.

**Endotoxin and bioburden.** For any material derived from animal tissue, endotoxin is a primary release test, not an afterthought. Report EU/mg and name the method. Sterility applies only if the product is sterile; a lyophilized extract that is not sterile should not carry a sterility claim.

**TSE risk documentation.** This is the item that separates a serious tissue-derived supplier from a repackager. The raw material should be accompanied by a statement of species and tissue of origin, the country of collection, and the inactivation process. The relevant framework is the EMA note on minimising the risk of transmitting animal spongiform encephalopathy agents, and a supplier should be able to say which edition it works to. A certificate that is silent on species and tissue is not adequate for a thymus-derived product, regardless of the purity figure.

**Lot traceability.** The lot on the vial, the lot on the certificate, and the lot on the tissue record should be the same. In a fraction supply chain, the link between the tissue batch and the filled vial is the first thing that breaks.

## Sourcing & Verification

Thymalin is sold in two very different forms, and the listing rarely makes the difference clear. One is a tissue-derived extract, produced by controlled digestion and fractionation. The other is a synthetic mixture, or a synthetic peptide blended to mimic a fraction. The analytical documents differ, the regulatory exposure differs, and the price differs. Ask which one is being offered before comparing quotes.

Tissue-derived material carries a supply chain that is short but opaque. The tissue is collected at an abattoir, frozen or dried, digested, clarified, and fractionated. Each step can be outsourced, and the further the material travels, the harder it is to trace. A supplier that buys "thymus peptide powder" from a second party and fills it into vials is not manufacturing the product; it is repackaging an unknown. The [manufacturer verification protocol](/blog/china-peptide-manufacturer-verification-a-2026-b2b-sourcing-protocol/) lists the questions that establish who actually performs each step.

Three checks are worth the effort. First, ask for the tissue country of origin and the inactivation method in writing. Second, ask for a lot-specific molecular-weight distribution, not a specification sheet. Third, ask for a sample of the actual lot and have an independent laboratory run SEC and a total-peptide assay. The third check is the one that catches a relabelled bulk powder.

The ≥98% figure needs a caveat. For a mixture, purity can be defined as the proportion of the chromatographic area that falls within the expected retention window, or as the proportion of material within the molecular-weight cut-off, or as the absence of a specific contaminant. Each definition gives a different number. A certificate should state which one it uses. Without that sentence, the percentage is a marketing device.

Price is informative here. A genuine tissue-derived fraction requires tissue collection, digestion, fractionation, and TSE documentation. A synthetic tetrapeptide does not. If thymalin is priced like a tetrapeptide, the buyer is probably looking at a synthetic blend or a very small fill. If it is priced like a biologic, the documentation should reflect that. The [purity versus concentration note](/blog/why-purity-and-concentration-tests-are-not-enough/) explains why this distinction is easy to miss when comparing catalogue prices.

Shipping and customs add another layer. A tissue-derived product may attract additional scrutiny, and the invoice description should be accurate without overstating what the material is. The [customs guide](/blog/research-peptide-customs-clearance-a-professional-b2b-sourcing-guide-2026/) covers the documents that should travel with the parcel, and the [compliance pillar](/peptide-compliance-risk/) sets out the research-use boundary.

## Handling & Storage

A lyophilized fraction is more hygroscopic than a pure crystalline peptide because it contains salts and low-molecular-weight components that hold water. Equilibrate the vial in a sealed bag before opening, and reseal it promptly. A damp fraction can cake, and a caked cake dissolves slowly and unevenly.

Reconstitution should be gentle. Add buffer down the wall, swirl or roll, and avoid vortexing. The mixture contains components of different solubility, so a cloudy solution may mean that one fraction has not dissolved rather than that the whole preparation has failed. If the protocol permits, centrifuge briefly and inspect the pellet before deciding to filter. Filtration through a 0.22 µm membrane will remove aggregated material and some large peptides, which changes the composition of what reaches the assay.

Buffer choice is a compromise. Phosphate-buffered saline is compatible with most cell work. A volatile buffer such as ammonium acetate is easier to remove if the sample will be analysed by mass spectrometry. Avoid alkaline conditions; peptide fractions contain aspartyl and asparaginyl residues that can deamidate, and the resulting charge change alters both retention and receptor behaviour.

The catalogue advises use within 24 hours of reconstitution at 2-8°C. That is a sensible default for a mixture with unknown protease content. If the fraction is to be used over several days, aliquot and freeze at −20°C or −80°C, and accept that each thaw introduces a small compositional change. Do not refreeze a solution that has been at room temperature for a working day.

Sterility is not the same as cleanliness. A non-sterile powder can be free of visible particles and still carry endotoxin. If the assay is sensitive to pyrogen, use depyrogenated glassware and a low-endotoxin water source, and run a media blank alongside the sample. The [endotoxin primer](/blog/what-are-endotoxins-why-they-matter/) explains the assay choices.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is the difference between thymalin and thymosin alpha-1?**

Thymosin alpha-1 is a single, well-defined 28-amino-acid peptide derived from prothymosin alpha, with a characterised sequence and receptor interactions. Thymalin is a polypeptide fraction from thymus extract containing multiple short peptide components without a single defined sequence. Thymosin alpha-1 suits receptor-specific mechanistic work; thymalin suits broader bioregulator and mixture-level research where the composition is part of the question.

**How does thymalin research relate to thymic rejuvenation?**

Thymic rejuvenation research asks whether the age-related loss of thymic tissue can be slowed or reversed, and it draws on several approaches: defined cytokines, genetic manipulations, and older fraction-based preparations such as thymalin. Thymalin appears in that literature as a historical reference point rather than a characterised agent. A review can cite it alongside defined peptides; a new experiment has to treat it as a variable with its own lot-to-lot composition. The studies measured thymus morphology and peripheral T-cell subsets, and those measurements describe a research context, not a property of the vial.

**What purity specification does your thymalin meet?**

The catalogue specification is ≥98% by HPLC, and the lot certificate should state how that figure was calculated. For a mixture, purity can mean the proportion of chromatographic area within a retention window, the proportion of material within a molecular-weight cut-off, or the absence of a named contaminant. Ask which definition applies before comparing lots.

**What are the storage requirements?**

Lyophilized thymalin should be held at −20°C, dry and protected from light. Reconstituted solutions should be kept at 2-8°C and used within 24 hours, as the catalogue states. The mixture is more susceptible to microbial growth and to peptide degradation than a sterile single-peptide solution.

**Does the certificate include a molecular-weight distribution?**

It should. A fraction has no single molecular weight to report, so SEC or calibrated SDS-PAGE is the identity test that matters. If the certificate lists only an HPLC purity percentage, request the distribution separately and check whether the lot falls within the stated 1-6 kDa range.

**Is the material animal-derived or synthetic?**

Both forms appear in the market, and the listing should say which one is supplied. Animal-derived material requires tissue-origin and TSE documentation. A synthetic blend requires a composition statement and per-component identity data. The analytical and regulatory questions are not interchangeable.

**Can thymalin be sterile-filtered after reconstitution?**

A 0.22 µm filter will remove particles and microorganisms, but it will also remove aggregated peptide and some larger components of the fraction. If the assay needs a sterile solution, filter a portion and accept that the filtered aliquot is not compositionally identical to the unfiltered stock.
