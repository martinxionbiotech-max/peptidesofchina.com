Relaxation PM is not a peptide. It is a four-component aqueous blend: GABA at 100 mg/mL, melatonin at 1 mg/mL, L-arginine at 100 mg/mL and L-glutamine at 25 mg/mL, supplied in a 10 mL vial. The catalogue classifies it as a neurotransmitter and neuromodulator combination. That description is more useful than the product name, because the analytical problem here is not peptide identity. It is the simultaneous measurement of four small molecules with different detection chemistries, different stabilities and different solubilities in one liquid matrix.

A single "≥98% purity" number cannot describe this product. Purity is a property of a compound; this vial contains four. The certificate has to report each component separately, plus the degradation markers that appear in an aqueous blend over time.

## Why This Compound

GABA is the principal inhibitory neurotransmitter in the mammalian central nervous system. It acts on two receptor families: GABA-A, a ligand-gated chloride channel, and GABA-B, a G-protein-coupled receptor that modulates potassium and calcium channels. In vitro work uses primary neuronal cultures, brain slices and heterologous expression systems, with electrophysiological readouts such as inhibitory postsynaptic currents and chloride conductance. Peripheral GABA receptors also exist in the enteric nervous system and on immune cells, which is relevant to research designs that use systemic exposure rather than slice work.

Melatonin is an indoleamine produced by the pineal gland and studied through two G-protein-coupled receptors, MT1 and MT2. The 2016 *Annual Review of Pharmacology and Toxicology* review remains a useful map of the receptor pharmacology: MT1 and MT2 differ in tissue distribution, signalling and ligand selectivity, and the field has struggled to develop subtype-selective tools. In cell models, melatonin is used to study receptor binding, circadian reporter expression and antioxidant chemistry. Its concentration in this blend is low — 1 mg/mL — which makes it the most analytically demanding component.

L-arginine is the substrate for nitric oxide synthase. Neuronal NOS converts arginine to nitric oxide and citrulline, and the pathway is studied in synaptic signalling, vascular tone and inflammatory models. The 2012 *European Heart Journal* review by Förstermann and Sessa is a standard reference for the three NOS isoforms and their regulation. In a blend, arginine is also a pH-active component: its guanidino group has a high pKa, and a 100 mg/mL solution contributes to the buffer capacity.

L-glutamine is a conditionally essential amino acid and the main precursor for glutamate and GABA synthesis in the brain. The glutamate/GABA-glutamine cycle between neurons and astrocytes is the framework for that work, and the 2006 review by Bak, Schousboe and Waagepetersen is a clear entry point. Glutamine is also chemically unstable in water: it hydrolyses to glutamate and ammonia, and the rate depends on pH, temperature and time. A 1999 study in the *European Journal of Pharmaceutical Sciences* characterised the degradation kinetics in aqueous solution. That chemistry is the main stability risk in this product, and it is measurable.

## What the Batch Record Should Show

The certificate should start with four separate assays, each with its own method. GABA has no strong chromophore and is usually measured after derivatisation with ortho-phthalaldehyde or by LC-MS/MS. Melatonin absorbs at 278 nm and can be measured by UV or fluorescence, but at 1 mg/mL in a matrix containing aromatic amino acids, the separation matters more than the detector. Arginine can be measured by HPLC after derivatisation or by an enzymatic assay. Glutamine is usually measured by amino acid analysis, which also reports glutamate — the degradation product.

The mass balance should be reported against the label claim. For each component, the certificate should give the measured concentration and the percentage of label. A result of 95–105% is a reasonable specification for a well-controlled liquid; a result outside that range is a formulation or stability problem. The certificate should also report the sum of the four components and the excipient/buffer composition, because the osmolarity and pH depend on all of them.

Glutamine degradation deserves its own line. For every mole of glutamine lost, one mole of glutamate and one mole of ammonia appear. The certificate should report glutamate and ammonia, or at least state that they were measured. A blend that passes a glutamine assay at 90% of label but contains a large glutamate peak has lost active material and gained a confounder. The [purity versus content note](/blog/why-purity-and-concentration-tests-are-not-enough/) explains why a single purity number cannot capture this.

pH and osmolality are formulation-release tests. A four-component blend at these concentrations is hypertonic, and the pH depends on the arginine and the buffer. Both should be reported, with the measurement temperature. A shift in pH over storage is an early sign of hydrolysis or container interaction.

Sterility and endotoxin are separate release decisions. The catalogue describes a sterile liquid formulation, but the certificate should state the method (membrane filtration or direct inoculation), the result, and the lot. Endotoxin should be measured by LAL with a stated limit. Particulate matter — visible and subvisible — should be checked, especially for a liquid in a multi-use vial. The [sterility guide](/blog/sterility-testing-for-research-peptides-a-protocol-guide/) covers the methods, and the [endotoxin note](/blog/what-are-endotoxins-why-they-matter/) explains the assay.

Container closure and preservative status belong in the record. A multi-use aqueous vial without a preservative has a short in-use window once opened; a preserved vial has a different one. The certificate or package insert should say which. If the product contains a preservative, the identity and concentration should be stated, because preservatives can interfere with cell assays.

Batch traceability closes the record. The lot number on the certificate should match the vial, and the certificate should name the fill site and the date. For a liquid blend, the fill date and the stability data are more informative than the synthesis date of any single component.

## Handling & Storage

Store at 2–8°C, protected from light, and do not freeze. Freezing an aqueous blend can concentrate solutes in the residual liquid phase and precipitate one or more components; on thawing, the concentrations may not return to the label claim. The catalogue's storage instruction is consistent with that chemistry.

Bring the vial to room temperature before opening, but do not warm it above room temperature. Swirl gently to mix; do not shake. A liquid blend can foam, and foaming concentrates surface-active components at the air-liquid interface. If a precipitate is visible, do not assume it will redissolve; check the certificate and the storage history before using the lot.

For cell work, prepare dilutions in the assay medium and record the final concentration of each component, not just the volume of blend added. The osmolarity of the final medium matters, and a hypertonic stock can change cell behaviour independently of the analytes. Run a vehicle control with the same buffer and preservative concentration.

If the vial is multi-dose, work in a laminar flow hood, swab the septum, and use a fresh needle or a sterile syringe. Do not leave the vial open. Record the date of first puncture and discard according to the in-use window on the label. The [handling pillar](/peptide-handling-protocols/) covers the general workflow.

Do not autoclave the vial. Heat will hydrolyse glutamine and degrade melatonin. If sterile filtration is needed for a dilution, use a 0.22 µm membrane and verify that the components do not adsorb to the filter.

The product is supplied for laboratory research only. Nothing here is a protocol for administration to humans or animals.

## How It Compares

**Single-component GABA.** A single GABA reagent is analytically simpler and lets you attribute an effect to one molecule. The blend adds three other active components, which is useful for a formulation study but poor for mechanism work. If the question is about GABA receptors, use GABA alone.

**Single-component melatonin.** Melatonin at 1 mg/mL is easy to measure and easy to dilute. A single-component reagent avoids the glutamine degradation problem entirely. The [melatonin receptor review](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-pharmtox-010814-124742) is the relevant pharmacology reference.

**Lyophilized peptide blends.** A dry blend is more stable than an aqueous one, but it requires reconstitution and has its own homogeneity problems. The analytical logic is similar: each component needs its own assay. The [handling pillar](/peptide-handling-protocols/) covers the reconstitution workflow.

**No equivalence claims.** The blend is not equivalent to any single-component reagent, and the published data for GABA or melatonin alone do not describe the combination. There is no published study of this exact four-component mixture that we could locate.

**What is in Relaxation PM?**

**Is it a peptide?**

No. None of the four components is a peptide. GABA is an amino acid neurotransmitter, melatonin is an indoleamine, and arginine and glutamine are amino acids. The product is listed in a peptide catalogue because of its research category, not its chemistry.

**Why are four components combined?**

The catalogue does not publish a rationale beyond the research axes: GABAergic signalling, melatonin receptor pharmacology, nitric oxide signalling and glutamine metabolism. A combination is useful for formulation or interaction studies; it is poor for attributing an effect to one mechanism. For mechanism work, single-component reagents are cleaner.

**How is a four-component blend tested?**

With four separate assays, each with its own method: derivatisation or LC-MS/MS for GABA, UV or fluorescence for melatonin, HPLC or enzymatic assay for arginine, and amino acid analysis for glutamine. The certificate should also report glutamate and ammonia as glutamine degradation markers.

**Does GABA cross the blood–brain barrier?**

GABA is a zwitterion at physiological pH and has limited passive permeability across the blood–brain barrier. That is a standard limitation in systemic GABA research and the reason most mechanistic work uses cell cultures, slices or local administration models. The blend's other components do not change that property.

**Can I use it in cell culture?**

## The Author's Take

**Position.** I would not buy a four-component liquid blend without a per-component certificate and a glutamine degradation result. A single purity number for this product is not a specification; it is a rounding exercise.

**Reasoning.** The analytical difficulty is the product. Four molecules with different detection chemistries, one of which hydrolyses in water, cannot be released on a single HPLC run. The failure modes are quiet: a blend that meets the label claim on GABA and arginine but has lost a fifth of its glutamine, or a vial that passes concentration testing but is not sterile. Those failures do not announce themselves in an assay; they appear as inconsistent results across batches. A supplier who reports each component and the degradation markers is selling a formulated product. A supplier who reports one number is selling a label.
