Cardiogen is a dipeptide: alanine followed by glutamic acid, two residues and a free N-terminus. At that size the molecule is barely a peptide. It has no secondary structure to preserve, no disulfide to mispair, and no hydrophobic core to aggregate. What it does have is a specification problem that most peptide buyers have never had to think about, because a two-residue chain sits at the boundary between a peptide and a small organic acid.

The research literature places it in the same St Petersburg series as bronchogen, cortagen, and cartalax: short peptides named for the tissue from which they were derived, proposed to act on gene expression rather than on a receptor. Cardiogen's tissue is cardiac muscle, and the published work is mostly transcriptional and mostly preclinical.

## Why This Compound

The bioregulator framework treats short peptides as transcription-level signals. In the Khavinson version, peptides of two to seven residues, corresponding to segments of larger tissue proteins, enter the cell and the nucleus and modulate the expression of genes associated with the tissue of origin. The claim is not that the peptide replaces a protein or binds a receptor; it is that the peptide acts as a regulatory fragment.

Cardiogen is the shortest member of that set. Its two residues, alanine and glutamic acid, are among the most common in any protein, which means the sequence is not unique in a whole-proteome sense. The specificity, if it exists, has to come from context: the combination of the two residues, the free termini, and the concentration range used. That is a thin basis for a tissue-specific claim, and the published work is correspondingly modest in scale.

The most cited cardiac paper from the group used DNA microarray analysis of mouse heart after peptide treatment, with cardiogen among the peptides tested. The readouts were changes in transcript abundance for genes involved in myocardial metabolism and stress response. The study is a screening exercise, not a mechanism study, and its sample size is small. Later reviews from the same group present the results as part of a broader pattern across tissues. Independent replication is limited.

None of that makes the compound uninteresting. A dipeptide is a tractable tool for asking whether a very short sequence can produce a transcriptional change in cardiomyocytes, and the answer matters for the broader bioregulator hypothesis. It is also a useful negative control: if a two-residue peptide produces the same effect as a ten-residue peptide, the effect is unlikely to be sequence-specific. A well-designed study uses cardiogen alongside a scrambled dipeptide and a vehicle, and reports all three.

The chemistry is the other reason to work with it. Dipeptides are small enough to be characterised completely by nuclear magnetic resonance and accurate mass, and small enough that impurities are easy to see. A laboratory that wants to validate an LC-MS method for polar compounds can use cardiogen as a test article: it is water-soluble, it has no chromophore, and it ionises in both positive and negative modes. The same properties make it a difficult analyte in a bioanalytical assay, which is why the method development literature uses dipeptides as challenge compounds.

## What the Batch Record Should Show

Identity for a dipeptide cannot rest on a single mass. Ala-Glu has a small number of possible isomers and sequence reversals, and a low-resolution mass spectrum may not distinguish them. The certificate should report an accurate mass with the calculated value and the mass error, and it should include either a tandem mass spectrum with fragment assignments or a nuclear magnetic resonance spectrum with the diagnostic signals. For a two-residue peptide, the NMR is often the more convincing document.

Purity by HPLC belongs on the record with the method. Cardiogen has no aromatic residues, so detection at 214 nm relies on the peptide bond and the carboxylic acid. The signal is weak, the baseline can drift, and the integration threshold matters. Ask for the chromatogram and the blank, and look for a peak that elutes before or after the main peak: a cyclic diketopiperazine is the most likely degradant and it can be close to the parent.

Diketopiperazine formation deserves its own field. A dipeptide with a free N-terminus and a free C-terminus can cyclise by intramolecular attack of the amine on the carbonyl, releasing water and forming a six-membered ring. The reaction is slow at neutral pH and room temperature, faster at high pH and higher temperature, and it is the reason dipeptide solutions should not be autoclaved or stored warm. The cyclic form has a different mass, a different retention time, and no free charges. A certificate that reports only "98%" may be counting the cyclic form as product if the chromatography does not resolve it.

Counterion and water content follow. The free peptide is acidic because of the glutamate side chain; the sodium salt is more soluble and more hygroscopic. The certificate should state which form was filled, the counterion content, and the water content by Karl Fischer. On a 20 mg fill, a few percent water changes the amount of peptide in the vial by hundreds of micrograms.

Residual trifluoroacetic acid and residual solvents belong on the record if the peptide was purified by preparative HPLC. For a dipeptide, TFA can be a significant fraction of the dry mass if the lyophilisation cycle was short. Ion chromatography or fluorine NMR gives the number.

Endotoxin and sterility are separate. A lyophilized powder is not sterile by default, and the LAL result should be reported with units and limit. The [endotoxin note](/blog/what-are-endotoxins-why-they-matter/) explains why a low endotoxin figure can matter more than an extra point of purity for primary cardiomyocytes.

Batch linkage closes the record. The vial number and the certificate number must match, and the document should carry synthesis date, purification method, fill date, and retest date. The [CoA guide](/blog/how-to-read-a-peptide-coa-a-researchers-guide-to-hplc-and-ms-reports/) walks through the fields.

## Sourcing & Verification

Start with the sequence and the salt form. "Cardiogen" is a catalogue name, not a chemical identity. Ask for the two residues in order, the counterion, and the observed accurate mass. A supplier who answers with a purity percentage has not answered the identity question.

Ask how the dipeptide was made. Solid-phase synthesis of two residues is simple, but the workup and the salt exchange are where the variability lives. A supplier who can describe the resin, the cleavage, the purification, and the ion exchange is a manufacturer or a close partner. A supplier who cannot is a trader.

Ask about the diketopiperazine control. This is the question that separates a peptide chemist from a reseller. A manufacturer who understands dipeptides will have a chromatographic method that resolves the cyclic form and will report it. A reseller will not know what the question means. The answer is more informative than the certificate's purity figure.

Ask about the fill and the peptide content. A 20 mg fill is small, and the difference between fill mass and peptide mass is larger for a hygroscopic salt. The certificate should give peptide content per vial, not only the fill weight. The [quality-testing overview](/peptide-quality-testing/) lists the tests and what each establishes.

Ask about storage history. A dipeptide is chemically stable when dry and cool, but a lot that sat in a warm warehouse for a year may have cyclised at the surface. Ask for the retest date and the storage condition, and treat a certificate older than the retest date as expired.

Finally, ask for a pilot vial and run your own HPLC. For a compound this small, a single injection with a photodiode array detector and a mass spectrometer will tell you more than any certificate. The [manufacturer verification protocol](/blog/china-peptide-manufacturer-verification-a-2026-b2b-sourcing-protocol/) covers the entity checks that sit behind the document.

## Handling & Storage

Store the lyophilized powder at −20 °C, desiccated, protected from light. Let the vial reach room temperature before opening; a cold vial collects condensation and a hygroscopic dipeptide will absorb it.

Reconstitute with water or a buffer at neutral pH. Add the solvent gently and swirl; do not heat, and do not sonicate for long periods. Heat and high pH both accelerate diketopiperazine formation. If the peptide is slow to dissolve, check the pH and the ionic strength before adding more solvent.

Avoid storing reconstituted solutions for long periods. A dipeptide has no secondary structure to lose, but it has a cyclisation pathway that runs faster in solution than in the dry state. Prepare the working solution on the day of the experiment, or aliquot and freeze it at −20 °C for short-term storage. The [reconstitution guide](/blog/peptide-lyophilization-reconstitution-guide-for-researchers/) covers solvent and container choice.

Do not autoclave the solution or the powder. Steam sterilisation will hydrolyse the peptide and drive cyclisation, and the container may not survive the cycle. If a sterile preparation is required, filter through a 0.22 µm membrane and confirm recovery.

Use polypropylene tubes for dilute stocks. The peptide is small and polar, and adsorption to glass and polystyrene is measurable at low concentrations. A recovery check with a quantitative assay is cheaper than repeating an experiment. The [handling hub](/peptide-handling-protocols/) covers general laboratory practice.

The steps above are bench procedures for a research reagent. They are not instructions for preparing anything for human or animal use.

## How It Compares

**Bronchogen (Ala–Glu–Asp–Leu).** The pulmonary tetrapeptide in the same family. It shares the first two residues with cardiogen, which makes it a useful comparator for asking whether the additional residues change the transcriptional readout. The two are not interchangeable; the mass difference is large and the literature is separate.

**Cartalax (Ala–Glu–Asp–Pro).** The cartilage tetrapeptide. Same N-terminal dipeptide, different tail and different tissue literature. A study that compares cardiogen with cartalax is testing the contribution of the C-terminal residues.

**Cortagen (Ala–Glu–Asp–Gly).** The cortical tetrapeptide, and the member of the family with the most published chromatin work. It is a comparator for mechanism studies rather than for cardiac endpoints.

**Chonluten (Glu–Asp–Leu).** A tripeptide that does not share the N-terminal alanine. It is a useful negative control when the question is whether the alanine at position one matters.

**Vesugen and other family members.** Catalogue entries with their own sequences and tissue associations. They are not substitutes for cardiogen, and their certificates will differ in mass and counterion. Compare sequences before comparing prices.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is cardiogen?**

Cardiogen is a synthetic dipeptide with the sequence Ala-Glu, supplied as a lyophilized powder for laboratory research. It is one of the short peptide bioregulators associated with the St Petersburg gerontology group and is named for cardiac tissue.

**Is a dipeptide stable in solution?**

It is chemically simple but not inert. The free amine and the free carboxylate can react to form a diketopiperazine, especially at high pH or elevated temperature. Prepare working solutions fresh, keep them cool, and avoid autoclaving.

**What should the certificate show for identity?**

An accurate mass with the calculated value and mass error, plus either a tandem mass spectrum with fragment assignments or an NMR spectrum with diagnostic signals. A single low-resolution mass is not enough to distinguish a dipeptide from its isomers.

**Why does the counterion matter?**

The free peptide and the sodium or acetate salt differ in mass, solubility, and hygroscopicity. The certificate should state which form was filled and give the peptide content per vial, not only the fill weight.

**Can cardiogen be used in cell culture?**

Yes, with a sterile, endotoxin-tested preparation and a vehicle control. Filter through a 0.22 µm membrane and check recovery. For primary cardiomyocytes, endotoxin can dominate the readout.

**Does the catalogue list a CAS number?**

No. The page does not print a CAS number, molecular formula, or molecular weight for this product. Ask the supplier for the accurate mass and the salt form rather than relying on a CAS lookup.

**How is cardiogen different from bronchogen?**

Bronchogen is the tetrapeptide Ala-Glu-Asp-Leu; cardiogen is the dipeptide Ala-Glu. They share the first two residues but differ in size, mass, and literature. They are separate products and should not be substituted.

**Is there clinical evidence for cardiogen?**
