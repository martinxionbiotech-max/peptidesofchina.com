It is the shortest member of the pulmonary branch of the short peptide bioregulator family, and it corresponds exactly to the last three residues of bronchogen's tetrapeptide sequence. That relationship is the most useful thing a buyer can know about it: chonluten is not a different concept from bronchogen, it is a truncated version of the same sequence, sold under a separate name.

## Why This Compound

The bioregulator hypothesis is built on fragments. A tissue protein is imagined as a set of short sequences that can act as regulatory signals, and each peptide in the family is a fragment of a larger protein associated with a tissue. Chonluten's EDL is the C-terminal tripeptide of bronchogen's AEDL, which is itself a fragment of a larger bronchial protein. The family logic predicts that the shorter fragment retains some of the activity and loses some of the specificity.

That prediction is testable, and it is the most interesting reason to work with the compound. A laboratory can compare EDL with AEDL in the same assay, at the same molar concentrations, and ask whether the extra alanine changes the readout. If the two behave identically, the N-terminal residue is not doing much; if they diverge, the difference is a clue about the recognition mechanism. Very few catalogue peptides come with such a clean structural comparator.

The published literature on chonluten itself is thin. It appears in reviews of the bioregulator family, in studies of pulmonary epithelium, and in the gerontology literature from the originating group. The endpoints are transcriptional and antioxidant: expression of genes involved in epithelial defence, and markers of oxidative stress in respiratory tissue. The studies are small and have not been widely replicated. A laboratory should treat the compound as a tool for testing the family hypothesis, not as a validated modulator of a pathway.

The chemistry is more tractable than the biology. EDL has three residues, two acidic side chains, and a leucine at the C-terminus. It has no aromatic residues, no cysteine, and no methionine. It is water-soluble, chemically stable when dry, and not prone to oxidation. The analytical challenge is detection: without a chromophore, UV absorbance at 214 nm depends on the peptide bond, and the signal is modest. That makes mass detection or a derivatisation step more useful than a simple UV chromatogram.

There is one structural detail worth noting. The Glu-Asp sequence contains two carboxylic acids in a row. At low pH, the peptide is protonated and less soluble; at neutral pH, it carries a net negative charge. That charge affects adsorption to surfaces, and it affects the choice of counterion. A sodium salt is more soluble than the free acid, and the certificate should say which one is in the vial.

## What the Batch Record Should Show

Identity for a tripeptide rests on mass and fragmentation. The certificate should give the observed mass, the calculated mass, and the mass error, plus a tandem mass spectrum with the b- and y-ion series assigned. With only three residues, the number of fragments is small, which makes the assignment easy to check — and makes a missing assignment harder to excuse.

Purity by HPLC should come with the method and the chromatogram. Because EDL lacks a chromophore, detection at 214 nm is standard but the baseline can be noisy. Ask for the blank injection and the integration threshold. A purity figure obtained with a steep gradient on a short column can hide diastereomers and deletion sequences that a longer method would resolve.

The impurity that matters most is the deletion sequence. A tripeptide synthesis that misses a coupling step produces a dipeptide, and the dipeptide can be close in retention time. Mass detection resolves it; UV alone may not. Ask whether the method used a mass spectrometer, and if not, ask for the mass balance.

Counterion and water content follow. EDL is acidic, and the sodium salt is the usual form for a water-soluble preparation. The certificate should state the counterion, the water content by Karl Fischer, and the peptide content per vial. On a 20 mg fill, a few percent water is a few hundred micrograms of peptide.

Residual TFA belongs on the record if the peptide was purified by reversed-phase HPLC. For a tripeptide, TFA can be a meaningful fraction of the dry mass, and it can affect cell assays. Ion chromatography or fluorine NMR gives the number.

Endotoxin and sterility are separate. A lyophilized powder is not sterile by default, and the LAL result should be reported with its limit. For primary epithelial cells, endotoxin can dominate an inflammatory readout. The [endotoxin note](/blog/what-are-endotoxins-why-they-matter/) explains why.

Before filing the certificate, confirm that its lot number matches the vial and that the dates — synthesis, purification, fill, retest — are present. Each field is defined in the [CoA guide](/blog/how-to-read-a-peptide-coa-a-researchers-guide-to-hplc-and-ms-reports/).

## Sourcing & Verification

The first question is the sequence. Ask for the three residues in order and the observed mass. A supplier who answers with a product name has not identified the molecule. EDL and its isomers are cheap to make, and a mislabelled dipeptide would be hard to detect without a mass detector.

The second question is the relationship to bronchogen. A supplier who sells both should be able to explain that chonluten is the C-terminal tripeptide of the tetrapeptide, and should not present them as unrelated products. If the supplier cannot explain the relationship, the catalogue is probably assembled from other catalogues.

The third question is the salt form. Ask whether the product is the free acid, the sodium salt, or the acetate salt, and ask for the peptide content per vial. The tests behind that answer are set out in the [quality-testing overview](/peptide-quality-testing/).

The fourth question is the analytical method. A tripeptide without a chromophore is a poor candidate for UV-only purity. Ask whether the certificate includes a mass spectrum and whether the HPLC method was validated for the deletion sequence. A supplier who understands the molecule will have an answer.

The fifth question is provenance. Business licence, production address, and certificate issuer are the checks listed in the [manufacturer verification protocol](/blog/china-peptide-manufacturer-verification-a-2026-b2b-sourcing-protocol/). For a low-cost peptide, the temptation to relabel is high, and the document is where it shows.

Finally, ask for a pilot vial and run your own LC-MS. For a tripeptide, one injection with a mass detector answers the identity question and most of the purity question at the same time.

## Handling & Storage

Put the powder in a −20 °C freezer, desiccated and dark. Warm the vial to room temperature before opening to avoid condensation on the cake.

Reconstitute with water or a neutral buffer. EDL dissolves readily at neutral pH; at acidic pH the free acid is less soluble. Add the solvent gently and swirl. If the solution is cloudy, check the pH and the concentration before assuming contamination, and consult the [solubility note](/blog/peptide-solubility-troubleshooting/).

Use polypropylene tubes for dilute stocks. At neutral pH the peptide is anionic and sticks less than a cationic sequence would, though the loss at low micromolar concentrations is still measurable. A recovery check with a quantitative assay is cheaper than repeating an experiment.

Aliquot before freezing. A tripeptide without cysteine or methionine is chemically stable, but repeated freeze-thaw cycles still cause concentration changes and aggregation. A reconstituted stock is kept at 2–8 °C and used within the window the assay defines. Solvent and container choice are covered in the [reconstitution guide](/blog/peptide-lyophilization-reconstitution-guide-for-researchers/).

Do not autoclave the powder or the solution. Heat accelerates hydrolysis and can change the salt form. Where sterility is needed, pass the solution through a 0.22 µm membrane and measure recovery. The [handling hub](/peptide-handling-protocols/) covers general practice, and the [compliance overview](/peptide-compliance-risk/) covers the research-use terms that apply to the catalogue.

## How It Compares

**Bronchogen (Ala–Glu–Asp–Leu).** The tetrapeptide parent. It shares the C-terminal EDL sequence and adds an N-terminal alanine. The extra residue changes the mass, the charge, and possibly the uptake. Bronchogen is the better choice when the study is designed around the tetrapeptide literature; chonluten is the better choice when the question is whether the shorter fragment is sufficient.

**Cortagen (Ala–Glu–Asp–Gly).** A cortical tetrapeptide with a different fourth residue. It is not a pulmonary comparator, but it is a useful example of how the family varies the C-terminus to encode tissue association.

**Cartalax (Ala–Glu–Asp–Pro).** The cartilage tetrapeptide. Same backbone length as bronchogen, different tail. It is a comparator for the family's structure-activity logic, not for pulmonary endpoints.

**Epithalon (Ala–Glu–Asp–Gly).** The pineal tetrapeptide and the family's most studied member. It shares the first three residues with bronchogen and differs at the fourth, which makes it a useful reference point for anyone mapping the family's sequence space.

**Glutathione (γ-Glu-Cys-Gly).** A tripeptide with antioxidant chemistry, often mentioned alongside short peptides in ageing research. It is a different molecule with a different mechanism, and it is not a substitute for EDL in a transcriptional assay.
