## Why This Compound

The thymus is the organ where T cells learn to distinguish self from non-self, and it involutes with age: the cortical epithelium thins, the output of naive T cells falls, and the repertoire narrows. Most thymic research is about that process, and most candidate interventions are judged by whether they change thymic output, T-cell proliferation, or the balance of naive and memory subsets.

Crystagen enters that literature as a proposed transcriptional regulator rather than a growth factor or a cytokine. The family's working hypothesis is that short peptides matching tissue protein fragments enter cells and modulate expression in a tissue-specific way. For the thymus, the predicted readouts are genes involved in T-cell development, cytokine signalling, and epithelial cell function. The published work reports effects on T-lymphocyte proliferation, cytokine profiles, and thymus-dependent immune competence in aged or immunodeficient models.

Those are reasonable endpoints, and they are also the endpoints most vulnerable to endotoxin contamination. A tripeptide preparation that carries lipopolysaccharide will increase T-cell proliferation in a mixed lymphocyte reaction, raise IL-6 and TNF-α in a monocyte assay, and shift a cytokine panel toward inflammation. The effect is not subtle, and it is not distinguishable from a real immunomodulatory signal without a vehicle control and an endotoxin measurement.

That is why the first experiment with crystagen should be a control experiment. Run the peptide against a vehicle, against a scrambled tripeptide, and against a known endotoxin standard at the level measured in the batch. If the peptide's effect disappears when the endotoxin is subtracted, the result was the impurity. If it survives, there is something to follow. This is standard immunology practice, and it is the difference between a publishable result and a retraction.

The mechanism claim is separate. Direct DNA or chromatin interaction by short peptides is reported in biophysical systems and is not universally accepted as the explanation for the family's effects. A laboratory can test it with chromatin assays or reporter constructs, but the immune readouts alone cannot distinguish a transcriptional mechanism from a receptor-mediated one. The compound is a tool for asking the question, not an answer.

Chemistry is straightforward. EDP has two acidic residues and a proline at the C-terminus. The Asp-Pro bond is acid-labile, so acidic buffers and TFA-containing mobile phases can cleave the peptide. It has no aromatic residues, no cysteine, and no methionine, so oxidation is not a concern and detection at 214 nm is weak. Mass spectrometry is the better identity tool.

## What the Batch Record Should Show

Identity comes first: observed mass, calculated mass, and a tandem mass spectrum with the b- and y-ions assigned. For EDP, the proline at the C-terminus produces a strong y-ion series, and the two acidic residues produce b-ions. The Asp-Pro bond also produces a characteristic cleavage product at about 97 Da below the parent, which should be visible in the impurity profile if the batch has been exposed to acid.

Purity by HPLC should come with the method and the chromatogram. Because the peptide lacks a chromophore, the 214 nm signal is modest and the baseline matters. Ask for the blank, the integration threshold, and the column details. A short column with a steep gradient can hide the deletion sequences that a tripeptide synthesis produces.

Endotoxin is the field that deserves the most attention. Ask for the limulus amebocyte lysate result in endotoxin units per milligram or per vial, the method (gel-clot, turbidimetric, or chromogenic), and the limit. A result reported as "pass" without a number is not useful for an immune assay, because the assay's sensitivity determines whether the number matters. Methods and their limits are described in the [endotoxin testing note](/blog/endotoxin-testing-peptide-manufacturing/).

Sterility is separate. A lyophilized powder is not sterile by default, and a contaminated preparation will produce its own immune stimulation. Ask for the sterility method and result. For primary immune cells, both tests belong in the release specification, not in a supplementary document.

Counterion and peptide content follow. EDP is acidic overall and is usually supplied as the sodium or acetate salt. The certificate should state the form, the water content by Karl Fischer, and the peptide content per vial. On a 20 mg fill, a few percent water is a few hundred micrograms of peptide.

Residual TFA belongs on the record if the peptide was purified by reversed-phase HPLC. TFA can affect a cell assay by changing the pH and the ionic strength, and it can be a meaningful fraction of the dry mass in a tripeptide.

Tie the paperwork to the glass: the lot number on the certificate should match the vial, and the dates should cover synthesis, purification, fill, and retest. A field-by-field explanation is in the [CoA guide](/blog/how-to-read-a-peptide-coa-a-researchers-guide-to-hplc-and-ms-reports/).

## Sourcing & Verification

The first question is the sequence and the salt form. A supplier who answers "crystagen" has given a name. Ask for the three residues in order, the observed mass, and the counterion. A tripeptide is cheap to make, and a mislabelled dipeptide is hard to detect without mass detection.

The second question is the endotoxin number. This is the question that separates a supplier who understands immunology from one who does not. A supplier who can quote EU per vial, name the method, and explain the limit has thought about the end use. A supplier who says "sterile" has answered a different question.

The third question is the acid exposure. Ask how the peptide was purified and how the TFA was removed. The Asp-Pro bond is labile, and a batch that was lyophilised from a strongly acidic solution may already contain the truncated peptide. A stability-indicating HPLC method should resolve it.

The fourth question is provenance. The [manufacturer verification protocol](/blog/china-peptide-manufacturer-verification-a-2026-b2b-sourcing-protocol/) walks through the entity checks: business licence, production address, and certificate issuer. For a low-cost tripeptide, relabelling is common.

The fifth question is the immune assay the supplier claims to support. A catalogue that says "immunity" without naming an assay has not said anything testable. The [quality-testing overview](/peptide-quality-testing/) lists the release tests; the [categories overview](/peptide-categories-mechanisms/) explains the family structure.

Finally, ask for a pilot vial and run your own LC-MS plus an endotoxin test. For an immune readout, those two results decide whether the experiment is interpretable.

## Handling & Storage

Keep it at −20 °C, desiccated and out of the light. Warm the vial to room temperature before opening; a cold vial collects condensation and a hygroscopic tripeptide will absorb it.

Reconstitute with water or a neutral buffer. Avoid acidic buffers: the Asp-Pro bond hydrolyses at low pH, and the resulting truncation will grow with storage time. Add the solvent gently and swirl; do not heat. A cloudy solution points to pH or concentration before it points to contamination.

Use polypropylene tubes for dilute stocks. At neutral pH the peptide is anionic and adsorbs less than a cationic sequence, but the loss at low micromolar concentrations is measurable. A recovery check is cheaper than repeating an experiment.

Aliquot before freezing. A tripeptide without cysteine or methionine is chemically stable when dry, but repeated freeze-thaw cycles change the concentration and can cause aggregation. Reconstituted material stays at 2–8 °C and is used within the window set by the assay. Details on solvent and container choice sit in the [reconstitution guide](/blog/peptide-lyophilization-reconstitution-guide-for-researchers/).

Do not autoclave the powder or the solution. Heat accelerates hydrolysis and can change the salt form. Where a sterile preparation is needed, filter through a 0.22 µm membrane and check recovery. The [handling hub](/peptide-handling-protocols/) covers general practice.

The steps above are bench procedures for a research reagent. They are not instructions for preparing anything for human or animal use.

## How It Compares

**Thymalin.** A polypeptide fraction from thymus rather than a single tripeptide. Its certificate shows a molecular weight distribution, not a single mass, and its literature is older and broader. It is not a like-for-like comparator, and the two should not be substituted in an assay without revalidation. The [Thymalin page](/catalog/thymalin/) lists its formats.

**Cortagen (Ala–Glu–Asp–Gly).** A cortical tetrapeptide with a chromatin literature. It is a comparator for mechanism work, not for immune endpoints, and the sequence differs at the fourth residue.

**Chonluten (Glu–Asp–Leu).** A pulmonary tripeptide. It shares the first two residues with crystagen and differs at the third, which makes it a useful negative control when the question is whether the C-terminal residue matters.

**Bronchogen (Ala–Glu–Asp–Leu).** The pulmonary tetrapeptide. Same backbone length as cortagen, different tissue association. It is a comparator for the family's structure-activity logic.

**Thymosin alpha-1.** A 28-residue thymic peptide with a defined sequence and a substantial immunology literature. It is a different class of molecule and a different research tool. Crystagen is not a substitute, and a study designed around thymosin alpha-1 should not be switched to a tripeptide without revalidation.

## The Author's Take

**Position.** Crystagen is the clearest example in this catalogue of why the endotoxin number should be read before the purity number. In an immune assay, a clean peptide with a dirty LAL result is worse than a slightly impure peptide with a clean one.

**Reasoning.** Immunology assays are exquisitely sensitive to lipopolysaccharide. A picogram per millilitre can shift a cytokine readout, and a tripeptide batch at 20 mg per vial can carry enough endotoxin to matter at working concentrations.
