## Why This Compound

The bioregulator hypothesis proposes that short peptides can act as chromatin-level regulators. The mechanism is usually described as direct interaction with DNA or with histone-associated proteins, producing a change in transcription factor access rather than a change in a receptor's activity. Cortagen is the family member most often used to test that idea, because the cortex is a tissue where chromatin state changes with age are measurable and because the peptide is small enough to be added to a nuclear preparation without disturbing it.

The Tbilisi work used calorimetric methods to characterise chromatin from aged cells before and after peptide exposure. The readouts were the temperature and enthalpy of the histone-DNA transition: a more condensed, "closed" chromatin melts at a higher temperature, and a peptide that opens it lowers the transition. The papers report that cortagen shifts the profile toward a less condensed state in aged heterochromatin, with a smaller effect on chromatin from young cells. That is a biophysical result, not a clinical one, and it is the kind of evidence that can be reproduced with the right equipment.

The gene-expression literature is separate and less precise. Reviews from the originating group describe changes in cortical gene expression after peptide treatment, with pathways involving neuronal function, oxidative stress, and inflammation. The studies are small, the effect sizes are modest, and independent replication is limited. A laboratory should read the chromatin work and the expression work as two different evidence streams: the first is a direct physical measurement, the second is a transcriptional correlation.

Why use the peptide at all? Because the chromatin assay is cheap and fast relative to a genetic model, and because a four-residue peptide is easy to add and easy to remove. If the hypothesis is that a short sequence can change chromatin condensation, cortagen is a reasonable test article. If the hypothesis is that the peptide has a specific therapeutic effect, the literature does not support that claim and this page does not make it.

The chemistry is straightforward. AEDG has two acidic residues, a small alanine at the N-terminus, and a glycine at the C-terminus. It has no aromatic residues, no cysteine, and no methionine. It is water-soluble, stable when dry, and not prone to oxidation. The analytical challenge is detection at low wavelength and the possibility of sequence isomers, which is why mass spectrometry with fragmentation is the right identity test.

## What the Batch Record Should Show

Identity comes first. The certificate should give the observed mass, the calculated mass for the free peptide or the stated salt, and a tandem mass spectrum with the b- and y-ion series assigned. For AEDG, the glycine at the C-terminus produces a distinctive y-ion series, and the two acidic residues produce strong b-ions. A spectrum without assignments is a picture, not a confirmation.

Purity by HPLC should come with the method and the chromatogram. AEDG has no chromophore, so detection at 214 nm depends on the peptide bond and the signal is modest. Ask for the blank, the integration threshold, and the column details. A purity figure obtained on a short column with a steep gradient can hide deletion sequences and diastereomers.

Counterion and peptide content follow. AEDG is acidic overall and is usually supplied as the sodium or acetate salt. The certificate should state which form was filled, the water content by Karl Fischer, and the peptide content per vial. On a 20 mg fill, a few percent water is a few hundred micrograms of peptide, which matters for a concentration-response experiment.

Residual TFA belongs on the record if the peptide was purified by reversed-phase HPLC. For a tetrapeptide, TFA can be a meaningful fraction of the dry mass, and it can affect a chromatin assay by changing the ionic strength. Ion chromatography or fluorine NMR gives the number.

Endotoxin and sterility are separate. Lyophilisation does not imply sterility, and the endotoxin figure should carry units and a limit. For nuclear preparations and primary cells, endotoxin can produce a signal that looks like a biological effect. In that setting the limit matters more than the purity percentage; the [endotoxin note](/blog/what-are-endotoxins-why-they-matter/) explains why.

A certificate without a lot number that matches the vial is an anonymous document. The dates should follow the same logic: synthesis, purification, fill, retest. Each field's meaning is set out in the [CoA guide](/blog/how-to-read-a-peptide-coa-a-researchers-guide-to-hplc-and-ms-reports/).

## Sourcing & Verification

The first question is the sequence, and the second is whether the supplier understands that the sequence is shared with another catalogue name. A supplier who sells both cortagen and epithalon should be able to say whether the two products are the same molecule or different lots of the same sequence. If the answer is evasive, the catalogue is not internally consistent.

The second question is the salt form. AEDG is acidic and hygroscopic as the sodium salt. The certificate should state the form and the peptide content. A buyer comparing two quotes on fill mass is comparing the wrong number.

The third question is the analytical method. A tetrapeptide without a chromophore needs mass detection or a validated UV method. Check that the certificate carries a mass spectrum and that the HPLC method was validated against deletion sequences. The [quality-testing overview](/peptide-quality-testing/) covers the test panel in more detail.

The fourth question is provenance. The entity checks — business licence, production address, certificate issuer — sit in the [manufacturer verification protocol](/blog/china-peptide-manufacturer-verification-a-2026-b2b-sourcing-protocol/). For a low-cost tetrapeptide, relabelling is common.

The fifth question is the chromatin literature. A supplier who claims the product is "epigenetic" should be able to name the assays that support the claim. If the answer is a marketing phrase rather than a method, the claim is decoration. The [categories and mechanisms overview](/peptide-categories-mechanisms/) explains how the family is classified.

Finally, ask for a pilot vial and run your own LC-MS. For a tetrapeptide, one injection answers the identity question and most of the purity question.

## Handling & Storage

The powder stores at −20 °C in a desiccator, away from light. Warm the vial to room temperature before opening; the powder is hygroscopic and a cold vial will pull moisture from the air.

Reconstitute with water or a neutral buffer. AEDG dissolves readily at neutral pH; at low pH the free acid is less soluble. Add the solvent gently and swirl. A cloudy solution usually reflects pH or concentration rather than contamination; check both before consulting the [solubility note](/blog/peptide-solubility-troubleshooting/).

Use polypropylene tubes for dilute stocks. The peptide is anionic at neutral pH, so it adsorbs less than a cationic sequence, though the loss at low micromolar concentrations is still measurable. A recovery check with a quantitative assay is cheaper than repeating an experiment.

Aliquot before freezing. A tetrapeptide with no cysteine or methionine is chemically stable, yet repeated freeze-thaw still shifts the concentration and can drive aggregation. Once dissolved, keep the solution at 2–8 °C and use it within the assay window. The [reconstitution guide](/blog/peptide-lyophilization-reconstitution-guide-for-researchers/) covers solvent and container effects.

Do not autoclave the powder or the solution. Heat accelerates hydrolysis and can change the salt form. Where a sterile stock is needed, filter through a 0.22 µm membrane and check what is recovered. The [handling hub](/peptide-handling-protocols/) covers general practice.

The steps above are bench procedures for a research reagent. They are not instructions for preparing anything for human or animal use.

## The Author's Take

**Position.** Cortagen is interesting for one specific reason — it has a physical measurement behind it — and uninteresting for the reason most catalogue peptides are uninteresting: the biology has not been independently replicated. I would buy it to test the chromatin hypothesis, not to treat anything.

**Reasoning.** The calorimetry work is the kind of evidence I can respect: a defined preparation, a measurable transition, and a comparison between young and aged chromatin. It does not prove a therapeutic effect, and it does not need to. The problem is the gap between that measurement and the marketing language. "Epigenetic regulator" is a phrase that sounds like a mechanism and often is not. A supplier who can describe the assay, the peptide concentration, and the direction of the shift is selling a characterised reagent. A supplier who says "epigenetic" and nothing else is selling a word. The sequence overlap with epithalon is the other thing I would flag: it is not a scandal, but it means the buyer should check the certificate rather than the product name.
