Cerebrolysin is not a peptide in the sense the rest of this catalogue uses the word. It is a standardized mixture produced by enzymatic hydrolysis of porcine brain protein, containing short peptides, free amino acids, and smaller organic constituents. The catalogue lists it as a 60 mg lyophilized powder in packs of six vials, with the active fraction described as roughly 25% low-molecular-weight peptides below 10 kDa and 75% free amino acids. There is no sequence, no molecular formula, and no CAS number, because there is no single molecule to describe.

That makes the specification question different from every other page on this site. You cannot confirm identity with a mass spectrum, and you cannot express purity as a single percentage. What you can do is define the mixture: peptide content, molecular weight distribution, amino acid composition, endotoxin load, sterility, and the consistency of these values across lots. A certificate that reports "98%" for cerebrolysin has applied a peptide-page template to a product that does not fit it.

The research interest is neurotrophic. The mixture is studied in neuron cultures, in ischaemia and excitotoxicity models, and in rodent models of neurodegeneration, where the readouts are cell viability, trophic factor expression, synaptic markers, and behavioural recovery. The clinical literature exists and is large, but it sits outside the scope of a research catalogue. This page deals with the material, its documentation, and its handling.

## Why This Compound

The rationale for a hydrolysate is that neurotrophic support is combinatorial. Single neurotrophins — BDNF, NGF, NT-3 — act on specific Trk receptors and have narrow working ranges, poor stability, and limited access to the central nervous system. A mixture of short peptides and amino acids proposes a different approach: supply a set of fragments that may act on several pathways at once, with the mixture's complexity providing some buffering against the fragility of a single agent.

Whether that works is a research question, not a catalogue claim. The published preclinical work reports effects on neuronal viability under glutamate excitotoxicity and oxygen-glucose deprivation, on mitochondrial function, and on caspase activation. Proposed mechanisms include induction of endogenous BDNF and NGF expression and activation of TrkB and TrkA survival signalling. Other studies examine amyloid precursor protein processing, tau phosphorylation, and synaptic markers in transgenic rodent models. The readouts are plausible and the assays are standard; the interpretation is limited by the mixture's complexity, because an effect cannot be assigned to a specific component.

That is the central methodological problem, and it is worth stating plainly. A mixture with dozens of components has dozens of possible active species, and the batch-to-batch composition depends on the hydrolysis conditions, the starting tissue, and the purification steps. Two lots that meet the same peptide-content specification can differ in their low-abundance components. A laboratory that runs a concentration-response experiment with one lot and a confirmatory experiment with another is not running the same experiment.

The counter-argument is that standardisation is the point. A manufacturer that controls the hydrolysis time, temperature, enzyme-to-substrate ratio, and ultrafiltration cut-off can produce a mixture with a consistent molecular weight profile, and that profile is a meaningful specification even without a sequence. The analytical task is to verify the profile, not to pretend it is a single molecule.

For a laboratory, cerebrolysin is useful in three situations: as a complex stimulus in neuron culture where the question is whether a mixture can support survival; as a comparator against a defined neurotrophic factor; and as a test article for method development in mixture analysis. It is a poor choice when the question requires a defined mechanism, because the material cannot supply one.

## What the Batch Record Should Show

Peptide content is the primary specification. The certificate should state the total peptide content as a percentage of fill mass, the method used, and the nitrogen-to-protein conversion factor if one was applied. A value without a method is not a specification. For a hydrolysate, the Lowry, bicinchoninic acid, and Kjeldahl methods give different answers, and the certificate should say which one was used.

Molecular weight distribution is the second field. Size-exclusion chromatography with a calibrated column gives a profile: the proportion of material above 10 kDa, between 3 and 10 kDa, and below 3 kDa. The catalogue's claim that about 25% is below 10 kDa should be checkable against the chromatogram. A batch that shifts toward higher molecular weight suggests incomplete hydrolysis; a batch that shifts lower suggests over-digestion.

Amino acid composition is the third field. Complete hydrolysis followed by amino acid analysis gives the molar ratios of the constituent residues. For a standardized hydrolysate, the profile should be consistent across lots, and a deviation is a signal that the starting tissue or the hydrolysis changed. The free amino acid fraction can be measured separately, without hydrolysis, and compared with the total.

Identity of the source material matters. The product is derived from porcine brain, and the certificate should state the species, the tissue, and the country of origin, along with the documentation for transmissible spongiform encephalopathy risk. Porcine tissue carries a lower theoretical risk than bovine, but the documentation is still part of the specification. A supplier who cannot name the tissue origin has not qualified the raw material.

Residual moisture, fill mass, and reconstitution behaviour belong on the record. A 60 mg fill of a hygroscopic powder can vary by several percent, and the peptide content per vial is what the experiment uses. Karl Fischer water content and a fill check are cheap tests that catch a surprising number of problems.

Endotoxin and sterility are separate release tests. The product is derived from tissue, so endotoxin removal is part of the process, and the LAL result should be reported with units and limit. Sterility is a separate claim; a lyophilized powder is not sterile by default. The [endotoxin testing note](/blog/endotoxin-testing-peptide-manufacturing/) explains why tissue-derived materials need a tighter limit than synthetic peptides.

Batch linkage closes the record. The vial number and the certificate number must match, and the document should carry the hydrolysis date, the ultrafiltration cut-off, the fill date, and the retest date. For a mixture, the lot is the product in a way that is not true for a synthetic peptide. The [CoA guide](/blog/how-to-read-a-peptide-coa-a-researchers-guide-to-hplc-and-ms-reports/) defines the fields, though a mixture certificate is shaped differently from a peptide certificate.

## Sourcing & Verification

The first question is what the product is derived from. Ask for the species, the tissue, and the country of origin. A porcine brain hydrolysate and a synthetic mixture of amino acids are different products, and the price difference is large. A supplier who describes the product as "peptide mixture" without naming the source has not answered the question.

The second question is the molecular weight profile. Ask for the size-exclusion chromatogram and the fraction boundaries. A supplier who can produce the profile has a controlled process; a supplier who cannot is repackaging someone else's powder.

The third question is the peptide content method. Ask which assay was used and what the result was as a percentage of fill mass. A certificate that reports only the fill mass has not described the active content.

The fourth question is the raw-material documentation. For a tissue-derived product, the TSE/BSE statement, the viral inactivation steps, and the country of origin are part of the specification. Those documents should be backed by the entity checks in the [manufacturer verification protocol](/blog/china-peptide-manufacturer-verification-a-2026-b2b-sourcing-protocol/).

The fifth question is consistency across lots. Ask for the last three certificates and compare the peptide content, the molecular weight profile, and the amino acid composition. A product that drifts between lots is not standardized, whatever the label says. The [purity note](/blog/why-purity-and-concentration-tests-are-not-enough/) explains why a single percentage cannot capture this.

Finally, ask for a pilot vial and run a size-exclusion profile in-house. For a mixture, that one chromatogram tells you more about the product than a page of claims.

## Handling & Storage

Keep the vial at −20 °C, dry, and protected from light. Warm it to room temperature before opening; the powder is hygroscopic and a cold vial will pull moisture from the air.

Reconstitute with water or a buffer that matches the assay. The catalogue gives a 24-hour window for aqueous solutions at 2–8 °C, which is short compared with a synthetic peptide. The reason is the free amino acid fraction and the short peptides: they are excellent substrates for microbial growth and for endogenous peptidases that survive the hydrolysis. Prepare only what the experiment needs.

Do not vortex or heat. A hydrolysate foams readily, and foaming denatures the larger peptides and creates an aerosol; the [lyophilisation note](/blog/lyophilization-cycle-peptides/) explains why the cake's structure is worth preserving. Add the solvent gently, swirl, and let the powder dissolve. If the solution is cloudy, centrifuge it at low speed and check the pellet before assuming contamination.

Filter through a 0.22 µm membrane for cell work, and confirm recovery. A mixture contains species across a wide size range, and a filter can remove the larger peptides preferentially, changing the composition of the filtrate. That is a real risk for a product whose activity may depend on the high-molecular-weight fraction.

Aliquot before freezing. Repeated freeze-thaw cycles change the composition of a mixture more than they change a single peptide, because different components aggregate at different rates. Solvent and container effects are covered in the [reconstitution guide](/blog/peptide-lyophilization-reconstitution-guide-for-researchers/); general bench practice sits in the [handling hub](/peptide-handling-protocols/).

The steps above are bench procedures for a research reagent. They are not instructions for preparing anything for human or animal use.

## How It Compares

**Recombinant BDNF.** A single, structurally defined protein that acts on TrkB. It is the right tool for a mechanistic study of TrkB signalling, and it has a narrow working range and poor stability in solution. Cerebrolysin is not a substitute; it is a mixture with a different question behind it.

**Recombinant NGF.** A single protein acting on TrkA, used in sensory neuron and sympathetic neuron work. Like BDNF, it is defined and expensive. A mixture cannot replace it in a receptor-specific assay.

**Semax.** A synthetic heptapeptide with a defined sequence and a neurotrophic transcriptional literature. It is the opposite design choice: one molecule, one mass, one mechanism hypothesis. The [Semax page](/catalog/semax/) lists the formats. A laboratory choosing between them is choosing between a defined tool and a complex stimulus.

**Cerebrolysin.** The mixture on this page. Its advantage is breadth and stability when dry; its limitation is that no single component can be assigned an effect. It is the right choice when the hypothesis is about a mixture or when the assay needs a complex trophic stimulus, and the wrong choice when the hypothesis is about a specific receptor.

**Other brain hydrolysates.** Several suppliers sell "brain peptide" preparations with different hydrolysis methods and different molecular weight profiles. They are not interchangeable, and a certificate from one says nothing about another. Compare the profile, not the name.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What exactly is cerebrolysin?**

It is a standardized mixture produced by enzymatic hydrolysis of porcine brain protein, containing short peptides, free amino acids, and smaller organic constituents. The catalogue describes the active fraction as roughly 25% peptides below 10 kDa and 75% free amino acids.

**Why is there no molecular weight or sequence?**

Because the product is a mixture, not a single molecule. A molecular weight or sequence would describe one component and misrepresent the whole. The meaningful specifications are peptide content, molecular weight distribution, and amino acid composition.

**What does "standardized" mean for a mixture?**

It means the manufacturing process is controlled so that the peptide content and molecular weight profile fall within defined limits from lot to lot. It does not mean every component is identified, and it does not guarantee that two lots are identical in every minor constituent.

**How should it be stored and reconstituted?**

Store the powder at −20 °C, desiccated and protected from light. Reconstitute with water or a compatible buffer, and use the solution within 24 hours at 2–8 °C. Do not vortex or heat.

**Is the product sterile?**

Not by default. A lyophilized powder is sterile only if the process and container closure support a sterility claim. Ask for the sterility method and the endotoxin result, especially for primary neuron work.

**Can it be used in cell culture?**

Yes, if the preparation is sterile and endotoxin-tested. The mixture contains free amino acids and can change the medium's composition, so a matched vehicle control is needed before a viability or trophic readout can be interpreted.

**How is peptide content measured?**

Different methods give different answers. The certificate should state whether the value came from a colorimetric protein assay, nitrogen determination, or amino acid analysis, and should report the result as a percentage of fill mass.

**Is there clinical evidence for cerebrolysin?**
