## Why This Compound

NAD+ is the oxidised form of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide. It carries hydride equivalents in glycolysis, the tricarboxylic acid cycle, and mitochondrial electron transport, and it is consumed as a substrate by sirtuins, PARP enzymes, and CD38. Ying's review remains a useful map of the redox and signalling roles, including the distinction between the NAD+/NADH couple and the phosphorylated NADP+/NADPH couple, which are not interchangeable in an assay (Ying, 2008). A laboratory that adds NAD+ to a system is usually trying to change the availability of that cofactor, not to introduce a drug.

The literature that surrounds NAD+ is often read as a story about decline. Camacho-Pereira and colleagues reported that CD38 activity increases with age in mouse tissues and that its consumption of NAD+ contributes to the age-related fall in tissue NAD+ (Camacho-Pereira et al., 2016). Davila and colleagues reported that mammalian mitochondria can transport NAD+ directly, which complicates the assumption that cells rely only on precursors such as NMN and NR (Davila et al., 2018). Both findings are mechanistic and both are model-dependent. Neither tells you what an oil formulation does in a dish, because the formulation question comes first: how much NAD+ is in the oil, in what physical state, and how it partitions when it meets an aqueous medium.

That last question is the reason an oil format exists at all in laboratory work. A non-aqueous vehicle changes the partition of a hydrophilic compound between phases, slows its release into water, and can be used to build sustained-exposure or two-phase models. It is a formulation research tool, not a delivery claim. If a study is designed around an aqueous buffer, an oil preparation is the wrong input; if the study is about partitioning, emulsion stability, or release kinetics, the oil is the variable of interest.

There is also a simple physical constraint to keep in view. NAD+ is a dinucleotide with two phosphate groups and a ribose-phosphate backbone. It is strongly polar and hydrogen-bonding. Triglyceride oils are non-polar and cannot solvate it to any meaningful extent. A 100 mg/ml preparation therefore cannot be a true molecular solution in oil. It must contain a dispersed aqueous or polar phase, a surfactant, or solid particles small enough to stay suspended. A supplier who describes the product as "NAD+ dissolved in oil" is either simplifying or has not asked the formulator. The [categories hub](/peptide-categories-mechanisms/) places NAD+ among the non-peptide cofactors that are sold alongside peptides.

The price is worth noting without over-reading it. At $23 for a 10 ml vial, the product is inexpensive relative to lyophilised NAD+ sold by weight. That is consistent with a dilute formulation in a commodity carrier, and it is a reason to ask for the carrier specification rather than assume a premium vehicle. A batch record that names the oil, the water content, and the assay method costs the manufacturer little to produce and tells the buyer a great deal.

## What the Batch Record Should Show

Start with the concentration unit. A label that says 100 mg/ml is not the same as 100 mg/g, and oils are usually formulated by weight. At a typical triglyceride density near 0.9 g/ml, 100 mg/ml corresponds to about 111 mg/g. If the batch record states one unit and the fill is checked in the other, the discrepancy is around 10%. Ask which unit was used for the assay and whether the density of the finished preparation was measured.

Next, ask what the NAD+ is dispersed in. The carrier should be named: a medium-chain triglyceride, a sesame or safflower oil, a benzyl benzoate blend, or something else. Each has a different peroxide profile, a different viscosity, and a different effect on the assay. A certificate that says "oil" is not a specification. If the preparation is an emulsion, ask for the surfactant identity and the aqueous-phase volume fraction. If it is a suspension, ask for the particle-size distribution and the sedimentation behaviour.

Third, ask for the content assay method. This is the field most oil products omit. NAD+ absorbs strongly near 259 nm, but the oil matrix does not, so the usual approach is an extraction: dilute the preparation with a water-miscible solvent, partition the NAD+ into an aqueous phase, separate the phases, and quantify by ion-pairing HPLC or by ultraviolet absorbance against a standard. The certificate should state the extraction recovery, the calibration range, and the measured concentration in mg/ml. An assay that reports only "100 mg/ml by formulation" is a calculation from the weigh-in, not a measurement of the finished product. The [purity-versus-concentration note](/blog/why-purity-and-concentration-tests-are-not-enough/) explains why the distinction matters in a non-aqueous matrix.

Identity confirmation should follow the same route. A mass spectrum of the aqueous extract, showing the protonated ion near m/z 664.4 and the negative-mode ion near m/z 662.4, is stronger evidence than a ultraviolet reading alone. The ultraviolet spectrum should show the adenine maximum near 259 nm and the expected absorbance ratio. If the preparation contains other ultraviolet-absorbing excipients, the spectrum may be distorted, and the certificate should say how the background was subtracted.

Carrier quality is the fourth field. Oil oxidises. Peroxide value and acid value are the standard measures, and both increase with storage time and light exposure. A rancid carrier is not inert: peroxides can oxidise the NAD+ and can also interfere with cell-based assays. Ask for peroxide value at release, and ask whether the oil was blanketed with nitrogen during filling. Water content by Karl Fischer matters as well, because a small aqueous phase is where microbial growth and hydrolysis happen.

Sterility and endotoxin are separate questions. An anhydrous oil does not support microbial growth well, but an emulsion with a dispersed aqueous phase can. If the preparation will contact cells, request sterility testing and LAL endotoxin data. A preservative, if present, should be named; some preservatives interfere with viability assays at concentrations that are not obvious from the label.

Fill volume closes the record. A 10 ml vial is rarely filled to exactly 10.0 ml. Headspace, overfill, and vial geometry all affect the delivered volume, and a preparation that is assayed at 100 mg/ml in the bulk tank can differ from the vial after settling. Ask for the fill weight and the density, not just the nominal volume.

## Sourcing & Verification

Oil formulations are easy to improvise and hard to document, which is why the questions below matter more than the price.

- Is the carrier named, and is there a specification for peroxide value and acid value?
- Is the preparation a solution, an emulsion, or a suspension?
- If an emulsion, what is the surfactant and the aqueous-phase fraction?
- If a suspension, what is the particle-size distribution?
- Is the concentration assayed after extraction, and what is the recovery?
- Is the unit mg/ml or mg/g, and was the density measured?
- Is identity confirmed by mass spectrometry of the extract?
- What is the water content by Karl Fischer?
- Is the product sterile, and is endotoxin data available?
- Is the fill volume verified per vial, and is the batch number on the vial?

A supplier who can answer the first three questions is describing a real formulation. A supplier who answers "NAD+ in oil, 100 mg/ml" is describing a label.

The extraction assay is the single most useful document. It tells you whether the manufacturer measured the finished preparation or calculated it from the input weights. It also tells you whether the method can distinguish NAD+ from its hydrolysis products, nicotinamide and ADP-ribose. In an aqueous system those species appear over time; in an oil system with trace water, the same chemistry can occur at the interface. A chromatogram that resolves them is worth more than a certificate that reports 99% purity of an unstated method. The [CoA reading guide](/blog/how-to-read-a-peptide-coa-a-researchers-guide-to-hplc-and-ms-reports/) explains which fields carry weight.

Ask about the carrier's own certificate. A food-grade medium-chain triglyceride and a technical-grade oil can carry the same name and very different impurities. Peroxide value below 10 meq/kg is a common specification for cosmetic-grade oils, but the number that matters depends on the assay. For cell work, a low-peroxide, low-water carrier is the safer starting point.

Finally, check the format against the study. If the experiment is an aqueous enzyme assay, a lyophilised [NAD+](/catalog/nad/) powder is the appropriate input, and the oil is a complication. If the experiment is a partition or release study, the oil is the point, and the carrier specification becomes the main variable. The [NAD+ blend page](/catalog/nad-100mg-mots-c-10mg-5-amino-1mq-10mg/) covers the powder format, and the [quality-testing overview](/peptide-quality-testing/) explains why identity, purity, and content are separate measurements.

## Handling & Storage

Store the vial upright at 2-8°C, protected from light. Do not freeze an oil preparation: freezing can crack an emulsion, irreversibly separate phases, and concentrate water into ice crystals that damage the dispersed structure. Let the vial equilibrate to room temperature before opening, and wipe the neck before drawing a sample.

Mix by gentle inversion, not by vortexing. Vortexing an emulsion introduces air, creates foam, and can cause coalescence. If the preparation is a suspension, roll the vial slowly for a minute and inspect for sediment before sampling. A suspension that does not resuspend evenly after gentle mixing is not suitable for a quantitative assay.

Sample with a dry glass syringe or a positive-displacement pipette. Water carried on a needle or a tip can create a local aqueous phase and destabilise the preparation at the puncture site. Use the sample immediately; do not return unused material to the vial. For a multi-use vial, aliquot into smaller amber vials under nitrogen if the workflow allows, and accept that each transfer adds air and moisture.

Light and oxygen are the two environmental variables that matter most. NAD+ is sensitive to prolonged light exposure, and the carrier oil is sensitive to oxidation. Amber glass, a nitrogen headspace, and a cool storage temperature address both. If the preparation changes colour, develops a rancid odour, or shows a visible separation, discard it. Those are gross indicators, not a quality assay, but they are reliable enough to stop a run.

For cell work, do not assume that an oil preparation can be added directly to an aqueous culture. It will float, coat the surface, and change the gas exchange of the culture. If the study requires a dispersed oil phase, that is a design decision with its own controls. If it does not, use an aqueous stock instead. The [handling hub](/peptide-handling-protocols/) covers reconstitution and container effects for powder formats, and the [stability note](/blog/peptide-stability-why-lyophilized-peptides-are-far-more-stable-than-you-think/) explains why lyophilised solids behave differently from solutions.

One more practical point: record the lot and the opening date on the vial. An oil preparation has a shorter working window than a lyophilised powder, and the peroxide value of the carrier rises after the vial is opened. A laboratory that tracks the opening date can distinguish a formulation problem from a storage problem when an assay drifts.

## How It Compares

**Lyophilised NAD+.** The standard laboratory input. It is a solid, it is assayed by weight and chromatography, and it reconstitutes in water or buffer. For any aqueous assay, it is the simpler and better-documented choice. The [NAD+ page](/catalog/nad/) lists the pack sizes.

**The NAD+ blend.** A three-component lyophilised product with MOTS-c and 5-Amino-1MQ. It is a screening format, not a substitute for the oil. Its analytical questions are about three components and a mixture assay; the oil's questions are about a carrier and a dispersed phase.

**NAD+ in aqueous buffer.** A laboratory-made stock. It is simple, but it has a short working life because NAD+ hydrolyses in water. An oil preparation trades that hydrolysis risk for a carrier-oxidation risk and a phase-stability risk. Neither is automatically better; they fail in different ways.

**NAD+ precursors.** NMN and NR are different molecules with their own transport and salvage pathways. A study that uses NAD+ itself is asking a different question from a study that uses a precursor. The oil format does not change that distinction, and no formulation claim should blur it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is NAD+ soluble in oil?**

Not to any meaningful extent. NAD+ is a polar dinucleotide with two phosphate groups, and triglyceride oils cannot solvate it as individual molecules. A 100 mg/ml preparation must be an emulsion, a fine suspension, or a formulation with a polar co-solvent and a surfactant.

**What does 100 mg/ml mean for an oil?**

It means 100 mg of NAD+ per millilitre of finished preparation. Because oils are often formulated by weight, confirm whether the assay used mg/ml or mg/g. At a density near 0.9 g/ml, the two differ by about 10%, which matters for any concentration series.

**How is the concentration verified?**

By extraction followed by chromatography or ultraviolet assay. The NAD+ is partitioned into an aqueous phase, separated from the oil, and quantified against a standard. A certificate that reports only the weigh-in calculation has not measured the finished product.

**Can the preparation be sterile-filtered?**

Not through an aqueous filter in the usual way. Sterile filtration of an oil requires a compatible membrane and a validated process, and an emulsion may be damaged by filtration. Ask how sterility was achieved, and request endotoxin data separately if the preparation will contact cells.

**Why is the price lower than lyophilised NAD+?**

The carrier is inexpensive and the NAD+ load per vial is 1 g at the declared concentration, so the comparison depends on the assay and the fill. Price is not evidence of quality in either direction; the carrier specification and the extraction assay are.

**Does the oil format change what NAD+ does in a cell assay?**

It changes the physical presentation, not the molecule. If oil is added to an aqueous culture, it forms a separate phase and can alter gas exchange and mixing. A study that wants a defined aqueous exposure should use a reconstituted powder instead.

**How should the vial be stored?**

Upright at 2-8°C, protected from light, and not frozen. Freezing can break an emulsion or separate phases. Equilibrate to room temperature before sampling, mix by gentle inversion, and record the opening date.

**What carrier is used?**

The carrier should be named on the certificate. Medium-chain triglycerides, sesame oil, and benzyl benzoate blends are common, and each has a different peroxide profile and viscosity. A label that says only "oil" is not a specification.

## The Author's Take

**Position.** I would not buy an oil-based NAD+ preparation without a named carrier and an extraction assay. The format is plausible for formulation research, but it raises the documentation bar rather than lowering it.

**Reasoning.** NAD+ cannot dissolve in triglyceride oil at 100 mg/ml. That is not an opinion; it follows from the molecule's polarity. So the product is necessarily a dispersed system, and a dispersed system has properties that a solution does not: phase stability, particle or droplet size, water content, and a carrier that oxidises over time. None of those can be inferred from a label that states a concentration. The extraction assay is the minimum evidence that the manufacturer measured the finished preparation rather than calculating it from the weigh-in. The carrier specification is the minimum evidence that the oil was chosen rather than bought. For a partition or release study, those documents are the experiment's foundation. For an aqueous enzyme assay, the product is the wrong format and no certificate will fix that.
