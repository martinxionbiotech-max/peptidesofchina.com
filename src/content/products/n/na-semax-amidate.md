## Why This Compound

The Semax scaffold has been studied for decades, mostly in Russian and Eastern European laboratories, and the literature is unusual: a small number of groups, a lot of animal work, and relatively little independent replication outside the originating network. The peptide is interesting to peptide chemists because it is short, stable and easy to modify; it is interesting to cell biologists because it has been reported to change neurotrophin expression in glial cultures; and it is interesting to sourcing people because the name is used loosely across a family of related sequences.

The amidated, acetylated form is a distinct member of that family. The N-acetyl group removes the positive charge on the N-terminal methionine and blocks aminopeptidase cleavage; the C-amide removes the negative charge on the C-terminal proline and blocks carboxypeptidase cleavage. The result is a peptide with a different charge profile and a longer expected lifetime in biological media than the parent. Whether those differences produce a different biological effect is a research question, not a catalogue claim.

For a laboratory, the practical value of NA Semax amidate is as a stability reference. If you are comparing the parent peptide with a modified analogue, the modified form gives you a control for proteolytic loss. If you are developing an LC-MS method for a family of Semax-like peptides, the mass difference between the parent, the acetylated form and the amidated form is a useful test of specificity. Those are modest, defensible uses. The page does not claim anything beyond them.

There is also a documentation benefit. Because the modifications are defined, the molecule is a good test of whether a supplier's certificate is actually describing the product. A batch that claims “NA Semax amidate” but reports the mass of the parent peptide is not a minor labelling error; it is a different compound. Laboratories that keep a reference standard of each form can screen incoming lots against it and catch that error before it reaches an experiment.

## What the Batch Record Should Show

Identity is the first and most important field. The certificate should state the calculated mass for the acetylated, amidated sequence, the observed mass, and the adduct pattern. The mass difference from the parent peptide should be explained: +42.01 Da for acetylation, −0.98 Da for amidation, and the combination should match the observed shift. A supplier who reports a mass without stating which form it corresponds to has not defined the product.

Sequence coverage should include the N-terminus and the C-terminus, because that is where the modifications sit. MS/MS fragmentation of a seven-residue peptide is usually straightforward; the b-ion series should show the acetylated N-terminus and the y-ion series should show the amidated C-terminus. If the coverage stops before the termini, the modifications are unproven.

Purity should be measured by reversed-phase HPLC at 214 or 220 nm. The peptide has one histidine and one phenylalanine, so 214 nm is the practical wavelength; 280 nm is too weak for reliable quantification. The method should resolve the parent peptide, the mono-modified forms and the di-modified product. A single peak at the expected retention time is not enough if the parent peptide co-elutes.

Counterion and water content are next. The peptide is usually supplied as an acetate or trifluoroacetate salt. The counterion affects solubility and the pH of the reconstituted solution. Karl Fischer water content should be reported. For cell work, endotoxin and bioburden should be reported as well.

Finally, the certificate should state the purification method and the residual solvents. The acetyl and amide groups are introduced during synthesis or post-synthetic modification, and the reagents used in those steps can leave traces. A certificate that reports only “purity ≥98%” is a summary, not a batch record. ## Sourcing & Verification

The Semax family is a good example of a category where the label and the molecule can drift apart. Suppliers list Semax, N-acetyl Semax, Semax amidate and NA Semax amidate as if they were interchangeable. They are not. The mass, the charge and the stability profile differ, and a study that uses one form while citing literature on another is not reproducible.

Start by asking for the exact structure and the mass. The supplier should be able to draw the molecule or provide a sequence with the terminal modifications marked. If the answer is “Semax with modifications” without specifying which, the product is not defined. Then ask for the MS/MS coverage of the termini. Then ask for the HPLC method and the retention time of the parent peptide as a system-suitability check.

The second check is the counterion. The acetate salt is more common for cell work; the trifluoroacetate salt is more common for analytical work. If the certificate does not state the salt, ask. A TFA salt can be toxic to cells at concentrations that are irrelevant for an HPLC assay, and an acetate salt can change the pH of an unbuffered solution.

The third check is the supplier's familiarity with the family. A supplier who can explain the difference between Semax and NA Semax amidate, and who can provide a mass for each, is more credible than one who lists all four products with the same description. The [peptide modification guide](/blog/how-peptide-modifications-affect-activity/) explains the chemistry; the [COA guide](/blog/how-to-read-a-peptide-coa-a-researchers-guide-to-hplc-and-ms-reports/) explains the documentation.

Finally, check the physical form and the storage history. The powder should be white to off-white and dissolve clear in water. A yellow or sticky powder suggests oxidation or moisture. If the shipment arrives warm, inspect the cake before use. The [quality-testing hub](/peptide-quality-testing/) has the broader context.

## Handling & Storage

Store the lyophilized powder at −20 °C, protected from light and moisture. Let the vial equilibrate before opening. Weigh or transfer in a dry environment; the powder is hygroscopic.

For reconstitution, use sterile water or a neutral buffer. The peptide is hydrophilic and usually dissolves readily. Add the solvent slowly and swirl; avoid vortexing. If the solution is hazy, centrifuge at low speed and inspect the pellet. Do not filter through a membrane that binds peptides unless you have validated recovery.

Dilute into the assay buffer in steps. Keep the pH and ionic strength constant across conditions. For cell-based work, use low-binding tubes and prepare fresh dilutions from a single stock. Run a vehicle control at the same counterion concentration. If the assay is sensitive to endotoxin, use a lot with a documented LAL result.

Aliquot and freeze at −20 °C or −80 °C. Avoid repeated freeze-thaw. A working solution at 2-8 °C should be used within a few days. Record the lot, the reconstitution date and the solvent. ## How It Compares

The parent compound, [Semax](/catalog/semax/), lacks the terminal modifications and has a different charge profile. It is the right comparator if the experiment is about the effect of the modifications, but it is not a substitute for the modified peptide. N-acetyl Semax without the C-amide is another intermediate; it blocks aminopeptidase attack but leaves the C-terminus charged. Semax amidate without the N-acetyl is the mirror image. The four forms make a useful panel for a stability study.

Against [NA Selank Amidate](/catalog/na-selank-amidate/), the comparison is sequence and receptor pharmacology; both are short modified peptides, but they are different molecules. Against [Selank + Semax](/catalog/selank-10mg-semax-10mg/), the blend is a mixture, not a single reference. Against [NA Epithalon Amidate](/catalog/na-epithalon-amidate/), the comparison is the effect of terminal modifications on a different peptide scaffold; the analytical logic is similar, the biology is not.

If your question is methodological, compare the parent and the modified forms by LC-MS. If your question is biological, use the form that matches the literature you are trying to replicate, and state the form explicitly in the methods.

**What is the difference between Semax and NA Semax amidate?**

Semax is the parent heptapeptide. NA Semax amidate has an acetyl group on the N-terminus and an amide on the C-terminus. The modifications change the mass, the charge and the stability profile. They are different products and should be labelled as such.

Use intact mass plus MS/MS coverage of both termini. The acetyl group adds about 42 Da; the amide subtracts about 1 Da. A mass match without terminal fragmentation does not prove the structure.
