## Why This Compound

Selank was designed at the Institute of Molecular Genetics in Moscow as a stabilised version of tuftsin, the tetrapeptide Thr-Lys-Pro-Arg released from IgG. The stabilising element was a Pro-Gly-Pro tail added to the C-terminus, intended to slow cleavage by circulating peptidases. The resulting heptapeptide kept the basic character of tuftsin, added a proline-rich tail, and acquired a C-terminal amide during synthesis. That amide is part of the parent molecule, not an upgrade.

The research literature on Selank has three recurring themes. The first is peptidase inhibition. Kost and colleagues reported that Selank and Semax inhibited enkephalin-degrading enzymes in human serum, a cell-free effect that has been proposed as one route to the peptide's behavioural readouts (Kost et al., 2001). The second theme is GABAergic gene expression. Filatova and colleagues exposed IMR-32 cells to Selank and measured transcript changes across a panel of GABA-transmission genes, comparing the response with olanzapine (Filatova et al., 2017). Volkova and colleagues reported changes in GABAergic gene expression in rat brain after exposure (Volkova et al., 2016). The third theme is behavioural pharmacology in rodents, where the readouts are locomotor and exploratory measures rather than clinical endpoints.

Those are three different experimental systems, and none of them establishes a receptor-level mechanism. That matters for the analogue because there is no published pharmacology for the acetylated form at all. The literature describes Selank. A catalogue page that cites Selank papers next to an acetylated product is citing the parent, and the reader should know which molecule was in the vial.

The chemical rationale for acetylation is straightforward. The N-terminus of Selank is a free α-amino group on threonine, and aminopeptidases attack exactly that position. Acetylation blocks the attack, and the acetyl group is small enough not to disturb the rest of the sequence. The C-terminal amide, by contrast, is redundant: Selank already ends in an amide, and adding a second one is not chemically possible. A listing that describes "dual modification" as an improvement is describing one real change and one inherited feature.

The charge consequence is the part most often missed. At neutral pH, Selank carries a positive charge on the N-terminus, one on the lysine side chain, and one on the arginine side chain, for a net of about +3. Acetylation removes the N-terminal charge, leaving +2. That is a meaningful shift for a small peptide: it weakens electrostatic binding to cation exchangers, reduces adsorption to negatively charged glass surfaces, and changes the molecule's partitioning in two-phase systems. It may also change how the peptide interacts with a cell surface, which is a reason to treat the analogue as a different reagent rather than a drop-in replacement.

## What the Batch Record Should Show

Identity starts with mass. Selank averages 751.86 Da; the acetylated analogue is about 42 Da heavier, near 793 Da. The exact value depends on the C-terminal form, which should be the amide. A certificate that reports 751.86 Da is describing the parent. A certificate that reports "793" without a spectrum is quoting a catalogue value. Ask for the deconvoluted mass and for fragment evidence that the acetyl group is on the N-terminus rather than on the lysine side chain.

That positional question is the main identity risk. Acetylation reagents do not always distinguish the N-terminal amine from the ε-amine of lysine, and a batch can contain N-acetyl, Lys-acetyl, and di-acetyl species with masses that differ by 42 Da steps. A low-resolution mass readout may show a single average; a chromatogram may show one broad peak. Ask for the acetyl positional distribution, or at least for a chromatogram with the satellite peaks integrated. A batch that is 85% N-acetyl and 15% Lys-acetyl is not the same reagent as a batch that is 98% N-acetyl.

The catalogue purity floor is ≥98% by area. Purity by reversed-phase HPLC should be run at 214 nm, because Selank has no tryptophan or tyrosine and gives little signal at 280 nm. The method should state the column, mobile phase, ion-pairing agent, gradient, and integration threshold. Selank is a basic heptapeptide, and its peak shape is sensitive to trifluoroacetic acid concentration and column age. A certificate that reports 98% by area without those parameters is not reproducible.

The counterion matters more here than for a neutral peptide. Acetylation removes one positive charge, but the molecule is still basic, and acetate or trifluoroacetate will associate with the remaining lysine and arginine side chains. The salt form changes the mass per milligram of peptide and can affect cell assays. Ask for the counterion identity and for residual TFA if the preparation will contact cells.

Water content and peptide content close the arithmetic. The 30 mg fill is larger than the parent's usual 5 mg or 10 mg packs, and a 30 mg fill of a hygroscopic powder can contain a meaningful fraction of water. Karl Fischer gives the water figure; nitrogen analysis or amino-acid analysis gives peptide content. At 793 Da, 30 mg is about 37.8 µmol, so a 10% shortfall is nearly 4 µmol of material, enough to matter in a concentration series.

Batch traceability and stability data are the last fields. The certificate should carry a batch number that matches the vial, a synthesis date, a retest date, and the manufacturing site. For the analogue specifically, ask whether the batch was made by acetylating the parent peptide or by synthesising the modified sequence directly. The first route can leave partially acetylated species; the second is cleaner but costs more. Either can produce a good batch, but the impurity profile differs, and the certificate should reflect which route was used. ## Sourcing & Verification

The analogue sits in a crowded part of the catalogue, where names are similar and certificates are often copied. The questions below are the ones that separate a documented batch from a relabelled parent.

- Does the certificate report a mass near 793 Da, or the parent's 751.86 Da?
- Is there fragment evidence that the acetyl group is on the N-terminus?
- Is the acetyl positional distribution reported, including Lys-acetyl species?
- Is the C-terminus confirmed as the amide, and is the parent's amide distinguished from a free acid?
- Is the counterion identified, and is residual TFA measured?
- What is the peptide content per vial, and by which method?
- Was the batch made by acetylating Selank or by direct synthesis?
- Can the supplier provide the chromatogram and the deconvoluted spectrum?
- Is a retain sample kept, and for how long?
- Can one vial be released as a pilot before a ten-vial pack?

The mass question is the first filter. A certificate at 751.86 Da is the parent. A certificate at 793 Da with no fragment data could still be Lys-acetyl rather than N-acetyl, which is why the second question matters.

The third question is the one most suppliers will not have data for. Positional isomers of acetylation are a real impurity class, and a manufacturer who understands the chemistry will either have a method or explain why the route makes the isomer unlikely. A direct synthesis that acetylates the N-terminus on resin, before cleavage, is less likely to produce Lys-acetyl species than a solution-phase acetylation of the folded peptide. Ask which route was used.

The pack size deserves a sanity check. A 30 mg vial is six times the usual 5 mg Selank fill, and the price reflects that. If the study only needs a few hundred micrograms per well, a smaller pack would be cheaper and would reduce the number of freeze-thaw cycles on the bulk. Ask whether a smaller fill is available before committing to the ten-vial pack.

Finally, compare the analogue with the parent on a molar basis, not a mass basis. The 42 Da difference is about 5.6% of the molecular weight, so a 30 mg fill of the analogue contains about 5% fewer moles than a 30 mg fill of Selank would. That is a small correction, but it is the kind of correction that matters when a concentration series is prepared by mass. ## Handling & Storage

Store the lyophilised powder at −20°C, desiccated, and protected from light. Let the vial reach room temperature before opening. The powder is hygroscopic, and condensation on a cold cake is a direct route to hydrolysis at the proline-rich C-terminus.

Reconstitution is usually water or a buffer in the pH 6-7 range. Add the solvent down the wall of the vial and swirl gently. Vortexing creates foam, and a basic heptapeptide adsorbs at the air-liquid interface. If the solution is not clear after a few minutes, inspect it before adding more solvent; over-dilution is harder to correct than under-dilution.

The acetylated peptide is less cationic than the parent, which changes its container behaviour. It should adsorb less to glass than Selank does, but it can still bind to polystyrene and to some filter membranes. Use polypropylene tubes for dilute stocks and check recovery after filtration. A recovery check is cheaper than repeating an experiment, and it takes only one ultraviolet reading.

Avoid alkaline buffers. The C-terminal amide can hydrolyse above pH 8, and the resulting free acid is a different molecule with a different mass. Avoid acidic buffers as well if the assay allows, because the proline-rich tail is acid-labile over long storage. A neutral buffer, used fresh, is the simplest choice.

Aliquot before freezing. One freeze-thaw cycle is usually tolerable; repeated cycles are not. For a working solution, keep it at 2-8°C for the day and discard the remainder. The catalogue storage line is a planning figure, not a stability study, and a laboratory that needs a defined working window should establish it with a fresh preparation and an analytical check.

For cell work, filter through a 0.22 µm membrane and request endotoxin data. Residual TFA is a common impurity and can affect cells at concentrations that are not obvious from the peptide mass. If the assay is sensitive to ionic strength, a volatile salt exchange before addition is worth the extra step. ## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is Selank already amidated?**

Yes. The parent sequence ends in a C-terminal amide, Thr-Lys-Pro-Arg-Pro-Gly-Pro-NH₂. The "amidate" in this product's name describes an inherited feature, not a new modification. The meaningful change is the N-terminal acetyl group.

**What mass should the certificate report?**

About 793 Da for the acetylated peptide, against 751.86 Da for the parent. The 42 Da difference corresponds to the acetyl group. A certificate that reports the parent mass is describing a different product.

**Why acetylate the N-terminus?**

To block aminopeptidase attack at the free α-amino group. The acetyl group is small and does not disturb the rest of the sequence, but it removes one positive charge, which changes the molecule's net charge from about +3 to about +2.

**Does the modification make the peptide more stable in serum?**

The rationale points that way, but there is no published stability study for this specific analogue. Selank already has a Pro-Gly-Pro tail that slows carboxypeptidase attack, so the added benefit is mainly at the N-terminus. Treat stability claims as hypotheses to test, not specifications.

**Can Lys-acetyl impurities be present?**

Yes, if the acetylation was done in solution without protecting the lysine side chain. The N-acetyl and Lys-acetyl species have the same mass, so a mass spectrum alone cannot distinguish them. Ask for the positional distribution or for a chromatographic method that resolves them.

**Is the 30 mg pack the only size?**

The catalogue lists a 30 mg fill in a ten-vial pack. If a smaller fill is available, it may suit a cell-culture study better and reduce the number of freeze-thaw cycles on the bulk. Ask before ordering.

**Can it be used in cell culture?**

Yes, with a sterile, endotoxin-tested preparation. Filter through a 0.22 µm membrane, request LAL data, and check residual TFA if the assay is sensitive to ionic strength. A lyophilised powder is not sterile by default.

**How should the reconstituted solution be stored?**

At 2-8°C for the working day, protected from light, in a neutral buffer. Do not freeze and refreeze. If longer storage is needed, aliquot and freeze once, then accept that a freeze-thaw cycle may change the impurity profile.
