## Why This Compound

NAD+ sits at the centre of cellular redox chemistry. It accepts hydride equivalents in glycolysis, the tricarboxylic acid cycle, and mitochondrial electron transport, and it is consumed as a substrate by enzymes that transfer ADP-ribose. That dual role explains why it appears in so many research contexts: energy metabolism, sirtuin deacylation, PARP-mediated DNA repair, CD38 signalling, and circadian biology. A laboratory does not usually study NAD+ as a drug candidate; it uses NAD+ to set or perturb the cofactor availability of a system.

The sirtuin connection is the most cited. SIRT1, SIRT3, and SIRT6 use NAD+ as a co-substrate, and their activity is sensitive to the NAD+/NADH ratio. In cell-free assays, adding NAD+ can drive deacetylation; in cell models, the relationship is more complicated because transporters, biosynthesis, and consumption all change the intracellular pool. The literature on CD38 is a useful counterweight: CD38 is a major NAD+ consumer, and its activity has been linked to the age-related decline in tissue NAD+ in mouse models (Camacho-Pereira et al., 2016). That does not mean a vial of NAD+ reverses ageing; it means the cofactor is part of a dynamic system that a well-designed experiment has to account for.

PARP biology adds another layer. PARP1 consumes NAD+ at DNA strand breaks, and severe or chronic activation can deplete the cellular pool. Kim, Zhang, and Kraus described the biochemistry of PARP-1-dependent poly(ADP-ribosyl)ation as a way of converting NAD+ into a nuclear signal (Kim et al., 2005). A laboratory measuring PARP activity may use NAD+ as a substrate, while a laboratory measuring sirtuin activity may use it as a cofactor. The same molecule, two different assay designs, and two different sets of purity requirements.

There is also a transport question. For years, the assumption was that NAD+ could not cross the plasma membrane or the mitochondrial inner membrane, and that cells relied on precursors such as NMN and NR. Work by Davila and colleagues reported that mammalian mitochondria can transport NAD+, which complicates the simple precursor model (Davila et al., 2018). That finding is relevant to anyone designing a compartment-specific experiment: adding NAD+ outside a cell is not the same as raising NAD+ inside a mitochondrion.

The catalogue places NAD+ among peptides because buyers often source it alongside them. That is a merchandising decision, not a chemical classification. The practical consequence is that NAD+ should be judged by different analytical criteria: identity by UV and mass, purity by ion-pairing HPLC, salt form, water content, and enzymatic activity. A peptide certificate template applied to a nucleotide will miss most of what matters.

## What the Batch Record Should Show

Identity for NAD+ starts with the UV spectrum. The adenine ring absorbs strongly near 259 nm, and the ratio of absorbance at 260 and 280 nm is a quick check on composition. A certificate should show the spectrum or at least the absorption maxima and the extinction coefficient used for quantitation. Mass confirmation is also straightforward: the average mass is 663.4 Da, and the protonated ion appears near m/z 664.4 in positive mode, with a complementary negative-mode ion near m/z 662.4. A certificate that lists only "663.4" without a spectrum is quoting the catalogue.

Purity is where nucleotide certificates often become vague. Reversed-phase ion-pairing HPLC is the usual method, because NAD+ is polar and carries phosphate groups. The chromatogram should resolve the main peak from nicotinamide, ADP-ribose, and cyclic ADP-ribose, which are common degradation products or process impurities. A method that reports "99% by area" without naming the column, ion-pairing reagent, gradient, and wavelength is not reproducible. For NAD+, the choice of ion-pairing agent matters more than for a peptide, because the phosphate groups interact strongly with the stationary phase.

The redox state should be stated explicitly. NAD+ and NADH are different molecules with different UV spectra and different biological roles. A supplier that sells "NAD" without specifying oxidised or reduced form is leaving the buyer to guess. The catalogue product is the oxidised form; NADH would be a separate item with its own specification.

Salt form and water content are the next fields. NAD+ is often supplied as the free acid or the disodium salt, and the two have different masses per milligram of active coenzyme. The certificate should name the form and, ideally, report sodium content. Karl Fischer water content matters because the powder is hygroscopic and because NAD+ hydrolyses in the presence of moisture. A 1,000 mg vial that contains 5% water contains 50 mg less material than the label suggests.

Enzymatic activity is a functional check that few certificates include but many assays need. A simple dehydrogenase recycling assay, such as alcohol dehydrogenase with ethanol and a tetrazolium dye, can confirm that the batch reduces and oxidises as expected. This is not a substitute for chemical purity, but it catches material that has degraded while still passing an HPLC area percentage. For cell-based work, endotoxin testing is separate and should be requested.

Traceability closes the record. The certificate's lot number should match the label, with a manufacture date, retest date, and site. NAD+ is not a peptide, so a certificate copied from a peptide template is an immediate warning: the fields that matter for a nucleotide are different, and a supplier who has not adjusted the template has not thought about the molecule. ## Sourcing & Verification

The questions below are specific to NAD+ and are worth asking before a first order.

- Is the product NAD+ or NADH, and does the certificate state the redox form?
- Is it free acid or disodium salt, and is sodium content reported?
- What is the water content by Karl Fischer?
- Which HPLC method was used, and does the chromatogram resolve nicotinamide and ADP-ribose?
- Is the purity reported by area normalisation or against an external standard?
- What is the actual NAD+ content per vial, and how was it measured?
- Is enzymatic activity data available?
- Is endotoxin data available for cell-based work?
- Can the supplier provide the raw chromatogram and the UV spectrum?
- Who manufactured the batch, and what is the retest date?

A pattern to watch for: certificates that report a single "purity 99%" line with no chromatogram, and certificates that use the same document for 100 mg, 500 mg, and 1,000 mg vials. Both suggest a template. ## Handling & Storage

NAD+ is unstable in aqueous solution. The glycosidic bond between nicotinamide and the ribose can hydrolyse, and the rate increases with temperature and pH. The catalogue storage note is explicit: −20°C for the powder, and 2-8°C for aqueous solutions used within 24 hours. That is a practical instruction, not a suggestion. A stock prepared on Monday and used on Friday is a different solution.

Weigh and reconstitute in a buffer that matches the assay. Phosphate-buffered saline is common, but the phosphate can participate in ion exchange and the pH should be checked after dissolving. Avoid alkaline buffers, which accelerate hydrolysis, and avoid prolonged exposure to light, which can degrade the nicotinamide ring. Prepare the stock at a concentration that allows a single dilution into the assay, so the buffer contribution stays small.

The powder is hygroscopic. Let the vial equilibrate to room temperature before opening, wipe the outside, and work quickly. If the powder is clumped or discoloured, that is a warning sign; a fresh vial should be a white to pale yellow powder. Store the unused powder with a desiccant at −20°C, and do not refreeze a vial that has absorbed moisture.

For cell work, filter the stock through a 0.22 µm membrane and check recovery by UV absorbance at 259 nm. NAD+ can adsorb to some filter materials, and the loss is easy to miss because the solution remains clear. Use polypropylene tubes for dilute stocks. If the assay is sensitive to endotoxin, use an endotoxin-tested batch rather than assuming that a nucleotide preparation is clean.

Aliquot aqueous stocks before freezing if the assay cannot be run fresh. Repeated freeze-thaw cycles promote hydrolysis and concentrate solutes as ice forms. The [handling protocols](/peptide-handling-protocols/) cover the general laboratory routine, and the [research-use terms](/research-use-disclaimer/) set out the supply conditions. The 24-hour figure in the catalogue is a useful default; a laboratory that needs a longer window should measure degradation under its own buffer and temperature conditions rather than extending the figure by assumption.

These are bench procedures. NAD+ is sold for laboratory research only and is not for human or animal use.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is NAD+ a peptide?**

No. It is a dinucleotide: two nucleotides joined through a phosphate linkage, with a nicotinamide ring and an adenine ring. It contains no amino acids and has no sequence. The catalogue groups it with peptides because buyers often source it alongside them, but the analytical methods and handling requirements are different.

**Why is NAD+ unstable in water?**

The glycosidic bond between nicotinamide and ribose hydrolyses in aqueous solution, and the rate rises with temperature and pH. Light and trace metals can accelerate the process. The practical rule is to prepare fresh solutions, keep them at 2-8°C, and use them within 24 hours.

**How is NAD+ purity measured?**

Reversed-phase ion-pairing HPLC is standard, usually with UV detection near 259 nm. The method should resolve NAD+ from nicotinamide, ADP-ribose, and cyclic ADP-ribose. Purity by area normalisation is common but does not account for water or salt, so pair it with a content assay and Karl Fischer water data.

**What is the difference between NAD+ and NADH?**

NAD+ is the oxidised form, the electron acceptor. NADH is the reduced form, the electron donor. They have different UV spectra and different roles in metabolism. A supplier should state which form is in the vial; the catalogue product is NAD+.

**Is the product free acid or sodium salt?**

It varies by manufacturer and batch. The certificate should state the form and, ideally, report sodium content. The two forms have different masses per milligram of coenzyme, so the distinction matters when preparing a concentration series.

**What is the best storage routine for the powder?**

At −20°C, desiccated, protected from light. Bring the vial to room temperature before opening to prevent condensation. Aqueous stocks should be kept at 2-8°C and used within 24 hours, or aliquoted and frozen if the assay cannot be run fresh.

**Can NAD+ be used in cell culture?**

Yes, but the preparation should be sterile and endotoxin-tested. Pass the stock through a 0.22 µm membrane and check recovery by UV absorbance. Keep the exposure time short, because NAD+ degrades in culture medium and the breakdown products can affect the readout.

**Why does my assay signal drop over time?**

The likely causes are hydrolysis of NAD+, adsorption to plastic or filter material, and freeze-thaw cycling. Prepare a fresh stock, use polypropylene tubes, check the UV spectrum before and after filtration, and run a no-substrate control to separate degradation from assay drift.
