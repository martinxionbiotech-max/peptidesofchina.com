## Why This Compound

NAD+ is a redox cofactor, not a peptide. It accepts and donates hydride equivalents in glycolysis, the tricarboxylic acid cycle, and mitochondrial electron transport, and it is consumed as a substrate by sirtuins, PARP enzymes, and CD38. That dual role, cofactor and substrate, is why it appears in so many assay designs. A laboratory measuring sirtuin deacetylation uses NAD+ as a co-substrate; a laboratory measuring PARP activity uses it as a substrate for poly(ADP-ribose) synthesis; a laboratory measuring mitochondrial function may use it to set the redox poise of a preparation. The three uses have different purity requirements.

The CD38 literature is a useful corrective to simple "NAD+ decline" stories. Camacho-Pereira and colleagues reported that CD38 activity rises with age in mouse tissues and that its NAD+ consumption contributes to the decline in tissue NAD+ (Camacho-Pereira et al., 2016). That is a mechanistic observation in a mouse model, not a rationale for adding NAD+ to a culture dish. Davila and colleagues added a further complication: mammalian mitochondria can transport NAD+ directly, which weakens the older assumption that cells depend entirely on precursors such as NMN and NR (Davila et al., 2018).

MOTS-c is the opposite case: a peptide encoded in mitochondrial DNA, translated inside the organelle, and exported to act on cytosolic targets. Lee and colleagues described it in 2015 as a 16-residue peptide from a short open reading frame in the 12S rRNA gene, and reported that systemic exposure in diet-induced obese mice activated AMPK, altered folate and methionine cycle metabolites, and changed insulin sensitivity and body composition (Lee et al., 2015). Reynolds and colleagues later reported that MOTS-c levels rise in skeletal muscle after exercise in mice and that the peptide was associated with physical capacity in older animals (Reynolds et al., 2021). Both are mouse studies with clear model limits, and the peptide's short half-life is why the field relies on repeated exposure rather than single measurements.

5-Amino-1MQ is smaller still, and its literature is thinner. It is a membrane-permeable inhibitor of nicotinamide N-methyltransferase, the enzyme that transfers a methyl group from S-adenosylmethionine to nicotinamide and produces 1-methylnicotinamide. Neelakantan and colleagues characterised a series of quinolinium inhibitors and reported that 5-amino-1MQ reversed high-fat-diet-induced weight gain in mice (Neelakantan et al., 2018). The underlying biology is interesting because NNMT sits at a junction: it consumes methyl donors and nicotinamide, and its product is excreted, so inhibition changes both methylation capacity and the nicotinamide pool available for NAD+ salvage. Hong and colleagues had earlier shown that NNMT regulates hepatic nutrient metabolism through Sirt1 protein stabilisation in mice (Hong et al., 2015). None of that makes it a metabolic drug; it is a tool for perturbing a specific enzyme.

The blend's rationale is presumably that all three touch mitochondrial readouts. That is a marketing category, not a mechanism. NAD+ acts on redox and signalling pools, MOTS-c acts through AMPK-associated pathways, and 5-Amino-1MQ inhibits an enzyme in one-carbon and nicotinamide metabolism. They do not share a single node, and a change in a Seahorse readout after exposure to the blend cannot be assigned to any one of them.

## What the Batch Record Should Show

The catalogue floor is ≥98% by area, but a blend needs more. The first question is whether one assay or three were used. NAD+ is a polar dinucleotide that requires ion-pairing reversed-phase chromatography and detection near 259 nm. MOTS-c is a peptide best run on a C18 gradient at 214 nm with mass confirmation. 5-Amino-1MQ is a small cation with strong ultraviolet absorbance and a very different retention profile. A single generic gradient at 214 nm will not quantify all three. If the certificate shows one chromatogram with one purity figure, it is describing the mixture as an entity, not the components.

Ask for a per-component assay table. It should list, for each component, the method, the column and mobile phase, the detection wavelength, the observed retention time, the calculated content in milligrams per vial, and the identity evidence. For NAD+, identity should include the ultraviolet spectrum and a mass consistent with 663.4 Da. For MOTS-c, a deconvoluted mass near 2174 Da and, ideally, sequence coverage. For 5-Amino-1MQ, a mass consistent with the cation at m/z 159.2 and a statement of the counterion.

The counterion question is not cosmetic. A quaternary quinolinium cation needs an anion, and the common choices are iodide, bromide, or chloride. Iodide is heavy and can interfere with cell-based assays at higher concentrations; bromide and chloride are lighter but change the mass per milligram of active cation. A certificate that names only "5-Amino-1MQ" leaves the buyer unable to convert a weighed amount into molar concentration. The same logic applies to NAD+, which is commonly supplied as the free acid or the disodium salt. Two salts, two masses per milligram.

MOTS-c oxidation deserves its own field. The sequence contains two methionines and one tryptophan. Methionine sulfoxide adds 15.99 Da per residue, and tryptophan oxidation adds another 16 Da and can open the indole ring under stronger conditions. A batch that was clean at release can acquire a leading shoulder after months on a shelf. Ask whether the +16 and +32 species were resolved from the main peak, and whether the method could have seen them.

NAD+ degradation products belong on the record too. NAD+ hydrolyses to nicotinamide and ADP-ribose, and it can cyclise to cyclic ADP-ribose. An ion-pairing chromatogram should show the main peak separated from those species. If the certificate reports "99% by area" without naming the impurity peaks, the method may be blind to them. For a cofactor assay, a few percent of hydrolysed material can change the effective concentration in a way that a purity figure hides.

Fill mass and water content close the arithmetic. The declared fill is 120 mg per vial, made up of 100 mg NAD+, 10 mg MOTS-c, and 10 mg 5-Amino-1MQ. Peptide content, counterion mass, and Karl Fischer water all reduce the amount of active material below the fill weight. On a 120 mg fill, 5% water is 6 mg. That is small in absolute terms and large relative to the 10 mg MOTS-c component. Ask for the water content and for the assay value of each component, not just the total.

Homogeneity is the last field, and the one most likely to be missing. Three powders with different particle sizes and densities will stratify during shipping unless they were co-dissolved and lyophilised together. Ask whether the blend was made in solution or by dry mixing, and whether uniformity was checked at the top, middle, and bottom of the batch.

## Sourcing & Verification

Blends of this type are common in catalogue listings and rare in well-documented manufacturing. The questions below are the ones that change the answer.

- Is there a per-component assay, or only a single mixture purity figure?
- What are the measured contents in milligrams for NAD+, MOTS-c, and 5-Amino-1MQ?
- Which salt form of NAD+ is present, free acid or disodium?
- What is the counterion for 5-Amino-1MQ, and is its mass accounted for?
- Is MOTS-c oxidation reported as a percentage, with the method stated?
- Are NAD+ hydrolysis products resolved in the chromatogram?
- Was the blend made in solution or by dry mixing, and how was uniformity checked?
- What is the water content by Karl Fischer?
- Can the supplier provide one vial as a pilot before a ten-vial pack?
- Does the batch number on the certificate match the vial?

A supplier who answers the first two questions precisely is usually a manufacturer or a well-organised distributor. A supplier who answers with "120 mg total, 98% pure" is describing a fill weight, not a composition.

The salt questions are where cost hides. NAD+ disodium salt contains roughly 6.5% sodium by mass, so 100 mg of the salt carries about 93.5 mg of the free acid equivalent. If the certificate does not state the form, a concentration calculated from the label can be off by several percent. For 5-Amino-1MQ, an iodide salt carries a large anion: the cation is 159.2 Da, iodide is 126.9 Da, so the salt is only about 56% cation by mass. A 10 mg fill of the iodide salt contains about 5.6 mg of the active cation. That is the kind of detail a blend label tends to omit.

The pack logic matters for study design. Ten vials of a three-component blend suit a screen where all three are present in every arm. They do not suit a study that needs to vary one component while holding the others constant, because the ratio is fixed at manufacture. If the hypothesis concerns NNMT inhibition specifically, buy 5-Amino-1MQ alone; if it concerns mitochondrial peptide signalling, buy MOTS-c alone. ## Handling & Storage

Store the lyophilised blend at −20°C, desiccated, and protected from light. Let the vial reach room temperature before opening. NAD+ is hygroscopic and acid-labile; moisture on a cold cake is a direct route to hydrolysis, and the resulting nicotinamide will be present in the first reconstitution whether or not it appears on the certificate.

Reconstitution is the step where the three components diverge. NAD+ dissolves readily in water. 5-Amino-1MQ, as a small organic cation salt, is also freely soluble. MOTS-c is the difficult one: a 16-residue peptide with several hydrophobic residues can be slow to dissolve and may need gentle swirling over time rather than vortexing. If the peptide goes into solution last, a stock made quickly can be undersaturated in MOTS-c while the other two are fully dissolved. That is a silent error, because the solution looks clear.

Use water or a buffer in the pH 6-7 range. NAD+ hydrolyses faster at alkaline pH, and the adenine ring is sensitive to prolonged light exposure. Avoid phosphate buffers if the assay is sensitive to phosphate; the dinucleotide already carries two phosphate groups, and adding more can complicate ion-pairing chromatography. If the stock will be stored for more than a few hours, purge the headspace with argon and keep it at 2-8°C.

Do not assume the three components freeze alike. NAD+ and 5-Amino-1MQ tolerate a freeze-thaw cycle better than a peptide with oxidisable residues. Aliquot before freezing, keep the aliquots dark, and discard rather than refreeze. For cell work, filter through a 0.22 µm membrane and check recovery; the peptide component may adsorb to the filter, and the loss will not be visible.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is the molar ratio of the three components?**

Approximately 33:1:14 for NAD+ : MOTS-c : 5-Amino-1MQ. The calculation uses 100 mg at 663.4 Da, 10 mg at about 2174 Da, and 10 mg at 159.2 Da for the cation. If the 5-Amino-1MQ is supplied as an iodide salt, the cation mass is lower and the molar figure changes.

**Does the 120 mg figure refer to each vial?**

It should. The declared fill is 100 mg NAD+, 10 mg MOTS-c, and 10 mg 5-Amino-1MQ, for 120 mg of solids per vial. Confirm this on the release document, because some listings quote the total across a pack rather than the fill per vial.

**Can one HPLC method quantify all three components?**

Not reliably. NAD+ needs ion-pairing chromatography and detection near 259 nm; MOTS-c is a peptide run on a C18 gradient at 214 nm; 5-Amino-1MQ is a small cation with a different retention profile. A single generic gradient may show peaks but will not quantify them without component-specific calibration.

**Is the MOTS-c component tested for methionine oxidation?**

That is the question to ask. The sequence contains two methionines, and the sulfoxide forms add 16 and 32 Da. A certificate that reports only a mixture purity figure has not addressed it. Ask for the oxidised fraction and the method used to resolve it.

**Which salt form of NAD+ is used?**

It varies. NAD+ is commonly supplied as the free acid or the disodium salt, and the two have different masses per milligram of coenzyme. The certificate should name the form and, ideally, report sodium content. Without that, a concentration calculated from the fill weight is approximate.

**What counterion does 5-Amino-1MQ carry?**

The common forms are iodide, bromide, and chloride. The counterion changes the mass per milligram of active cation, and iodide can interfere with some cell-based assays. Ask for the identity and the assay value of the cation, not just the fill weight of the salt.

**Can the blend be sterile-filtered for cell work?**

Yes, through a 0.22 µm membrane, but check recovery of the peptide component and request endotoxin data. A lyophilised powder is not sterile by default, and a three-component blend gives three opportunities for contamination.

**Is there published evidence that the three act synergistically?**

No. The published work treats NAD+, MOTS-c, and 5-Amino-1MQ as separate tools with different targets. The blend is a procurement format, not a studied combination. A study that needs to attribute an effect should use the components separately.

## The Author's Take

**Position.** I would buy this blend only as a convenience for a broad mitochondrial screen, and I would budget for three assays rather than one.

**Reasoning.** The three components do not share a mechanism, a compartment, or an analytical method. NAD+ is a cofactor that participates in redox chemistry and serves as a substrate for sirtuins, PARP, and CD38. MOTS-c is a mitochondrial-encoded peptide with AMPK-associated effects in mouse models. 5-Amino-1MQ is an NNMT inhibitor whose counterion may be iodide, which matters for cell work. The label's 100 + 10 + 10 mg looks balanced, but the molar ratio is about 33:1:14, so the blend is overwhelmingly NAD+ by molecule count. None of that makes it a bad product; it makes it a screening format. If a laboratory wants to know which component drives a signal, it needs single-compound vials and a design that can separate them. If the goal is to expose a panel of mitochondrial readouts to three perturbagens at once and then follow up, the blend saves handling time.
