NA Epithalon Amidate is the four-residue peptide Ala-Glu-Asp-Gly with an acetyl group on the N-terminus and an amide on the C-terminus. The parent molecule, Epitalon, is 390.35 Da; the dual modification adds about 41 Da, putting the analogue near 431 Da. Those two terminal caps are a standard medicinal-chemistry move against exopeptidases, and they are easy to describe. The harder question is what evidence supports the analogue specifically, because almost all of the published AEDG literature was generated with the unmodified tetrapeptide. This page separates the parent literature from the analogue's actual documentation, and sets out what a batch record should prove before the material goes into an assay.

## Why This Compound

Epitalon comes out of the peptide bioregulator programme associated with Vladimir Khavinson and the St. Petersburg Institute of Bioregulation and Gerontology. The programme began with epithalamin, a polypeptide fraction prepared from bovine pineal glands, and narrowed over time to short synthetic fragments. AEDG is the fragment that became the catalogue standard. The research themes are consistent across the papers: telomerase activity and telomere length in cultured human cells, pineal melatonin endpoints in aged rodents, and antioxidant enzyme measurements in several tissue models.

The central citation is Khavinson and colleagues' 2003 report that Epitalon induced telomerase activity and telomere elongation in human somatic cells (Khavinson et al., 2003). A 2025 study in *Biogerontology* added an important qualification. Al-dulaimi and colleagues found that Epitalon increased telomere length in human cell lines, but that the mechanism differed by line: some lines showed telomerase upregulation, others showed alternative lengthening of telomeres (Al-dulaimi et al., 2025). That distinction matters because "telomerase activator" is a cleaner label than the data supports. A cell line that uses ALT is not responding through the pathway the label implies.

The literature has a shape that a careful reader should notice. It is concentrated in a small number of laboratories, much of it is in cell culture rather than in vivo, and the endpoints are often surrogate measures such as hTERT transcript level or TRAP assay signal rather than a functional outcome. That does not make the work invalid. It does mean that a catalogue page citing "telomere research" is citing a narrow and model-dependent body of evidence, and that a laboratory repeating an experiment should expect the result to depend on the cell line.

The modification is a separate question from the parent biology. Acetylating the N-terminus and amidating the C-terminus blocks the two ends that aminopeptidases and carboxypeptidases attack. For a tetrapeptide, those ends are a large fraction of the molecule, so the protection is not trivial. The trade-off is that the caps change the molecule's interaction with membranes and with any assay that depends on terminal charge. They do not change the internal sequence, and they do not address the degradation route that matters most for AEDG: isomerisation at the Asp-Gly bond.

That route deserves emphasis because it is invisible on a certificate that reports only purity. Aspartyl residues can cyclise to a succinimide intermediate, which then opens to isoaspartate or to the original aspartate. The reaction is favoured at acidic pH and is accelerated by heat. Geiger and Clarke described the succinimide-linked chemistry in detail decades ago (Geiger & Clarke, 1987). For AEDG, the Asp-Gly sequence is the vulnerable site, and an isoAsp form is a different molecule with the same mass. A purity figure by ultraviolet area does not distinguish them if they co-elute.

There is no published pharmacology for the acetylated, amidated analogue as a distinct molecule. The published AEDG literature describes the parent peptide; the modified form does not appear as a separately studied molecule in the sources reviewed for this page. That is not a reason to avoid the product, but it is a reason to treat claims about the analogue as formulation reasoning rather than evidence. The terminal caps are a plausible stability improvement; whether they produce a measurable difference in a given assay is an empirical question the buyer has to answer.

## What the Batch Record Should Show

Identity starts with mass, and the mass has to be specific. The parent tetrapeptide averages 390.35 Da. The N-acetyl group adds about 42 Da, and the C-terminal amide removes about 1 Da relative to the free acid, so the analogue sits near 431 Da. A certificate that reports "MW 390.35" is describing the parent, not the product. A certificate that reports a single integer near 431 without a spectrum is quoting a catalogue value.

Ask for the deconvoluted mass and for fragment evidence of both termini. The acetyl group is on the N-terminus and the amide is on the C-terminus; a mass alone cannot tell you whether the acetyl landed on the N-terminus or on a side-chain hydroxyl, and it cannot tell you whether the C-terminus is amidated or merely amidated in part. MS/MS coverage of the terminal residues is the direct evidence. For a four-residue peptide, the fragment series is short and easy to read, which makes the omission of terminal evidence harder to excuse.

The catalogue purity floor is ≥98% by area, a figure that means little without the method behind it. Purity by reversed-phase HPLC should be run at 214 nm. AEDG has no tryptophan, tyrosine, or phenylalanine, so there is no useful 280 nm signal. The method should state the column, mobile phase, ion-pairing agent, gradient, and integration threshold. Truncation products of a tetrapeptide differ by one residue, which can be as little as 71 Da, and a shallow gradient is needed to resolve them. A generic 10-minute gradient may show a single clean peak that contains several closely related sequences.

The isoaspartate question belongs on the certificate as a separate value. It will not appear unless the manufacturer was asked for it, because the standard purity method does not distinguish Asp from isoAsp. Ask whether the batch was tested for isoAsp by a method capable of separating the two, such as ion-exchange chromatography, capillary electrophoresis, or enzymatic methylation with protein isoaspartyl methyltransferase. If the answer is that the method was not run, the buyer should know that the main peak may contain a positional isomer.

Counterion and residual reagents come next. Acetate is the usual salt for a small acidic peptide, and residual trifluoroacetate from purification is common. Both contribute mass. The acetylation reaction itself may leave residual acetic acid or acetic anhydride by-products, and the amidation step may leave ammonia or a coupling reagent. A certificate should name the counterion and state residual TFA if the material will contact cells.

Water content and peptide content close the arithmetic. AEDG is hygroscopic, and a 5 mg fill can contain a meaningful fraction of water. Karl Fischer gives the water figure; nitrogen analysis or amino-acid analysis gives peptide content. Without both, the nominal 5 mg is a fill weight, not a quantity of peptide. On a 431 Da molecule, 5 mg is about 11.6 µmol, so a 10% shortfall in peptide content is more than a micromole of material.

Batch traceability is the last field. The certificate should carry a batch number that matches the vial, a synthesis date, a retest date, and the manufacturing site. A document that could apply to any AEDG lot is a template. The [CoA reading guide](/blog/how-to-read-a-peptide-coa-a-researchers-guide-to-hplc-and-ms-reports/) explains the fields that carry weight, and the [quality-testing overview](/peptide-quality-testing/) separates identity, purity, and content.

## Sourcing & Verification

The analogue is a niche product, and the sourcing questions are correspondingly specific. The list below is ordered by how quickly it exposes a supplier who is reselling the parent peptide under a new name.

- Does the certificate report a mass near 431 Da, or the parent's 390.35 Da?
- Is there MS/MS evidence for the N-acetyl and the C-terminal amide?
- Is isoaspartate reported as a separate value, with the method named?
- Is the counterion identified, and is residual TFA measured?
- What is the peptide content per vial, and by which method?
- Is the batch synthesized to the modified sequence, or is the parent peptide modified in a separate step?
- Can the supplier provide the chromatogram and the deconvoluted spectrum rather than a summary PDF?
- Is a retain sample kept, and for how long?
- Can one vial be released as a pilot before a ten-vial pack?
- Does the batch number on the certificate match the vial?

The first question is the fastest filter. A supplier who sends a certificate for 390.35 Da has either shipped the parent or copied the wrong document. Either way, the batch is not what the listing describes.

The second filter is the terminal evidence. Acetylation and amidation are small mass changes, and a low-resolution instrument can miss a partial reaction. A batch that is 80% acetylated and 20% free N-terminus will show a main peak at 431 and a satellite at 389, and the area ratio tells you the extent. Ask for the satellite peaks, not just the main one.

The third filter is the isoAsp value. This is the question most suppliers will not have anticipated, and the answer is informative either way. A manufacturer who understands the molecule will know that Asp-Gly is the weak point and will either have data or explain why the method was not run. A reseller will ask what isoaspartate is.

Finally, check that the pack size fits the study. A 5 mg vial at 431 Da is about 11.6 µmol, enough for a series of cell-culture wells at micromolar concentrations but not for a large in vivo programme. The ten-vial pack is 50 mg total. If the study needs more, ask about bulk pricing and about whether the batch can be reserved. ## Handling & Storage

Store the lyophilised powder at −20°C, desiccated, and protected from light. Let the vial reach room temperature before opening. A cold tetrapeptide cake is hygroscopic, and condensation on the surface is enough to start hydrolysis and to feed the succinimide route.

Reconstitution is usually water or a buffer in the pH 6-7 range. Avoid acidic buffers: the Asp-Gly bond isomerises faster at low pH, and a stock left in an acidic solution will accumulate isoAsp even at refrigerator temperature. Avoid alkaline buffers as well, because the C-terminal amide can hydrolyse to the free acid above pH 8. A neutral, non-amine buffer is the safer choice.

Add the solvent gently and swirl. Do not vortex; a small peptide foams easily, and the air-liquid interface is where oxidation and aggregation begin. If the solution is not clear after a few minutes of swirling, inspect it against a dark background before assuming it is dissolved. A faint haze may be undissolved peptide or a residual carrier.

Use polypropylene tubes for dilute stocks. AEDG is acidic, with two carboxylates and no aromatic residues, and it adsorbs to glass and to some low-bind plastics at low concentration. Check recovery after filtration through a 0.22 µm membrane; a few percent loss is normal, a large loss suggests adsorption or an incompletely dissolved preparation.

Aliquot before freezing. A single freeze-thaw cycle is usually tolerable; repeated cycles are not. For a working solution, keep it at 2-8°C for the day and discard the remainder rather than refreezing. The catalogue storage line is a planning figure, not a stability study, and a laboratory that needs a defined working window should establish it with a fresh preparation and an analytical check.

For cell work, request endotoxin data and filter the stock. TFA is a common residual from purification and can affect cells at concentrations that are not obvious from the peptide mass. If the assay is sensitive to ionic strength, dialyse or use a volatile salt exchange before adding the peptide to the culture. ## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is NA Epithalon Amidate the same molecule as Epitalon?**

No. The sequence is the same, Ala-Glu-Asp-Gly, but the N-terminus is acetylated and the C-terminus is amidated. The modifications add about 41 Da, putting the analogue near 431 Da against the parent's 390.35 Da. They are different molecules with different terminal chemistry.

**Do the modifications improve stability?**

They should slow exopeptidase attack at both ends, which is the standard rationale for terminal capping. There is no published stability study for this specific analogue. The caps do not prevent aspartimide formation at the Asp-Gly bond, which is the main chemical degradation route for AEDG.

**What mass should the certificate report?**

Approximately 431 Da for the acetylated, amidated peptide. The parent is 390.35 Da. A certificate that reports only the parent mass is describing a different product. Ask for the deconvoluted spectrum and for terminal fragment evidence.

**Is the analogue more active than Epitalon?**

There is no published comparison. The analogue appears in catalogue listings, not in the AEDG literature, and the parent's cell-culture findings should not be transferred to it without a batch-specific study. Treat it as a distinct reagent.

**What is the net charge of the analogue?**

At neutral pH it is about −2, the same as the parent. The N-acetyl group removes one positive charge and the C-terminal amide removes one negative charge, so the net change is zero. The charge distribution changes, which can alter retention and membrane interaction, but not the overall charge.

**Can it be used in cell culture?**

Yes, with a sterile, endotoxin-tested preparation. Filter the reconstituted stock through a 0.22 µm membrane, request LAL data, and check residual TFA if the assay is sensitive to ionic strength. A lyophilised powder is not sterile by default.

**How should the reconstituted solution be stored?**

At 2-8°C for the working day, protected from light, in a neutral buffer. Do not freeze and refreeze. If the solution will be stored longer, aliquot and freeze once, then accept that a freeze-thaw cycle may change the isoAsp content.

**Why is the pack size 5 mg?**

Because the molecule is small. At about 431 Da, 5 mg is roughly 11.6 µmol, which is a practical quantity for cell-culture work at micromolar concentrations. The ten-vial pack contains 50 mg total.

## The Author's Take

**Position.** I would buy the amidated analogue only if the experiment specifically needs exopeptidase resistance at both termini. For a replication of the published AEDG work, the parent peptide is the better choice.

**Reasoning.** The published telomerase and telomere work used Epitalon, not the acetylated, amidated analogue. Terminal capping is a reasonable stability strategy, but it changes the molecule, and there is no published pharmacology for the changed molecule. The modification also does nothing about the degradation route that matters most here: aspartimide formation at Asp-Gly, which produces isoaspartate with the same mass and a different conformation. A buyer who wants the analogue should ask for isoAsp data and terminal fragment evidence, because those are the two fields that separate a real batch from a relabelled parent. A buyer who wants to repeat a published experiment should use the peptide that was published.
