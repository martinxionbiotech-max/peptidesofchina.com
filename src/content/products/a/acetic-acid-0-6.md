Acetic acid 0.6% is the least glamorous item in a peptide catalog and one of the easiest to get wrong. It is not an active compound, it has no sequence, and nobody asks about its mechanism. It is a diluent: sterile water with enough acetic acid to bring the pH to roughly 3, which is the range where several aggregation-prone peptides dissolve cleanly instead of clumping into a cloudy suspension.

That narrow job is why the specification matters. A vial of dilute acid that is 0.4% instead of 0.6%, or that was diluted with tap-quality water, or that sat in a warm warehouse for a year, will still look like water. The failure shows up later, as a peptide that will not dissolve or a baseline that drifts.

## Why This Compound

Peptide solubility is a charge problem before it is a concentration problem. In water at neutral pH, a peptide with a cluster of hydrophobic residues and few ionizable side chains sits close to its isoelectric point, where net charge approaches zero and molecules attract each other rather than the solvent. The result is a gel, a haze, or a film on the vial wall. Lowering the pH protonates basic residues — arginine, lysine, histidine — and restores a net positive charge, which increases electrostatic repulsion between peptide molecules and gives water something to solvate.

Acetic acid is a convenient way to do that. It is a weak acid with a pKa near 4.76, it is volatile enough to be removed by lyophilization, and it is already accepted in peptide manufacturing as a counterion. A 0.6% v/v solution works out to about 0.105 M, which puts the pH near 3. That is acidic enough to protonate histidine substantially and to keep many GHRH analogues, IGF-1 fragments, and CJC-1295 variants in solution, without the hydrolysis risk that comes with stronger acid.

The concentration is a compromise, and the compromise is worth understanding. Stronger acid dissolves more stubborn peptides but accelerates peptide-bond hydrolysis, particularly at aspartyl-proline bonds, and can strip the tertiary structure of anything that depends on it. Weaker acid is gentler but may leave the peptide aggregated. The 0.6% figure sits in a practical window that peptide labs have converged on; it is not a pharmacopoeial constant, and a different peptide may need a different vehicle.

Water quality is the other half of the formulation. "Sterile water" is a specification, not a description. Water for injection is controlled for conductivity, total organic carbon, microbial bioburden, and bacterial endotoxin; water that meets those limits behaves predictably as a solvent. Water that does not can carry trace metals that catalyse oxidation, or organic residue that interferes with UV detection at low wavelengths. For a diluent used at millilitre volumes, the water is the product.

## What the Batch Record Should Show

The record for a diluent should be boring, complete, and specific to the lot. Start with concentration: the actual acetic acid content determined by titration against a standardized base, reported as % w/v or % v/v with the convention stated. A supplier that reports only "0.6%" without saying which convention is used has left a small ambiguity that becomes a larger one when a lab tries to reproduce a buffer.

pH belongs on the record as a measured value with the meter calibration date, not as a theoretical range. The specification here is pH 2.9–3.2, and a lot that reads 2.6 is more concentrated than labelled while a lot that reads 3.5 is weaker. Neither is catastrophic for a rinse step, but both change the behaviour of a peptide that is sensitive to acidity.

Osmolality is reported for formulations intended for laboratory cell work, where tonicity affects viability independent of the compound. The ~100 mOsm/kg figure tells the user this is a hypotonic solution that will need dilution or buffering before it contacts cells. That is a handling instruction disguised as a number.

Sterility and endotoxin data close the microbiology section. A sterile-filtered solution should carry a filter integrity record, a bioburden result before filtration, and an endotoxin value in EU/mL. Because dilute acetic acid is inhibitory to many organisms, a "sterility test passed" result is less reassuring than it looks; the acid can suppress growth in the test itself. Buyers who need assured sterility should look for gamma-irradiated or aseptically filled product with a validated process, not just a negative culture.

Container and fill complete the picture. The vial, stopper, and crimp should be named, the fill volume should be checked gravimetrically, and the headspace should be adequate for expansion if the product is frozen. Acetic acid vapour is corrosive to some elastomers over time, and a stopper that degrades will shed particles into a solution that is supposed to be particle-free.

## Sourcing & Verification

Dilute acetic acid is cheap to make and therefore easy to counterfeit in the sense that matters: a supplier can dilute technical-grade glacial acetic acid in purified water, filter it, and label it as sterile. The chemistry is identical; the impurities are not.

The first question is the grade of the starting acid. USP, EP, or JP grade glacial acetic acid is controlled for non-volatile residue, heavy metals, chloride, sulphate, and reducing substances. Technical grade is not. A certificate that names the starting material's compendial grade, and the water system used for dilution, is a better document than one that lists only the final concentration.

The second question is the water system. A supplier that buys purified water from a validated system and can show conductivity and TOC trends is operating a pharmaceutical process. A supplier that dilutes with distilled water from a laboratory still is not, even if the final product passes a one-time test.

The third question is the fill environment. Filling a sterile-filtered solution into vials requires a controlled environment, and the environmental monitoring data for the fill day is part of the batch record. Without it, "sterile" is an assertion about a filter, not about the vial in the box.

For buyers comparing Chinese suppliers, a practical approach is to order one lot, measure pH and osmolality in-house, and run a peptide dissolution trial against a vehicle made in the lab. If a peptide that normally dissolves in freshly prepared 0.6% acetic acid fails in the purchased vehicle, the concentration or the water is off. That test costs a few milligrams of peptide and settles the question faster than a document review.

## Handling & Storage

Store the solution at room temperature, upright, with the cap tight. The main risks are evaporative concentration and microbial ingress. Acetic acid is volatile; a vial left open in a warm room will lose acid and rise in pH, and the change is gradual enough to go unnoticed. Keep the septum intact, swab it with 70% ethanol before withdrawing, and never return unused solution to the vial.

Working in a fume hood is sensible when handling the concentrate, less so for the 0.6% solution. Gloves and eye protection are still appropriate, because the liquid is acidic and will sting a cut. If the solution contacts skin, rinse with water; there is no neutralization step needed at this concentration.

Dilution is where the arithmetic goes wrong. To make a working buffer at a target acetic acid concentration, calculate from the measured concentration on the certificate rather than the nominal 0.6%. If the lot is 0.58%, a 1:10 dilution gives 0.058%, not 0.06%. For most dissolution work the difference is irrelevant; for a pH-sensitive assay it is not.

Do not autoclave the vial. Heat accelerates hydrolysis of any acetic acid esters present as impurities and can deform the closure. If a sterile working solution is needed, filter through a 0.22 µm PVDF or PES membrane into a sterile container and use it the same day.

| Phosphate-buffered saline | ~7.4 | Physiological pH work | Poor solvent for hydrophobic peptides |
| DMSO | n/a | Stock solutions for poorly soluble compounds | Cytotoxic at high final concentrations |

The comparison that matters is acetic acid against bacteriostatic water. Bacteriostatic water is convenient and isotonic-ish, but its benzyl alcohol can interfere with cell assays and does not solve a solubility problem. Acetic acid solves the solubility problem but adds an acid load that must be accounted for in the final buffer. Neither is a universal vehicle, and a lab that keeps both on the shelf is better equipped than one that standardizes on either.

Against PBS, the choice is about what the experiment can tolerate. PBS is the right answer for a peptide that dissolves at neutral pH, and the wrong answer for one that does not. Trying to force a hydrophobic peptide into PBS usually produces a suspension that looks like a solution until it is filtered, at which point most of the peptide is on the membrane.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Which peptides specifically require acetic acid 0.6% for reconstitution?**

GHRH analogues such as CJC-1295 without DAC, sermorelin, and tesamorelin; IGF-1 LR3; AOD-9604; and several other hydrophobic or aggregation-prone sequences. The common feature is a cluster of hydrophobic residues or a tendency to gel at neutral pH. After dissolution, the acidic stock is diluted into a buffered vehicle to reach the working concentration.

**What is the reconstitution protocol for peptides using acetic acid?**

Add the 0.6% solution directly to the lyophilized cake, using the smallest volume that gives complete dissolution, and swirl or roll gently without vortexing. Once the peptide is in solution, dilute into PBS or another compatible buffer to the working concentration and check the final pH. Prepare dilutions on the day of use rather than storing the acidic stock.

**Can I use regular vinegar instead of 0.6% acetic acid?**

No. Household vinegar is a food product with variable acidity, colour bodies, sulphites, and microbial content. It is not a controlled laboratory reagent, and its use introduces unknowns that cannot be corrected afterward. Use a compendial-grade diluted acid prepared with water for injection.

**Is 0.6% acetic acid the same concentration as 0.1 M acetic acid?**

Approximately, yes. Glacial acetic acid has a density of about 1.049 g/mL and a molecular weight of 60.05, so 0.6 mL per 100 mL corresponds to roughly 0.105 M. The two descriptions are close enough for most laboratory work, but use the measured concentration on the certificate for calculations rather than assuming equivalence.

**How long does an opened vial last?**

Treat an opened vial as a working reagent: keep it capped at room temperature, swab the septum before each withdrawal, and discard it after the session or within a few days. Dilute acid at pH 3 is not a hospitable environment for most organisms, but it is not sterile once the septum has been pierced.

**Can I filter the solution to sterilize it?**

Yes, a 0.22 µm PVDF or PES filter is appropriate. Avoid cellulose acetate membranes, which are attacked by acid over time. Filter into a sterile container and use the filtrate the same day; filtration does not preserve sterility indefinitely.

**Does the acetic acid interfere with cell-based assays?**

At the final concentrations typically used after dilution, the acid is neutralized by the buffer. The risk is osmotic and pH shock if the acidic stock is added directly to culture medium without dilution. Always add the stock slowly with mixing and confirm the final pH.
