KPV is a three-residue peptide — lysine, proline, valine — that corresponds to the last three amino acids of α-melanocyte-stimulating hormone. It is supplied as a lyophilized powder, usually in 10 mg vials, and it sits in an unusual corner of the catalog: small enough to synthesise cheaply, well characterised in rodent gut models, and almost always sold without the analytical data that would let a laboratory confirm what is in the vial.

That gap is the reason this page exists. The research literature on KPV is narrow but consistent, and the sourcing questions are specific. A buyer needs to know whether the powder is the tripeptide rather than a longer melanocortin fragment, whether the counterion has been declared, and whether the certificate describes this batch or a generic template. The sections below cover the biology, the batch record, the handling details that change assay outcomes, and the comparisons that come up most often.

## Why This Compound

KPV takes its name from its sequence: Lys-Pro-Val. In the melanocortin system it is written as α-MSH(11-13), the C-terminal tripeptide of a 13-amino-acid hormone produced by the pituitary and by peripheral tissues. The full hormone has a broad receptor profile across the five melanocortin receptors; the truncated fragment does not. That difference is the whole research rationale. Laboratories use KPV when they want to study a melanocortin-associated signalling event without the full peptide's receptor breadth.

The mechanistic literature centres on two ideas. The first is receptor engagement. KPV has been described as interacting with MC1R, a G-protein-coupled receptor expressed on leukocytes, intestinal epithelial cells, and other tissues. The second is downstream signalling: several reports describe inhibition of NF-κB activation, with reduced nuclear translocation of the p65 subunit and lower output of pro-inflammatory cytokines in stimulated cells.

The most cited transport paper is Dalmasso and colleagues' 2008 study in *Gastroenterology*. The group reported that KPV uptake into intestinal epithelial cells depended on PepT1, the di- and tripeptide transporter that is expressed in the small intestine and induced in inflamed colon. When PepT1 was silenced, the peptide's effect on NF-κB activation was lost. That finding matters for two reasons. It explains why a tripeptide, which would normally be expected to have poor oral bioavailability, is studied in enteral models at all. It also means the assay system has to express the transporter for the result to be reproducible.

Kannengiesser and colleagues published a companion observation in *Inflammatory Bowel Disease* in 2008, testing KPV in two murine colitis models. They reported lower disease activity and reduced cytokine output in treated animals, with effects on macrophage and epithelial signalling. Xiao and colleagues took the delivery question further in *Molecular Therapy* in 2017, encapsulating KPV in hyaluronic acid-functionalised nanoparticles and reporting improved colonic delivery in a murine ulcerative colitis model. Read together, the three papers describe a small peptide with a plausible transport route, a receptor-linked mechanism, and a delivery problem that is still being worked on.

Chemistry explains the market. A three-residue peptide with no disulfide bonds, no glycosylation, and no unusual modifications is about as simple as peptide synthesis gets. Crude material can be produced at low cost, purified by preparative HPLC, and lyophilised in small batches. The barrier to entry is low, which is good for price and bad for documentation. A trading company can buy 10 mg vials from a contract manufacturer, relabel them, and never see a chromatogram.

There is also a stability story. Proline at position two restricts backbone flexibility and reduces susceptibility to some peptidases, and the PepT1 literature implies that the peptide survives long enough in the intestinal lumen to be transported. That is a research observation, not a formulation claim: it does not mean a KPV solution is stable indefinitely, and it says nothing about what happens at 37°C in a buffer at pH 8.

## Compound Profile

The free base mass is 342.4 Da, which puts the singly protonated ion at m/z 343.4. There are no aromatic residues, so the peptide has no useful absorbance at 280 nm; detection depends on the peptide bond at 200-220 nm. That single fact shapes most of the analytical method choices described below.

## What the Batch Record Should Show

A KPV certificate of analysis should let an independent analyst answer four questions: is this the right sequence, how much of it is present, what else is in the vial, and which lot does the document describe. Most certificates answer the first question badly and the second not at all.

**Identity by mass.** Electrospray ionisation in positive mode should give an [M+H]+ ion near m/z 343.4 and, depending on the instrument, a doubly charged ion near 172.2. A certificate that prints "molecular weight 342.4" is quoting the catalogue value. It is not evidence. Ask for the spectrum with the charge states annotated, and for MS/MS fragmentation if the supplier claims sequence confirmation. For a tripeptide, the b2 and y2 fragments are diagnostic and easy to read.

**Purity by reversed-phase HPLC.** KPV is typically run on a C18 column with a water/acetonitrile gradient containing 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid, with detection at 214 nm. Area normalisation at that wavelength reports the main peak as a percentage of ultraviolet-absorbing material. It does not see water, salts, or the counterion. A certificate that says 99% by area normalisation and 86% peptide content by mass is internally consistent and far more useful than one that reports only the first number.

The low detection wavelength is a practical trap. At 214 nm, trifluoroacetate absorbs strongly, so baseline drift and gradient artefacts are common. A chromatogram with a flat baseline and a single symmetrical peak is reassuring; one with a rising baseline and an unintegrated shoulder is not. Ask whether the method was run against a reference standard or by area normalisation alone.

**Counterion.** KPV is commonly supplied as the acetate salt, but trifluoroacetate can remain from purification, and some suppliers use hydrochloride. The counterion changes the mass of material in the vial, which changes the amount of peptide a laboratory actually weighs out. The certificate should name it. If it does not, treat the fill weight as total powder rather than peptide.

**Water content and peptide content.** Lyophilised powders retain water, and Karl Fischer titration is the standard measurement. Peptide content is usually derived from nitrogen analysis or amino acid analysis after hydrolysis. Without both values, a nominal 10 mg vial may contain meaningfully less than 10 mg of peptide. This is the most common source of irreproducibility between two laboratories using material from different suppliers.

**Endotoxin and bioburden.** For cell-based work, endotoxin by LAL is worth requesting. It is not a universal release test for catalogue peptides, and a certificate without it is not automatically deficient, but the omission should be known before a primary-cell assay is designed. The same applies to sterility: a lyophilised powder is not sterile by default.

**Batch traceability.** The lot number on the certificate should match the vial label. The document should carry a synthesis date, a retest or expiry date, and the manufacturing site. A certificate that could describe any KPV batch ever made is a template, not a record. The [CoA reading guide](/blog/how-to-read-a-peptide-coa-a-researchers-guide-to-hplc-and-ms-reports/) walks through the fields in more detail, and the [purity testing note](/blog/why-purity-and-concentration-tests-are-not-enough/) explains why a single percentage is not a specification.

## Sourcing & Verification

KPV is sold by peptide catalogues, cosmetic ingredient suppliers, and trading companies that list hundreds of compounds. The questions below separate them faster than a price comparison.

- Does the certificate print the sequence Lys-Pro-Val, or only the trade name? A supplier who cannot print three amino acids is almost certainly reselling.
- Is the counterion declared, and is residual trifluoroacetic acid reported? TFA is a common purification residue and a useful marker of process control.
- Is purity reported by area normalisation or against an external standard? The first is standard practice; the second is better evidence.
- What is the peptide content in milligrams per vial, and how was it measured?
- Can the supplier provide the chromatogram and mass spectrum as separate files rather than a single combined PDF?
- Does the supplier name the manufacturing site, and can it answer a process question without forwarding it to a third party?
- Is a retained sample kept from the lot, and for how long? Retention matters if an assay fails six months later.
- How was the batch stored before shipment, and is there a temperature record for the international leg?

The [manufacturer verification protocol](/blog/china-peptide-manufacturer-verification-a-2026-b2b-sourcing-protocol/) covers entity checks and site questions in more detail. For KPV specifically, the most useful single question is about the counterion, because it is the field most often omitted and the one that most directly affects how a laboratory calculates concentration.

A note on catalogue variants. KPV appears as a standalone vial and as a component of fixed blends, including an oral KPV format, a [GHK-Cu + KPV blend](/catalog/ghk-cu-50mg-kpv-10mg/), and a [BPC-157 + TB-500 + KPV blend](/catalog/bpc-157-10mg-tb-500-10mg-kpv-10mg/). For a first study, the single compound gives cleaner attribution.

## Handling & Storage

Lyophilised KPV should be stored at −20°C, desiccated, and protected from light. Three handling details matter more than the storage temperature on the label.

First, equilibrate the vial to room temperature before opening. A cold vial attracts condensation, and moisture on the lyophilised cake accelerates hydrolysis and aggregation. Wipe the outside, wait twenty minutes, then open in a low-humidity environment.

Second, choose the solvent for the assay rather than by habit. KPV dissolves in water and in phosphate-buffered saline; the pH of the final solution should sit near neutrality for most cell work. Avoid alkaline buffers, which promote deamidation at the proline-adjacent bond and shorten the useful life of the stock. Add the solvent gently down the wall of the vial and swirl rather than vortex. Vortexing creates foam, and peptide at the air-liquid interface is prone to aggregation.

Third, aliquot before freezing. A reconstituted stock kept at 2-8°C is a short-term reagent; repeated freeze-thaw cycles promote aggregation and adsorption to plastic. Divide the solution into single-use portions, freeze at −20°C or below, and do not refreeze. Low-binding tubes reduce losses at low concentrations, which matters for a tripeptide that is often used in the low micromolar range.

Filtration is reasonable for cell-culture work, but check adsorption. A 0.22 µm polyethersulfone filter is a common choice; a small peptide can bind to some membrane materials, so run a recovery check with a known concentration before trusting the filtered stock. The [reconstitution guide](/blog/peptide-lyophilization-reconstitution-guide-for-researchers/) and the [solubility troubleshooting note](/blog/peptide-solubility-troubleshooting/) cover the common failure modes.

All handling described here is bench-scale laboratory work. KPV is supplied for research use only and is not intended for administration to humans or animals.

## How It Compares

**BPC-157.** The comparison buyers ask about most often. BPC-157 is a 15-residue peptide studied in gastric lesion, tendon, and vascular models, with proposed mechanisms involving nitric oxide, VEGFR-2, and growth factor expression. KPV is a three-residue melanocortin fragment studied in intestinal inflammation and transport models. The overlap is topical — both appear in gut research — but the assays are not interchangeable. A PepT1-dependent epithelial readout is a KPV experiment; a tendon fibroblast migration assay is a BPC-157 experiment. The [BPC-157 page](/catalog/bpc-157/) sets out the other side of the comparison.

**TB-500.** A 43-amino-acid actin-binding fragment derived from thymosin beta-4. There is no mechanistic overlap with KPV beyond the fact that both are peptides. Comparisons usually come from blend marketing rather than from the literature.

**GHK-Cu.** A copper-binding tripeptide with its own literature on extracellular matrix and wound models. Like KPV it is small and inexpensive to synthesise, and like KPV it is often sold with thin documentation. The [GHK-Cu page](/catalog/ghk-cu/) covers the copper-chelating chemistry, which is the main analytical difference.

**KPV oral formats.** The same sequence in a different presentation. Oral delivery research is the reason KPV is interesting, but a capsule or enteric format adds excipients and a dissolution step to the analytical picture. If the study endpoint is cellular, the lyophilised powder gives the cleanest starting material.

**Blends.** Fixed combinations are convenient for pipetting and awkward for analytics. If a blend contains KPV, ask for a component-specific assay rather than a single purity figure, because a co-eluting peptide can hide behind the main peak.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is KPV supplied as?**

Lyophilised powder in sealed vials, typically 10 mg per vial in 10-vial packs. The catalogue lists the current formats; the batch certificate should state peptide content rather than only fill weight.

**Is KPV the same as α-MSH(11-13)?**

Yes. α-MSH(11-13) is the positional name for the C-terminal tripeptide of α-melanocyte-stimulating hormone, and Lys-Pro-Val is the sequence. Suppliers use all three names interchangeably, which is one reason sequence confirmation matters.

**How is KPV purity measured?**

Reversed-phase HPLC with ultraviolet detection at 214 nm, usually with a water/acetonitrile gradient and trifluoroacetic acid as ion pair. Because KPV has no aromatic residues, there is no 280 nm option. Purity by area normalisation should be read alongside peptide content by mass.

**What counterion does the powder contain?**

It varies by manufacturer and by batch. Acetate is common; trifluoroacetate can remain from purification; hydrochloride is used in some processes. The certificate should name the counterion explicitly.

**How should KPV be stored?**

At −20°C, desiccated, protected from light. Let the vial reach room temperature before opening to avoid condensation. Reconstituted stock should be aliquoted and frozen rather than stored for weeks at 2-8°C.

**How stable is KPV in solution?**

The tripeptide is more resistant to peptidase digestion than a linear peptide of similar size, which is part of why it is studied in enteral models. That does not make it stable at any pH or temperature. For cell work, prepare fresh stock, keep it cold, and avoid alkaline buffers.

**How does KPV differ from BPC-157?**

Different sequence, different receptor logic, different assays. KPV is a melanocortin fragment studied through MC1R and NF-κB in intestinal models; BPC-157 is a larger peptide studied in gastric, tendon, and vascular models. They are sometimes combined in blends but are not substitutes.

**What documentation should accompany an order?**

A lot-specific certificate with HPLC method and chromatogram, mass spectrum with charge states, counterion, water content, peptide content, and a retest date. Endotoxin and sterility data are separate tests; confirm whether they were run rather than assuming they were.

## The Author's Take

**Position.** I would not buy KPV on price per vial. I would buy the lot with a declared counterion and a peptide-content value, even at a premium, because those two fields are where cheap material usually fails.

**Reasoning.** KPV is a three-amino-acid peptide. The synthesis is not the hard part; the documentation is. A supplier can produce a 99% area-normalisation chromatogram from almost any lot, because the method reports only what absorbs at 214 nm. It will not reveal that the vial contains 7 mg of peptide and 3 mg of acetate and water, and it will not reveal that the certificate was copied from a previous batch. The counterion and peptide-content fields are harder to fake because they require the supplier to know what was weighed and what was filled. When a supplier can answer those two questions without forwarding the email, the rest of the record is usually competent too.
