Benzyl alcohol 0.9% is not a peptide. It is a formulated aqueous solution: 9 mg of benzyl alcohol per millilitre of water, with no buffer, no salt, and no peptide. The catalogue places it under reconstitution because that is how laboratories use it — as a preservative-containing diluent that lets a reconstituted stock be handled repeatedly without a fresh preparation at every bench session. Calling it "BAC water" compresses that description into a nickname, and the nickname hides the specification work that matters.

The specification is a monograph question, not a peptide question. Benzyl alcohol has a defined identity, a defined assay range, and a known degradation route to benzaldehyde. Water has a conductivity limit, a total organic carbon limit, and a microbial limit. The container has a closure integrity requirement. None of these are optional, and none of them are visible on a one-line product listing.

There is a second reason to read this page carefully. Benzyl alcohol is biologically active. It intercalates into membranes, which is exactly why it suppresses microbial growth, and that same activity is why the concentration has to be controlled rather than estimated. A laboratory that treats the preservative as inert will misinterpret a vehicle control. The sections below cover the specification, the batch record, and the handling rules for a reagent that is simple to make and easy to get wrong.

## Why This Compound

Benzyl alcohol is a aromatic alcohol with a hydroxyl group on a benzylic carbon. That structure gives it two useful properties in a laboratory reagent. It is miscible with water up to about 4% at room temperature, and it partitions into lipid bilayers, where it increases membrane fluidity and interferes with the proton-motive machinery that microbes need to maintain viability. At 0.9%, the effect is bacteriostatic rather than reliably bactericidal: it stops multiplication and gives a working solution a margin of safety against low-level contamination introduced during repeated sampling.

The activity is pH-dependent. Benzyl alcohol is a weak acid with a pKa near 15, so it is essentially un-ionised across the pH range of a peptide solution. That is an advantage for formulators: the preservative does not carry a charge that would compete with a peptide for binding sites or change the ionic strength. It also means the preservative's own solubility is the limiting factor, not its ionisation.

The degradation route is oxidation to benzaldehyde, and then to benzoic acid. Light, oxygen, and trace metals accelerate the first step. Benzaldehyde has a characteristic bitter-almond odour, which is a useful bench signal: a bottle that smells strongly of almonds has been open too long or stored warm. The pharmacopoeial assay limits benzaldehyde because it is both a degradant and a reactivity hazard — the aldehyde can form Schiff bases with primary amines, including the N-terminus of a peptide.

That reactivity is the main compatibility question for peptide work. A peptide with a free N-terminus and a lysine side chain can react slowly with benzaldehyde in a preserved solution, producing a covalent adduct that will appear as an extra peak in HPLC and may be biologically inactive. The rate is low at 2–8 °C and over a short working window, but it is not zero, and it is one reason some laboratories prefer a preservative-free diluent for amine-rich peptides. The [reconstitution materials note](/blog/peptide-reconstitution-materials/) covers the solvent choice in more detail.

The hypotonicity is the second practical property. At roughly 90 mOsm/kg, the solution has about one-third of the osmotic pressure of plasma. For a laboratory assay, that matters whenever the diluent is added at a volume that is not negligible relative to the sample. A 1:100 dilution into a buffer is irrelevant; a 1:5 dilution can change cell volume and perturb a readout. The fix is simple — dilute into an isotonic buffer, or match the osmolality of the assay medium — but it has to be designed in, not discovered in the results.

Benzyl alcohol also has a UV absorbance maximum near 257 nm, which is close to the 260/280 nm region used for nucleic acid and protein quantification. A preserved diluent blank is therefore mandatory for any spectrophotometric assay. This is a small point that has ruined more than one concentration measurement.

## What the Batch Record Should Show

Identity and assay come first. A gas chromatography method with a flame ionisation detector is the standard approach for benzyl alcohol in water; the assay should be reported against the label claim, typically within 90–110%, with the method and column stated. A certificate that reports "0.9%" without an assay method has repeated the label rather than measured it.

Benzaldehyde belongs on the same chromatogram. A limit of not more than a few tenths of a percent is typical for a pharmacopoeial-grade solution. If the certificate does not mention benzaldehyde at all, the method probably was not designed to see it, and the batch may be older than the label suggests.

Water quality is the next field. Purified water used in a pharmacopoeial preparation is tested for conductivity, total organic carbon, and microbial counts; the [purity-testing note](/blog/why-purity-and-concentration-tests-are-not-enough/) explains why those limits do not substitute for an assay of the active ingredient. The certificate should state which water grade was used and give the conductivity and TOC results. A pharmacopoeial water grade is a claim about a validated process, not a synonym for clean water.

Sterility and endotoxin are separate. A solution can be sterile and still contain endotoxin, and it can be low in endotoxin and not sterile. For cell work, both matter. Ask for the sterility method (membrane filtration or direct inoculation) and the limulus amebocyte lysate result with its units and limit. The [endotoxin primer](/blog/what-are-endotoxins-why-they-matter/) covers how that limit is chosen.

Particulate matter is often skipped in research-grade material and should not be. A visible-particle inspection and a sub-visible particle count by light obscuration give a direct measure of container and filling quality. A solution that is "clear" to the eye can still fail a sub-visible count by an order of magnitude.

The container closure matters for a multi-dose reagent. A vial that is opened repeatedly must hold a vacuum or at least resist ingress. Ask whether the closure is a chlorobutyl or bromobutyl stopper, whether it is latex-free, and whether the crimp was integrity-tested. A cheap vial with a poor seal turns a preserved solution into a contaminated one, and the preservative only buys time.

Finally, the batch record should link the lot to the fill date and the expiry. Benzyl alcohol solutions are chemically stable for years when kept closed, but the preservative's effectiveness declines as the headspace oxygen is consumed and the pH drifts. A five-year-old bottle with a good assay can still be a poor reagent if it has been opened and closed a hundred times.

## Sourcing & Verification

The first question is what the product actually is. "BAC water" is sold as a laboratory reagent, as a cosmetic ingredient, and as a pharmacy compound. The three are not the same. A laboratory buyer should ask for the pharmacopoeial reference, the water grade, and the sterility claim, and should reject a listing that answers only with "0.9% benzyl alcohol".

The second question is the grade of the benzyl alcohol itself. The compendial monograph sets limits on related substances, including benzaldehyde and chlorinated impurities. A supplier using technical-grade benzyl alcohol and diluting it in clean water can hit 0.9% by assay and still fail the related-substances limit. Ask for the starting-material specification as well as the finished solution.

The third question is fill volume and headspace. A 10 mL vial that contains 10.5 mL is a different product from one that contains 9.8 mL, and the difference matters when the reagent is used as a diluent at a fixed ratio. Ask for the fill range and the overfill policy. The [quality-testing overview](/peptide-quality-testing/) lists the tests that apply to formulated reagents as well as to peptides.

The fourth question is documentation chain. A certificate issued by a trading company for a product made by someone else is still useful, but the manufacturing site must be named; the [third-party CoA note](/blog/why-we-do-not-provide-third-party-coas-and-what-that-actually-means/) sets out what such a document can and cannot prove. Entity checks for a simple solution are the same as for a peptide; the [manufacturer verification protocol](/blog/china-peptide-manufacturer-verification-a-2026-b2b-sourcing-protocol/) lists them.

The fifth question is compatibility. If the solution will be used with a specific peptide, ask the peptide supplier whether benzyl alcohol is compatible with that sequence. A preservative that reacts with an N-terminal amine or a lysine side chain is not a neutral choice. Where compatibility is unknown, run a small pilot: prepare the solution, hold it at the intended storage temperature, and check for new peaks by HPLC at 24 and 72 hours.

A final check is the odour and appearance at receipt. A fresh lot has a faint, sweet aroma. A strong almond smell indicates benzaldehyde formation. A yellow tint or a visible particle indicates a problem that the certificate should have caught. Reject the lot rather than filtering it and hoping.

## Handling & Storage

Keep the vial closed and upright. Store at room temperature or 2–8 °C according to the label, and refrigerate after the first opening. Light exposure accelerates oxidation, so a dark cupboard or an amber secondary container is better than a windowsill. Do not freeze the solution: ice formation concentrates the solutes and can crack the vial.

Open in a clean area, wipe the stopper with 70% isopropanol, and let it dry before inserting a needle or a pipette tip. Do not leave a needle in the stopper between sessions; the septum leaks, and the headspace exchanges with room air. Use a fresh sterile syringe or tip for each withdrawal.

Record the opening date on the label. The catalogue's working window is a planning figure for laboratory use; the actual window depends on how often the vial is opened, the temperature, and the contamination load in the bench area. A solution that looks clear can still carry a biofilm on the stopper.

Do not autoclave the solution. Benzyl alcohol is volatile enough that steam sterilisation will strip it from the water and change the concentration, and the container may not survive the cycle. If a sterile diluent is needed, buy a sterile-filled product rather than sterilising a preserved one in-house.

Dispose of expired or cloudy solution as chemical waste under your local [compliance rules](/peptide-compliance-risk/), not down the sink. Benzyl alcohol is biodegradable but the solution may contain peptide or buffer residues from a previous use, and the container is glass. The [handling hub](/peptide-handling-protocols/) covers general laboratory practice for formulated reagents, and the [CoA guide](/blog/how-to-read-a-peptide-coa-a-researchers-guide-to-hplc-and-ms-reports/) shows how to read a certificate that mixes peptide and excipient tests.

## How It Compares

**Unpreserved sterile water.** No preservative, no buffering capacity, and no margin against contamination once opened. It is the right diluent for a single-use preparation or for a peptide that reacts with benzyl alcohol. It is the wrong choice for a multi-dose working stock that will be sampled repeatedly.

**Phosphate-buffered saline (PBS).** Isotonic, buffered, and compatible with most cell assays, but not preserved. A PBS stock that is opened repeatedly is a microbial risk unless it is filter-sterilised into single-use aliquots. PBS also contains phosphate, which can precipitate with some counterions.

**0.9% sodium chloride.** Isotonic and preservative-free. It is a better choice than BAC water when osmolality matters and a single-use aliquot is acceptable. It has no antimicrobial cover, so the same handling discipline applies.

**Benzyl alcohol 0.9% (BAC).** Preserved, hypotonic, and slightly acidic. It buys handling convenience at the cost of a vehicle that is not inert. For a multi-dose laboratory stock of a stable peptide, that trade is often worth it. For an amine-rich peptide or a sensitive cell assay, it may not be.

**Ethanol 0.5–1%.** Sometimes used as a preservative alternative. It is volatile, flammable, and can denature peptides at higher concentrations. It is not a drop-in substitute, and the assay blank changes.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is benzyl alcohol 0.9% a peptide?**

No. It is a formulated aqueous solution of benzyl alcohol in water. The catalogue files it under reconstitution because it is used as a diluent, not because it has peptide structure. Its CAS, molecular weight, formula, and sequence fields are marked not applicable for that reason.

**What is the difference between BAC water and unpreserved sterile water?**

Unpreserved sterile water contains no benzyl alcohol and is intended for single use. BAC water contains 0.9% benzyl alcohol, which suppresses microbial growth and makes repeated sampling less risky. The preservative does not sterilise a contaminated solution, and it does not replace aseptic technique.

**Why is the solution hypotonic?**

It contains 0.9% benzyl alcohol and no sodium chloride, so its osmolality is around 90 mOsm/kg against roughly 290 mOsm/kg for plasma. For small additions to a buffered assay, the effect is negligible. For larger volume replacements, the diluent should be matched to the assay medium.

**Can the solution be autoclaved?**

No. Benzyl alcohol is volatile and steam sterilisation can strip it from the solution, changing the concentration. Buy a sterile-filled product if sterility is required, and do not re-sterilise an opened vial.

**Does benzyl alcohol interfere with UV assays?**

Yes. It absorbs near 257 nm, close to the wavelengths used for nucleic acid and protein quantification. Run a diluent blank and subtract it, or use a preservative-free buffer for the assay.

**How is the concentration verified?**

The standard method is gas chromatography with flame ionisation detection, reported against the label claim with the column and conditions stated. A related-substances test for benzaldehyde belongs on the same certificate. An assay without a method is a label, not a measurement.

**Can it react with peptides?**

Benzaldehyde, the oxidation product, can form Schiff bases with primary amines, including a peptide's N-terminus and lysine side chains. The reaction is slow at 2–8 °C but not zero. Peptides with free amines are better handled in a preservative-free diluent, or in a preserved diluent for a short working window.

**Is the solution sterile?**

Only if the lot was filled and tested as sterile. The catalogue's pharmaceutical-grade variants are described as sterile, but the certificate should state the sterility method and the result. A preserved solution is not automatically sterile.

## The Author's Take

**Position.** I would rather buy a preservative-free diluent and aliquot it than buy a preserved one and treat it as sterile. The preservative is a convenience, and convenience is where laboratories get careless.

**Reasoning.** The failure mode with BAC water is not chemical; it is behavioural. A bottle that can be opened twenty times invites twenty openings, and each one is a chance for a contamination event that the preservative may suppress but not eliminate. The second failure mode is analytical: benzyl alcohol absorbs in the UV region and reacts slowly with amines, so a preserved diluent can change an assay without anyone noticing. Neither problem is hypothetical. I have seen concentration curves shift after a diluent switch, and I have seen a "sterile" bottle grow a film on the stopper after a month on the bench. Buy the grade you can document, label the opening date, and do not use a preserved solution where a single-use aliquot will do.
