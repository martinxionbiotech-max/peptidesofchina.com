## Why This Compound

The blend combines three peptides with separate research histories. BPC-157 is a 15-residue pentadecapeptide studied in gastrointestinal cytoprotection, angiogenesis, and tendon/ligament models. TB-500 is the 43-residue Thymosin Beta-4 Acetate studied for actin sequestration, cell migration, and cardiac ischemia-reperfusion. KPV is a tripeptide corresponding to the C-terminal end of α-melanocyte-stimulating hormone, studied mainly in intestinal inflammation and epithelial barrier models.

The rationale for putting them together is procedural rather than mechanistic. A laboratory running a gut-focused study may want BPC-157's cytoprotective profile and KPV's NF-κB-related profile in the same well, with TB-500 added for a cell-migration readout. Preparing three separate stock solutions introduces three weighing steps, three reconstitution volumes, and three opportunities for concentration error. A pre-blended vial removes those steps. That is a real advantage in a screening setting, where the cost of pipetting error can exceed the cost of the peptides.

The scientific case for the combination is weaker than the procedural case. Most published studies examine one peptide at a time. Combination studies exist, but they are fewer, and the designs vary in ratio, timing, and endpoint. A three-component blend fixes the ratio at 1:1:1 by mass, which may not match any published protocol. If your study is designed to compare against a specific paper, check whether the paper used the same ratio; if it did not, the blend is a new condition, not a replication.

There is also an analytical reason to be careful. The three peptides differ in size by more than an order of magnitude: KPV is 342.4 Da, BPC-157 is 1419.5 Da, and TB-500 is about 4963 Da. In a single reversed-phase HPLC run, the small tripeptide elutes early and may sit near the solvent front, while the 43-mer elutes late and may be broad. A method optimised for one component is rarely optimal for all three. A certificate that reports one purity number for the blend is therefore reporting an aggregate, not a component-level result.

## What the Batch Record Should Show

A blend batch record has to do two jobs: prove that each component is the right molecule, and prove that the three are present in the stated ratio throughout the batch. Most certificates do the first badly and the second not at all.

**Component identity.** Each peptide should have its own identity evidence. For BPC-157, the check is the mass near 1419.5 Da and the 15-residue sequence. For TB-500, the check is the deconvoluted mass near 4963 Da, plus the N-terminal acetylation state and the methionine oxidation level. For KPV, the check is the mass near 342.4 Da and the Lys-Pro-Val sequence. A single "blend confirmed by MS" statement is not component-level evidence; the spectrum of a mixture contains ions from all three, and a dominant peak can hide a missing minor component.

**Blend uniformity.** A lyophilized blend is not automatically homogeneous. The three peptides may crystallise or phase-separate differently during freezing, and the dry cake can vary from the top to the bottom of the vial. A serious batch record includes a content-uniformity study: samples taken from multiple vials across the batch, each assayed for all three components, with acceptance criteria. Without that study, the 1:1:1 ratio is a statement about the fill solution, not about the vial a researcher opens.

**Component assay and purity.** Each component needs a method that resolves it from the other two and from its own impurities. For KPV, UV detection at 214 nm works because the peptide bond absorbs, but the tripeptide has no aromatic residue, so it is invisible at 280 nm. For TB-500, the 43-mer may require a different gradient or a different column chemistry than the two shorter peptides. Ask for the chromatograms, not just the percentages. A method that integrates all peptide peaks together cannot tell you whether one component is 20% low and another 20% high.

**Counterion and water content.** Each component's counterion contributes to mass. The certificate should state the counterion for each peptide and give water content by Karl Fischer. If the blend was lyophilized from a single solution, the counterion profile is a mixture; if the components were lyophilized separately and then combined, the blend record should say so. The two processes have different uniformity risks.

**Fill mass and vial content.** The nominal 30 mg is a sum. The actual fill weight, the density of the cake, and the headspace all affect how much material a researcher can recover. A certificate that gives only the nominal composition without fill weight is not a complete batch record.

**Endotoxin and bioburden.** For cell-based work, endotoxin is a relevant release test. For a blend, the test is performed on the final material, not on the individual components, unless the components were tested separately before mixing. Confirm which approach was used. Sterility is a separate claim and should not be inferred from the word "lyophilized".

## Sourcing & Verification

The first question is who made the blend. A supplier that synthesises all three peptides and blends them in-house can describe the process. A supplier that buys three finished peptides and fills them into one vial is a secondary manufacturer; that can still be a valid product, but the batch record has to be assembled from three incoming certificates, and the uniformity study is often missing.

The second question is whether the certificate is component-specific. Ask for a table with one row per peptide: identity method, observed mass, purity, assay, and counterion. If the certificate has one row for "BPC-157/TB-500/KPV blend" and one purity value, it is not a blend certificate. The [CoA reading guide](/blog/how-to-read-a-peptide-coa-a-researchers-guide-to-hplc-and-ms-reports/) shows what a component-level table looks like.

The third question is the ratio. The catalogue says 10 mg + 10 mg + 10 mg. Ask whether that is peptide mass or salt mass, and whether the assay confirms the ratio after lyophilization. A blend can be 30 mg by fill weight and still be 9 mg + 9 mg + 12 mg by peptide content. For a screening study, that difference may not matter; for a concentration-response study, it does.

The fourth question is the KPV content specifically. KPV is the smallest component, and it is the one most likely to be under-filled or to be lost in a shared HPLC method. Ask for a KPV-specific assay with a calibration curve, not a peak-area percentage. If the supplier cannot provide one, the blend is effectively a two-component product with a label claim for the third.

The fifth question is storage history. A three-component blend has three degradation pathways: BPC-157 is relatively stable, TB-500 is prone to methionine oxidation, and KPV is a small peptide whose stability depends on pH, and it can be lost to adsorption or hydrolysis under some conditions. Ask how the batch was stored before shipment and whether the certificate reports degradation products for each component. ## Handling & Storage

Store the lyophilized blend at −20°C, desiccated and protected from light. Let the vial equilibrate to room temperature before opening. A blend cake can be more hygroscopic than a single-peptide cake because the lyophilization formulation may include bulking agents or salts; moisture uptake is a real risk in a humid room.

Reconstitution is the step where a blend differs most from a single peptide. Add the solvent slowly and swirl; do not vortex. The three components have different solubilities. KPV is highly soluble in water, BPC-157 dissolves readily in water and in dilute acid, and TB-500 benefits from a solvent that avoids strong shear. If one component is slow to dissolve, do not assume the others are still in solution; inspect for particles and give the vial time.

pH matters. KPV contains a free amino group and a C-terminal carboxylate; its solubility and stability change with pH. BPC-157 is more stable away from strongly basic conditions. TB-500's methionine oxidises faster at neutral to slightly alkaline pH. A single buffer cannot be optimal for all three, so choose the buffer that matches the assay and accept a compromise on the others. If the assay allows, prepare the working solution fresh rather than storing it.

Aliquot before freezing. A three-component solution that is frozen and thawed repeatedly will not necessarily degrade at the same rate for each peptide; the resulting ratio can drift. Divide the reconstituted solution into single-use portions, freeze at −20°C or below, and thaw once. Filter sterilization, if needed, should be validated for recovery of all three components; a 0.22 µm filter can adsorb a measurable fraction of a peptide, and the fraction may differ by size.

**How is blend uniformity verified?**

A proper batch record includes content-uniformity data from multiple vials across the batch, with each component assayed separately. If the supplier cannot provide that data, the 1:1:1 ratio is a fill-solution claim rather than a vial-level claim.

## The Author's Take

**Position.** I treat a three-peptide blend as an analytical claim before I treat it as a product. The ratio on the label is only credible if the supplier can show a component-specific assay and a uniformity study, and most cannot.

**Reasoning.** Blending is easy; proving a blend is hard. The three molecules here differ in size, charge, and stability, so a single HPLC method will favour one and penalise another. KPV is the smallest component and the one most likely to be under-filled, and it is also the hardest to see at 280 nm. A certificate that reports one purity number for the mixture is not telling you whether the KPV is present at 10 mg or 6 mg. The uniformity question is separate: a lyophilized cake can be inhomogeneous even when the fill solution was correct. If a laboratory needs the combination, I would ask for the component data first and the price second. If the data is not available, buying the three peptides separately gives a cleaner experiment for roughly the same analytical effort.
