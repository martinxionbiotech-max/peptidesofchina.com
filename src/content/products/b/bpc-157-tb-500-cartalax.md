## Why This Compound

The three components come from different research frameworks. BPC-157 is a 15-residue pentadecapeptide studied in gastrointestinal cytoprotection, angiogenesis, and tendon/ligament models. TB-500 is the 43-residue Thymosin Beta-4 Acetate studied for actin sequestration, cell migration, and cardiac ischemia-reperfusion. Cartalax is a four-residue peptide — Ala-Glu-Asp-Pro — from the Russian bioregulator tradition, studied in cartilage and chondrocyte models.

The rationale for combining them is tissue-specific. BPC-157 contributes the vascular and cytoprotective arm; TB-500 contributes the cell-migration and cytoskeletal arm; Cartalax contributes a cartilage-directed arm. In a connective-tissue study, those three processes are not independent: vascular supply, cell migration, and chondrocyte function all affect the repair outcome. A single vial with all three removes the need to prepare three stocks and fixes a ratio that the laboratory can test as a single condition.

The evidence base is uneven. BPC-157 has a large rodent literature, TB-500 has a smaller but well-cited cardiac and wound literature, and Cartalax has a much thinner independent literature. Most of what is published on Cartalax comes from the bioregulator research tradition, where short peptides are proposed to interact with DNA regulatory sequences and modulate transcription. That is a hypothesis, not a settled mechanism. A researcher should treat the Cartalax arm as exploratory and design the study so that the BPC-157 and TB-500 contributions can be separated from it.

There is also a practical reason for caution. Cartalax is a tetrapeptide with a molecular weight of 430.4 Da. It is small, inexpensive to synthesise, and difficult to quantify in a mixture because it has no aromatic residue. In a blend dominated by two larger peptides, the Cartalax peak can sit near the solvent front or co-elute with buffer components. A certificate that reports one purity figure for the blend may say nothing useful about the Cartalax content.

## What the Batch Record Should Show

A three-component blend with a 10-fold mass range is an analytical stress test. BPC-157 is 1419.5 Da, TB-500 is about 4963 Da, and Cartalax is 430.4 Da. A single reversed-phase method cannot be optimal for all three, and a single purity number cannot describe the mixture.

**Cartalax identity.** The tetrapeptide sequence is Ala-Glu-Asp-Pro. The certificate should print it and show a mass near 430.4 Da. Because the peptide is small and has no aromatic residue, UV detection at 280 nm will not see it. Detection at 214 nm works, but the peak may be close to the solvent front. Ask for an LC-MS confirmation or an amino acid analysis, not just a peak-area percentage.

**Cartalax content.** This is the number most likely to be missing. A four-residue peptide is inexpensive, and a blend can be under-filled on the smallest component without the total mass looking wrong. Ask for a Cartalax-specific assay with a calibration curve. If the supplier cannot provide one, the blend is effectively a two-component product with a label claim for the third.

**BPC-157 and TB-500 identity.** BPC-157: mass near 1419.5 Da and the 15-residue sequence. TB-500: deconvoluted mass near 4963 Da, N-terminal acetylation state, and methionine oxidation level. These are the same checks as the single-compound pages, but in a blend the mass spectrum contains ions from all three components. Ask for component-specific chromatograms or an LC-MS method that reports each mass window.

**Blend uniformity.** A powder blend with three particle sizes and three densities can segregate during filling. The lyophilization step may reduce that risk, but it does not eliminate it. A content-uniformity study should sample multiple vials across the batch and assay all three components. A single vial analysed in triplicate is not a uniformity study.

**Ratio and peptide content.** The label's "10 + 20 mg" needs a per-component breakdown. The certificate should state whether the masses are peptide content or salt content, and give water content by Karl Fischer. If Cartalax is supplied as a free base or an acetate, the peptide content per milligram differs. A researcher preparing a nominal concentration needs the peptide mass, not the fill weight.

**Counterion profile.** BPC-157 and TB-500 are commonly acetates; Cartalax may be a free base or an acetate. A blend lyophilized from a mixed solution can have a mixed counterion profile. The certificate should say what was measured, not just "acetate".

**Endotoxin and sterility.** For cell-based cartilage work, endotoxin is a relevant release test. Sterility is separate. Confirm which tests were run on the final blend rather than assuming the powder is sterile.

## Sourcing & Verification

The first question is the composition table. Ask for the mass of each component, the sequence of each component, and the assay method for each component. A supplier who can provide that table is selling a defined blend. A supplier who provides only a total mass is selling a convenience format with an unknown ratio.

The second question is the Cartalax definition. "Cartalax" is used for the AEDP tetrapeptide in this catalogue, but the name is not a pharmacopoeial standard. Ask for the sequence in writing. If the supplier uses a different sequence under the same name, the research literature may not apply.

The third question is the TB-500 form. The blend name says TB-500, which in this catalogue means the full 43-residue Thymosin Beta-4 Acetate. Confirm that the certificate's mass is near 4963 Da and not the mass of a short fragment. A fragment would change the blend's molar ratio and its research interpretation. The [TB-500 naming comparison](/blog/tb-500-vs-thymosin-beta-4-what-you-re-actually-getting/) explains why this check matters.

The fourth question is uniformity. Ask for the sampling plan: how many vials, how many positions per vial, and what acceptance criteria were used. A blend with a 10-fold mass range is more prone to segregation than a two-component blend of similar-sized peptides. If the supplier has no uniformity data, the ratio is a fill-solution claim.

The fifth question is the Cartalax assay. Ask whether it was quantified by LC-MS, amino acid analysis, or UV peak area. The first two are more informative. A UV peak-area percentage at 214 nm can be distorted by the solvent front and by the other peptides. If the supplier cannot answer, the Cartalax content is unverified.

The sixth question is the evidence base for the cartilage claim. Cartalax is proposed to act at the gene-regulatory level, but the independent literature is limited. Ask what preclinical data the supplier relies on. A supplier who cites a specific paper is easier to evaluate than one who repeats a mechanism paragraph. ## Handling & Storage

Store the lyophilized blend at −20°C, desiccated, protected from light. Let the vial warm to room temperature before opening. The cake may contain bulking agents and salts; do not assume the fill weight is peptide.

Reconstitute gently. Add the solvent down the vial wall and swirl. Cartalax is small and highly soluble; BPC-157 dissolves readily in water or dilute acid; TB-500 benefits from slow addition and avoids shear. If one component is slow, give it time rather than adding more solvent. Inspect the solution against a light before filtering.

pH is a compromise. BPC-157 is more stable away from strongly basic conditions; TB-500's methionine oxidises faster at neutral to slightly alkaline pH; Cartalax's charge profile depends on its glutamate and aspartate residues. Choose the buffer that matches the assay and prepare the working solution fresh when possible. If the assay requires a pH outside the stable range for one component, run a short stability check rather than assuming all three behave the same.

Aliquot before freezing. Repeated freeze-thaw events can change the ratio if one component adsorbs or degrades faster. Divide the solution into single-use portions, freeze at −20°C or below, and thaw once. Use low-binding tubes for dilute solutions; the tetrapeptide can adsorb to plastic at low concentrations, and the effect may differ from the larger peptides.

Filter sterilization, if needed, should be validated for recovery of all three components. A 0.22 µm filter can retain aggregates but may also adsorb a fraction of the peptide. Check recovery with a UV assay or, for Cartalax, an LC-MS assay before trusting the nominal concentration.
