## Why This Compound

BPC-157 takes its name from a partial sequence found in a protein isolated from human gastric juice. The synthetic peptide corresponds to 15 amino acids — Gly-Glu-Pro-Pro-Pro-Gly-Lys-Pro-Ala-Asp-Asp-Ala-Gly-Leu-Val — and is also catalogued as PL 14736 or stable gastric pentadecapeptide. Its research history is unusual for a peptide of this size: it has been studied continuously since the early 1990s, largely by a small group of laboratories in Zagreb, and the literature is dense with rodent experiments across several organ systems.

The compound's original research hook was acid stability. Most peptides are degraded quickly in gastric fluid; BPC-157 was reported to remain intact under conditions that destroy comparable peptides, which made it a candidate tool for gastrointestinal studies. That property still shapes how laboratories use it. A researcher can add it to an assay at a defined point without worrying that the peptide will disappear before the first measurement, and that predictability is part of why the compound appears in so many screening panels.

Three research themes recur. The first is cytoprotection in the gut: gastric lesion models, NSAID injury models, and intestinal permeability assays. The second is connective tissue: transected Achilles tendon, ligament injury, and tendon-to-bone attachment models in rats. The third is vascular and central nervous system work, including ischemia-reperfusion and dopaminergic lesion models. The proposed mechanisms usually involve nitric oxide signalling, VEGFR-2-dependent angiogenesis, EGR-1 induction, and changes in growth factor expression. None of these mechanisms is settled, and the studies are almost entirely preclinical.

That distinction matters for sourcing. A supplier page can describe a peptide as "tissue repair research material" and still ship a vial that is 80% peptide and 20% counterion and water. The biology literature does not tell you which vial you have. Only the batch record does.

## What the Batch Record Should Show

A BPC-157 certificate of analysis should let an independent analyst reconstruct what was made. In practice, most certificates do not. The fields below are the ones worth reading closely.

**Identity by mass.** The theoretical monoisotopic mass of the free base is about 1418.70 Da, and the singly protonated ion appears near m/z 1419.71. A certificate that lists "molecular weight 1419.5" without a spectrum is quoting the catalogue value, not confirming the batch. Ask for the mass spectrum with charge states annotated, or a peptide map if the laboratory uses LC-MS/MS. The difference between a real confirmation and a copied number is visible in the isotope envelope.

**Purity by HPLC with method detail.** "≥98%" means little without the column, mobile phase, gradient, wavelength, and integration method. Peptide purity is usually measured at 214 or 220 nm, where the peptide bond absorbs. Area normalization at that wavelength will report the peptide peak as a percentage of UV-absorbing material; it will not see water, counterions, or salts. A certificate that reports 99% by area normalization and 85% peptide content by mass is internally consistent and more useful than one that reports only the first number.

**Counterion and residual solvents.** BPC-157 is commonly supplied as the acetate salt, but trifluoroacetate (TFA) is also possible, and the counterion changes the mass of material in the vial. TFA is a common HPLC ion-pairing reagent and can remain after purification; acetate is introduced deliberately in some processes. The certificate should name the counterion and, ideally, give residual TFA or acetate content. If the certificate is silent, the "10 mg" on the label may refer to total vial content rather than peptide mass.

**Peptide content and fill.** A 10 mg vial typically contains peptide plus counterion plus residual water. Karl Fischer water content and a nitrogen or amino acid analysis-based peptide content assay are the two measurements that convert vial mass into peptide mass. Without them, a researcher preparing a nominal concentration may be off by a meaningful margin. This is one of the most common sources of irreproducibility between laboratories.

**Endotoxin and bioburden, when the assay needs them.** Endotoxin testing by LAL is relevant for cell-based work and for any preparation that contacts primary cells. It is not a universal release test for every catalogue peptide, and a certificate that omits it is not necessarily deficient — but the omission should be known before the assay is designed. The same applies to sterility: a lyophilized powder is not sterile by default.

**Batch traceability.** The certificate should carry a batch number that matches the vial label, a synthesis date, a retest or expiry date, and the manufacturing site. A certificate that could apply to any batch of BPC-157 is not a batch record. Ask whether the chromatogram raw file is available; many suppliers will share the PDF but not the raw data, and the PDF alone can be edited.

## Sourcing & Verification

The questions below are specific to BPC-157 and are worth asking before a first order. They are also useful for comparing quotes that look identical on a price sheet.

- Does the certificate show the full 15-residue sequence, or only the trade name? A supplier who cannot print the sequence is probably reselling.
- Is the reported mass the free base or a salt form? The two differ by the counterion mass, and the label may not say which one was weighed.
- Is purity reported by area normalization or against an external standard? The first is common; the second is more informative.
- What is the peptide content in mg per vial, and how was it measured?
- Is residual TFA reported? TFA is not toxicologically irrelevant in cell culture at high concentrations, and it is a useful marker of purification quality.
- Can the supplier provide the chromatogram and mass spectrum as separate files, not a combined PDF?
- Is the supplier the actual manufacturer, and can it name the synthesis site? A trading company can still supply a good batch, but it cannot answer process questions.
- How was the batch stored before shipment, and is there a cold-chain record for the international leg?

A pattern worth recognising: certificates that list every possible test but show no values, and certificates that use the same peak table for 5 mg, 10 mg, and 30 mg vials. Both suggest the document was generated from a template. ## Handling & Storage

Lyophilized BPC-157 is stable for long periods at −20°C when kept dry and protected from light. Three handling points matter more than the storage temperature on the label.

First, let the vial equilibrate to room temperature before opening. A cold vial attracts condensation, and moisture on the lyophilized cake accelerates degradation. Wipe the outside of the vial, wait, then open in a low-humidity environment.

Second, choose the reconstitution solvent for the assay, not by habit. Water, phosphate-buffered saline, and dilute acetic acid give different pH and ionic conditions, and BPC-157 solubility depends on them. Add the solvent gently down the wall of the vial; vortexing creates foam and can denature peptide at the air-liquid interface. If the solution is cloudy, let it stand rather than adding more solvent immediately. The [reconstitution guide](/blog/how-to-reconstitute-bpc-157/) covers the mechanics, and the [solubility troubleshooting note](/blog/peptide-solubility-troubleshooting/) covers the common failure modes.

Third, aliquot before freezing. Aqueous BPC-157 kept at 2-8°C is a short-term stock; repeated freeze-thaw events promote aggregation and adsorption to plastic. Divide the reconstituted solution into single-use portions, freeze at −20°C or below, and avoid refreezing. For long-term storage of the powder, use a desiccator and a freezer without a frost-free defrost. The [long-term storage protocol](/blog/long-term-storage-of-bpc-157-a-professional-laboratory-protocol-for-2026/) has a more detailed schedule.

All handling described here is bench-scale laboratory work. The compound is supplied for research use only and is not intended for administration to humans or animals.

**TB-500.** The usual comparison. TB-500 is a 43-amino-acid polypeptide studied for actin sequestration and cell migration; BPC-157 is a 15-mer studied more often in local gut and tendon models. The mechanistic overlap is real but often overstated: both appear in tissue-repair literature, and both are frequently combined in one study design. A researcher choosing between them should start from the assay endpoint, not from the blend marketing. The [direct comparison](/blog/bpc-157-vs-tb-500/) sets out the differences, and the [TB-500 catalogue page](/catalog/tb-500/) lists its formats.

**BPC-157 arginine salt.** Same sequence, different counterion. The arginine salt is more soluble in some buffers, while the acetate form is the more common catalogue material. Neither is "better" in the abstract; the choice depends on the buffer and the detection method. If a supplier cannot tell you which form you are buying, that is a documentation problem rather than a scientific one.

It is sometimes studied alongside BPC-157 in gut models, but the two are not interchangeable: KPV is an α-MSH fragment, and its proposed mechanism is receptor-mediated rather than growth-factor-mediated. ## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is BPC-157 supplied as?**

Lyophilized powder in sealed vials, typically 5 mg, 10 mg, 20 mg, or 30 mg per vial, in 10-vial packs. The catalogue lists the available formats; the batch certificate should state the peptide content rather than only the fill weight.

**Is BPC-157 the same as PL 14736?**

Yes. PL 14736 is a synonym used in the older literature and in some regulatory filings. The sequence is the same 15-residue pentadecapeptide. A supplier using only the synonym is not necessarily selling a different compound, but the certificate should still print the sequence.

**Is the product acetate or trifluoroacetate?**

It varies by batch and by manufacturer. Acetate is more common in catalogue material; TFA can remain from HPLC purification. Ask for the counterion on the certificate and for residual TFA if your assay is sensitive to it.

**How should I store the lyophilized powder?**

At −20°C, desiccated, protected from light. Let the vial reach room temperature before opening to avoid condensation. Under those conditions the powder is stable for extended periods; the exact retest date should come from the batch record, not from a generic web page.

**How do I confirm identity?**

Compare the observed mass with the theoretical values: about 1418.70 Da for the free base and m/z 1419.71 for the singly charged ion. A certificate that shows only "MW 1419.5" has not confirmed the batch. HPLC retention alone is not identity confirmation.

**Can the powder be autoclaved or heat-treated?**

No. Autoclaving exposes the peptide to steam and heat that can hydrolyse the sequence and promote aggregation. If a sterile solution is required, filter through a 0.22 µm membrane and verify that the peptide does not adsorb to the filter material.

**What documentation should come with an order?**

A batch-specific certificate of analysis with HPLC method and chromatogram, mass spectrum, counterion and water content, peptide content, and a retest date. Endotoxin and sterility data are separate tests; confirm whether they were run rather than assuming they were.

**Why do two suppliers quote very different prices for the same size?**

Synthesis scale, purification method, counterion, fill mass, and documentation all affect cost. A low price can reflect a smaller peptide content per vial, a cruder purification, or a trading layer with no batch records. Compare price per milligram of peptide, not price per vial.
