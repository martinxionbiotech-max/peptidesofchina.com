## Why This Compound

The bioregulator hypothesis holds that short peptides corresponding to segments of larger tissue proteins can enter cells, reach the nucleus, and modulate transcription in a tissue-specific way. In the version developed by Khavinson and colleagues, the peptides are four to seven residues long, derived from extracts of specific organs, and proposed to bind directly to DNA or to chromatin-associated proteins. The proposed effect is a normalisation of gene expression in the tissue of origin rather than a pharmacological agonism or antagonism at a receptor.

Bronchogen fits that template as the bronchial member. The published work describes effects on pulmonary epithelial cells, on bronchial mucosa, and on markers that the authors associate with respiratory ageing. The readouts are typically transcriptional: expression of genes involved in epithelial barrier function, antioxidant defence, and extracellular matrix turnover. Some studies use cultured cells; others use aged rodents. The sample sizes are small by current standards, and the groups are often single-laboratory.

The mechanistic claim is the part that attracts scepticism, and the scepticism is reasonable. A tetrapeptide is small enough to be cleared by plasma proteases within minutes, and its ability to reach the nucleus intact is not established for every member of the series. The counter-argument is that the observed effects are consistent across several peptides and several tissues, and that direct DNA binding of short peptides has been reported in biophysical systems. Both positions are represented in the literature. A laboratory using bronchogen as a tool should treat the mechanism as a hypothesis under test, not as a settled fact.

What makes the compound useful anyway is its simplicity. AEDL has no aromatic residues, no cysteine, and no methionine. It cannot form a disulfide, it is not prone to oxidation, and its synthesis is straightforward. The analytical problem is therefore not degradation but identity: a four-residue peptide has many near-neighbours, and a supplier that ships AED instead of AEDL, or a scrambled sequence, can produce a chromatogram that looks acceptable. Sequence confirmation by tandem mass spectrometry is the control that matters.

There is a second research use that does not depend on the bioregulator hypothesis. Short peptides are used as model compounds in stability studies, in permeability assays, and in method development for LC-MS. AEDL's lack of chromophores makes it a useful test case for low-wavelength detection and for derivatisation chemistry. A laboratory can use the same vial for both purposes, provided it characterises the batch once and records the result.

## What the Batch Record Should Show

Identity comes first, and for a tetrapeptide the standard is tandem mass spectrometry with a stated fragmentation pattern. A single mass peak at the expected value is necessary but not sufficient: isobaric sequences can share a mass. The certificate should show the precursor mass, the main fragment ions, and the assignment of the b- and y-series that fixes the order. If the supplier reports only a molecular weight, the sequence order is unverified.

Purity by reversed-phase HPLC should be reported with the method. AEDL has no aromatic side chains, so detection at 214 nm relies on the peptide bond and the signal is weaker than for a tryptophan-containing peptide. That makes the blank and the integration threshold more important, not less. Ask for the column, gradient, flow, wavelength, and the integration criteria, and look at the chromatogram rather than the number.

Counterion and peptide content are the fields most often missing. AEDL is acidic overall, with two carboxylates in the Glu and Asp side chains, and it is usually supplied as the acetate or the sodium salt. The counterion changes the mass of the powder without changing the sequence. Water content by Karl Fischer and a peptide-content assay turn fill mass into peptide mass, and on a 20 mg fill a few percent of water is a few hundred micrograms of peptide.

Residual solvents and trifluoroacetic acid belong on the record if the peptide was purified by preparative HPLC. TFA binds to basic residues and can persist through lyophilisation; it is detectable by ion chromatography or by fluorine NMR. For a tetrapeptide, the amount is small, but it can affect cell assays at higher concentrations.

Endotoxin and sterility matter for any cell-based work. A lyophilized powder is not sterile by default, and the LAL result should be reported with its units and limit. The [endotoxin note](/blog/what-are-endotoxins-why-they-matter/) explains why a low endotoxin figure can matter more than an extra point of purity for primary cells.

Batch linkage closes the record. The vial number and the certificate number must match, and the document should carry synthesis date, purification method, fill date, and retest date. A certificate that could describe any lot is a template. The [CoA guide](/blog/how-to-read-a-peptide-coa-a-researchers-guide-to-hplc-and-ms-reports/) walks through the fields in order, and the [purity note](/blog/why-purity-and-concentration-tests-are-not-enough/) explains what area normalisation does not measure.

## Sourcing & Verification

The first question is the sequence, written out. A supplier who answers "bronchogen" has given a brand name, not an identity. Ask for the four residues in order and the observed mass, and compare the two. For AEDL, the PubChem record lists C₁₈H₃₀N₄O₉ and 446.5 g/mol, which gives a reference point for the mass check.

The second question is the synthesis route. A four-residue peptide can be made by solid-phase synthesis at small scale, and the crude can be purified by preparative HPLC. Ask about the resin, the coupling chemistry, and the purification step. A supplier who cannot describe any of these is a trader, and a trader's certificate is a copy of someone else's document.

The third question is the counterion. AEDL is often supplied as the acetate salt; some lots are the sodium salt. The two have different masses and different hygroscopicity. Ask which one is in the vial, and ask whether the certificate's mass refers to the salt or the free peptide. The [counterion note](/blog/role-of-counterions-in-peptide-stability/) explains the practical consequences.

The fourth question is the batch size and the fill. A 20 mg fill in a 10-vial pack is a small order, and small fills are less forgiving of weighing error. Ask for the fill range and the peptide content per vial. A supplier who reports only the fill mass has not told you how much peptide you are buying.

The fifth question is documentation provenance. The [manufacturer verification protocol](/blog/china-peptide-manufacturer-verification-a-2026-b2b-sourcing-protocol/) sets out the entity checks: business licence, production address, reactor scale, and whether the certificate issuer is the manufacturer. For short peptides, the gap between manufacturer and reseller is wide, and the certificate is where it shows.

Finally, ask for a pilot vial. Reconstitute it, run HPLC and MS in-house, and compare with the certificate. The [quality-testing overview](/peptide-quality-testing/) lists the tests and what each can establish.

## Handling & Storage

Store the lyophilized powder at −20 °C, desiccated, protected from light. Let the vial reach room temperature before opening; condensation on a cold cake is a direct route to hydrolysis and to a sticky powder that is hard to weigh.

Reconstitute with water or a buffer that matches the assay. AEDL is acidic and dissolves readily at neutral pH; a phosphate buffer at pH 7.2 is a reasonable starting point. Add the solvent gently, swirl, and avoid vortexing. If the solution is cloudy, check the concentration and the pH before assuming contamination, and consult the [solubility note](/blog/peptide-solubility-troubleshooting/).

Use polypropylene tubes for dilute stocks. Short acidic peptides adsorb less than cationic peptides, but the loss at low micromolar concentrations is still measurable, and a recovery check is cheaper than repeating an experiment. For cell work, filter through a 0.22 µm membrane and confirm sterility and endotoxin before use.

Aliquot before freezing. A tetrapeptide without cysteine or methionine is chemically stable, but repeated freeze-thaw still causes aggregation and concentration changes. Reconstituted solutions should be kept at 2–8 °C and used within the working window established for the assay, not one copied from another peptide. The [reconstitution guide](/blog/peptide-lyophilization-reconstitution-guide-for-researchers/) covers solvent choice and container effects.

The [handling hub](/peptide-handling-protocols/) covers general practice. The steps above are bench procedures for a research reagent, not instructions for preparing anything for human or animal use.

## How It Compares

**Chonluten (Glu–Asp–Leu).** The C-terminal tripeptide of bronchogen's sequence, sold as a separate bioregulator with a pulmonary and gerontology literature of its own. The relationship is structural, not pharmacological: removing the N-terminal alanine changes the size, the charge, and possibly the uptake. A study that compares the two is asking a real question; a buyer who substitutes one for the other is assuming an answer.

**Epithalon (Ala–Glu–Asp–Gly).** The best-known member of the series, with a pineal and telomerase literature. It shares the first three residues with bronchogen and differs at the fourth. That single substitution is the basis of the tissue-specific claim, and it is also the reason the two should not be used interchangeably.

**Cortagen (Ala–Glu–Asp–Gly).** Another tetrapeptide in the same family, associated with cortical tissue. The sequence is identical to epithalon's, which is a useful reminder that the family's naming is historical rather than systematic. Check sequences, not labels.

**Cartalax (Ala–Glu–Asp–Pro).** The cartilage member. Same backbone length, different fourth residue and different tissue literature. It is a comparator for anyone testing whether the fourth position carries the specificity.

**Thymalin.** A polypeptide fraction from thymus rather than a single tetrapeptide. It is not a like-for-like comparator, and its certificate will look different: a molecular weight distribution rather than a single mass.
