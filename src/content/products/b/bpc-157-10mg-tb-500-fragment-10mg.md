## Why This Compound

BPC-157 and the TB-500 fragment are both small peptides, but they come from different research traditions. BPC-157 is a 15-residue pentadecapeptide derived from a gastric juice protein, studied in gastrointestinal cytoprotection, angiogenesis, and tendon/ligament models. The TB-500 fragment is a short sequence from the actin-binding domain of Thymosin Beta-4, studied for cell migration and cytoskeletal reorganisation. The full Thymosin Beta-4 protein is 43 residues; the fragment keeps the region that contacts actin and removes the rest.

The case for using the fragment rather than the full protein is practical. A short peptide is easier to synthesise, easier to purify, and easier to characterise by mass spectrometry. It also avoids the methionine at position 6 of the full sequence, which is the main oxidation site in Thymosin Beta-4. For an assay that only needs the actin-binding motif, the fragment removes a variable without removing the activity of interest. For an assay that depends on the full protein's structure, its N-terminal Ac-SDKP sequence, or its behaviour in a cardiac model, the fragment is not a substitute.

The case for combining the fragment with BPC-157 is less established. The two are studied in overlapping tissue-repair contexts, and a laboratory may want both in one well. But the published combination data is thin, and most of it uses the full TB-500 sequence rather than the fragment. A researcher should treat this blend as a new condition, not as a replication of a BPC-157/TB-500 study. That is not a reason to avoid it; it is a reason to include single-compound controls.

There is a chemical detail that matters more than the marketing. The blend is 10 mg + 10 mg by mass. BPC-157 has a molecular weight of 1419.5 Da; the fragment is a short peptide with a mass well below that. Equal masses therefore do not mean equal molar amounts. On a molar basis, 10 mg of the fragment is a larger quantity than 10 mg of BPC-157, by a factor that depends on the fragment's exact length and whether its N-terminus is acetylated. If a study is designed around equimolar concentrations, the blend's fixed mass ratio is a variable, not a constant.

## What the Batch Record Should Show

A two-component blend is easier to document than a three-component one, but it still needs component-level evidence. The fragment adds a specific risk: because it is short, a generic purity method can hide deletion sequences that differ by a single amino acid.

**Fragment identity.** The certificate should print the fragment's sequence and its observed mass. If the fragment is acetylated, the mass will be about 42 Da higher than the free amine form. If it is amidated at the C-terminus, the mass will be about 1 Da lower. These are small differences, but they change the molecule. Ask which form was synthesised and whether the certificate's mass matches it.

**Full-length contamination.** A fragment batch should not contain full-length Thymosin Beta-4. In practice, the risk is not carry-over from the full protein but incomplete coupling during synthesis, which produces shorter deletion sequences. A mass spectrum of the fragment should show a clean main peak with a co-eluting impurity profile that the HPLC can resolve. If the certificate reports only "98%" without a chromatogram, the impurity distribution is unknown.

**BPC-157 identity.** The same checks as a standalone BPC-157 batch: sequence, mass near 1419.5 Da, counterion, water content, peptide content. The fragment does not interfere with the BPC-157 mass if the method is orthogonal, but a single HPLC run may not resolve both. Ask for two chromatograms or an LC-MS method that reports each component separately.

**Blend uniformity.** The two components are both small, but they may crystallise differently during lyophilization. A content-uniformity study across multiple vials is the only evidence that the 1:1 mass ratio holds in the vial, not just in the fill solution. For a two-component blend, this study is simpler than for a three-component blend and should be easier to obtain.

**Ratio and peptide content.** The label says 10 mg + 10 mg. The certificate should state whether those are peptide masses or salt masses, and give the assay for each component. If the fragment is supplied as an acetate salt, the peptide content may be lower than the fill weight. A researcher preparing a nominal 1 mg/mL solution of each component needs the peptide content, not the fill weight.

**Counterion and water.** Both components may carry acetate or trifluoroacetate. The certificate should name the counterion for each and give water content by Karl Fischer. A blend lyophilized from a mixed solution can have a different residual solvent profile than either component alone.

**Endotoxin and sterility.** For cell-based work, endotoxin testing on the final blend is the relevant check. Sterility is a separate claim. Confirm which tests were run rather than assuming a lyophilized powder is sterile.

## Sourcing & Verification

The first question is the fragment's exact definition. "TB-500 Fragment" is used in the market for sequences of different lengths, and the catalogue name says 17-24. Ask for the sequence in writing. A supplier who cannot state the residues is not selling a defined fragment.

The second question is acetylation. The native Thymosin Beta-4 N-terminus is acetylated, and the fragment may or may not be. The mass difference is small enough that a low-resolution spectrum can miss it. Ask for the deconvoluted mass and the expected value for the form being sold.

The third question is whether the blend ratio is mass or molar. The label is mass. If the study needs equimolar concentrations, the researcher has to calculate the molar ratio from the fragment's molecular weight and adjust the working solution. A supplier who understands this will provide the molecular weight on the certificate; a supplier who does not will describe the blend as "1:1" without qualification.

The fourth question is the purity method. A short fragment and a 15-mer can be resolved by reversed-phase HPLC, but the method needs a gradient that separates them from each other and from their deletion sequences. Ask for the chromatograms at 214 nm and, if available, the mass spectrum of each component. The [molecular-weight verification note](/blog/peptide-molecular-weight-verification-steps/) explains how to read the mass evidence.

The fifth question is the source of the fragment. A supplier that synthesises the fragment in-house can describe the coupling and purification steps. A supplier that buys a finished fragment and blends it is a secondary manufacturer; that can be valid, but the batch record has to include the incoming certificate. Ask for the chain of custody.

The sixth question is the counterion. The [counterion guide](/blog/role-of-counterions-in-peptide-stability/) explains why two batches with the same sequence can differ in solubility and stability. For a blend, the counterion profile is a mixture, and the certificate should say so.

## Handling & Storage

Store the lyophilized blend at −20°C, desiccated, protected from light. Let the vial reach room temperature before opening. The cake may contain bulking agents, so do not assume the entire fill weight is peptide.

Reconstitute gently. Add the solvent down the vial wall and swirl; avoid vortexing. The fragment is small and usually dissolves quickly; BPC-157 also dissolves readily in water or dilute acid. If one component is slower, give it time rather than adding more solvent. A cloudy solution may indicate incomplete dissolution or aggregation; inspect it against a light before filtering.

pH is a compromise. BPC-157 is more stable away from strongly basic conditions; the fragment's charge profile depends on its lysine and glutamate residues. Choose the buffer that matches the assay and prepare the working solution fresh when possible. If the assay requires a pH outside the stable range for one component, run a short stability check rather than assuming both behave the same.

Aliquot before freezing. Repeated freeze-thaw events can change the ratio if one component adsorbs to the vial or degrades faster. Divide the solution into single-use portions, freeze at −20°C or below, and thaw once. Use low-binding tubes for dilute solutions; small peptides can adsorb to plastic at low concentrations, and the effect may differ between the two components.

Filter sterilization, if needed, should be validated for recovery of both peptides. A 0.22 µm filter can retain aggregates but may also adsorb a fraction of the peptide; check recovery with a UV assay before trusting the nominal concentration.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is TB-500 Fragment 17-24?**

It is a short synthetic peptide corresponding to residues 17-24 of Thymosin Beta-4, the region associated with actin binding. It is not the full 43-residue protein. The exact sequence and acetylation state should be printed on the batch certificate.

**How does the fragment differ from TB-500?**

TB-500 in this catalogue is the full Thymosin Beta-4 Acetate sequence. The fragment is a much shorter peptide from the actin-binding region. The fragment is easier to synthesise and purify, but it lacks the rest of the protein, including the N-terminal Ac-SDKP region.

**Is the 10 mg + 10 mg ratio equimolar?**

No. The ratio is by mass. Because BPC-157 is 1419.5 Da and the fragment is smaller, 10 mg of the fragment is a larger number of moles than 10 mg of BPC-157. If the study needs equimolar conditions, calculate the molar ratio from the fragment's molecular weight and adjust the working solution.

**What purity does the blend have?**

The catalogue specification is ≥98%. That figure should be supported by a chromatogram for each component, not a single aggregate value. Ask for the method and the impurity profile.

**How should the blend be stored?**

At −20°C, desiccated, protected from light. Let the vial warm to room temperature before opening. Reconstituted solution should be kept at 2-8°C for short-term use or aliquoted and frozen for single use.

**Does the blend contain full-length TB-500?**

The label says TB-500 Fragment, not TB-500. A certificate that lists the fragment sequence and mass is the evidence. If the certificate only says "TB-500" without a sequence, ask for clarification before assuming which form is present.

**Can I use the blend in a cell-migration assay?**

The fragment is studied in cell-migration models, and BPC-157 is studied in angiogenesis and repair models. The combination may be appropriate, but the assay needs single-compound controls to attribute the result. Check that the buffer and pH are compatible with both peptides.

**Why is the fragment's CAS number not listed?**

The catalogue page does not print a CAS number for the fragment, and the batch certificate should. CAS numbers for peptide fragments are sometimes not assigned or are supplier-specific. The sequence and mass are the more useful identity checks.
