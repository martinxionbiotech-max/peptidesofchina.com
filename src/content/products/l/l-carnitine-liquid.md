L-Carnitine liquid is the same zwitterionic compound sold in the powder listing, supplied as a 500 mg/mL solution in 10 mL vials. The format exists for laboratories that pipette rather than weigh: a solution can be transferred volumetrically, it generates no dust, and it dissolves instantly into medium. The price of that convenience is a different set of analytical questions.

A powder specification is mostly about identity, water content, and enantiomeric purity. A concentrated solution adds density, fill volume, pH, osmolarity, sterility, and container compatibility to the list. At 500 mg/mL, a small error in density or fill volume becomes a large error in the amount of carnitine a laboratory believes it has added. This page covers the chemistry, the batch record a solution should carry, and the handling points that decide whether a stock remains usable after the first week.

## Why This Compound

Carnitine's role is narrow and well defined: it carries long-chain fatty acids across the inner mitochondrial membrane. The cytosolic acyl-CoA is converted to acylcarnitine by carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1, translocated by the carnitine-acylcarnitine translocase, and reconverted by carnitine palmitoyltransferase 2 inside the matrix. Bremer's 1983 review in *Physiological Reviews* remains the standard description, and Rebouche's 1992 review in *The FASEB Journal* covers function and requirements across the life cycle.

The compound is a zwitterion. The trimethylammonium group carries a permanent positive charge, and the carboxylate deprotonates near pH 3.8. At neutral pH the molecule is electrically neutral overall but strongly polar, which is why it dissolves readily in water and poorly in non-polar solvents, and why reversed-phase chromatography needs an ion-pairing reagent to retain it.

A 500 mg/mL solution is roughly 3.1 M. That concentration has consequences. Viscosity rises, density exceeds 1 g/mL, and the solution has a measurable osmotic pressure. When a laboratory pipettes 100 µL of that stock into a litre of medium, the molarity of the final solution depends on the density of the stock and on the fill accuracy of the vial, not just on the label. For most qualitative work the difference is irrelevant; for dose-response experiments it is the difference between a clean curve and a scattered one.

Why supply a liquid at all? Three reasons recur. First, carnitine powder is hygroscopic, and weighing it accurately in small amounts is slow. Second, some automated platforms cannot handle powder. Third, a sealed liquid vial reduces the chance of bench contamination during preparation. The format is a workflow choice, not a pharmacological one: the molecule is identical, and the research context — carnitine shuttle capacity, acylcarnitine profiling, mitochondrial substrate oxidation — is the same.

What changes is stability. Carnitine itself is not an ester and does not hydrolyse in water, so the active molecule is reasonably robust in solution. The risks are elsewhere: microbial growth in a non-sterile container, pH drift, adsorption to surfaces at low concentration, and freeze damage to the container or to the solution's homogeneity. A liquid product is only as good as its container and its cold chain.

## What the Batch Record Should Show

A liquid certificate has to do two jobs: confirm identity and quantify concentration. The second job is where most documents are weakest.

**Identity and assay.** The same three method families used for the powder apply here: LC-MS/MS with an isotope-labelled internal standard, an enzymatic assay coupled to CoA release, or HPLC with a universal detector. For a 500 mg/mL solution, the sample must be diluted by several orders of magnitude before injection. That dilution is itself a source of error, and the certificate should state the dilution factor and the acceptance criterion for the dilution check. A method that starts with a gravimetric dilution and verifies it by density is more trustworthy than one that assumes a volumetric flask is exact.

**Density.** Concentration in mg/mL is only meaningful alongside density in g/mL. A laboratory that pipettes by volume and calculates by mass needs both numbers. Density also reveals whether the fill is homogeneous: a solution that has separated or stratified will show a density that drifts from the specification. Ask for density at a stated temperature, because it changes with temperature and a 2-8°C vial warmed to room temperature will not have the same value.

**pH and osmolarity.** A concentrated carnitine solution sits near neutral pH in most formulations, but the exact value matters for cell work and for stability. Osmolarity is the field most often missing. At 500 mg/mL, the osmotic contribution of carnitine alone is substantial, and any buffer salts or pH adjusters add to it. A laboratory adding a large volume of stock to a small well can change the medium's osmolarity without noticing.

**Sterility, endotoxin, and particulate matter.** A liquid that contacts cells should carry sterility and endotoxin data. Particulate matter is visible by inspection against a light box, and sub-visible particles are measured by light obscuration or membrane microscopy. For research-grade material, the tests may be omitted; the certificate should say so rather than leave the reader to guess.

**Fill volume and container.** The nominal fill is 10 mL. Check whether the certificate states the actual fill and the method used to verify it. Container material matters too: glass leaches alkali over time, and some plastics adsorb carnitine. A supplier who can name the container and the closure is a supplier who has thought about the product as a package rather than as a bulk chemical.

**Batch traceability.** Lot number, fill date, expiry or retest date, and the site where the solution was prepared. A solution that was diluted from a bulk lot has a second layer of traceability: the bulk lot number should appear on the certificate. ## Sourcing & Verification

Liquid carnitine is harder to verify than powder, because the analytical result depends on the dilution step and the container. The questions below are specific to the format.

- Is the concentration verified by assay, or calculated from the weight of powder dissolved? Calculated values are common and weaker.
- Is density reported at a stated temperature, and does the certificate give both mg/mL and g/mL?
- What is the pH, and what buffer or pH adjuster was used?
- Is osmolarity reported? At this concentration it is not a minor detail.
- Is the solution sterile, and by which method — aseptic fill or terminal filtration?
- Are endotoxin and particulate matter results included, or explicitly stated as not tested?
- What is the container material and closure, and is there an extractables statement?
- Does the certificate identify the bulk lot from which the solution was prepared?

The [specification sheet note](/blog/why-peptide-specification-sheets-matter-in-procurement/) explains why a complete specification is more useful than a certificate alone, and the [supplier GMP assessment guide](/blog/how-to-assess-peptide-supplier-gmp-compliance-in-2026/) covers site-level questions. For liquids, the site question is particularly relevant: aseptic filling is a different capability from powder filling, and not every peptide supplier has both.

One practical check that costs nothing: request a photograph of the actual vial label and the certificate side by side. A label that says "500 mg/mL" without a lot number, or a certificate whose lot number does not match, tells you more about the supplier's document control than any questionnaire.

## Handling & Storage

Store the vial at 2-8°C, protected from light, and do not freeze. Freezing a concentrated solution can cause phase separation or precipitation, and repeated freeze-thaw cycles stress the container. If the vial arrives cold, let it stand at room temperature for thirty minutes before opening, then invert it gently several times. Do not shake: foaming is not a problem for carnitine itself, but it makes volumetric sampling unreliable.

Sampling technique matters more than most protocols admit. A cold, concentrated solution is more viscous and less homogeneous than a warm one. Warm to room temperature, invert, and sample immediately. If the laboratory takes 100 µL from the top of a vial that has stood for a week, the concentration may not match the certificate.

Once opened, the vial is a potential microbial culture. Aseptically transfer working aliquots and store them at 2-8°C; do not return unused material to the stock vial. If the solution will contact cells, filter it through a 0.22 µm membrane into a sterile container. Carnitine is small and does not adsorb strongly, but a recovery check is cheap insurance.

Dilution schemes deserve a written record. Because the stock is 3.1 M, a two-step dilution is usually more accurate than a single large jump, and the intermediate should be made fresh. Record the density used, the temperature, and the dilution factor; if a result looks anomalous three weeks later, that record is the only way to find out whether the stock or the assay changed.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is the liquid the same compound as the powder?**

Yes. It is L-carnitine at 500 mg/mL in a sterile liquid formulation. The difference is presentation and the analytical fields that come with it: density, pH, osmolarity, fill volume, and container.

**How should the liquid be stored?**

At 2-8°C, protected from light. Do not freeze. Warm to room temperature and invert gently before sampling, because a cold concentrated solution is more viscous and less homogeneous.

**Can I dilute the stock directly into cell culture medium?**

Yes, but calculate the osmotic contribution first. At 500 mg/mL the stock is about 3.1 M, so even a small volume changes the medium's osmolarity. A two-step dilution into buffer or medium is easier to control than a single large jump.

**How is the concentration verified?**

By assay of the diluted solution — LC-MS/MS with an isotope-labelled standard, an enzymatic assay, or HPLC with a universal detector — together with density measurement. A value calculated only from the weight of dissolved powder is weaker evidence.

**Why does density matter?**

Because concentration in mg/mL and volume in mL are linked by density. A laboratory that pipettes by volume and converts to mass needs the density at the temperature of use. Density also detects stratification or fill errors.

**Is the solution sterile?**

The catalogue describes it as a sterile liquid formulation, and the batch certificate should state the sterilization method and the sterility result. If the certificate is silent, treat the vial as non-sterile until confirmed.

**Can the liquid be frozen for long-term storage?**

No. Freezing a concentrated solution can cause phase separation or precipitation and stresses the container. Keep it at 2-8°C and use working aliquots.

**What happens if the solution turns cloudy?**

Do not use it. Cloudiness suggests microbial growth, precipitation, or container leaching. Discard the vial and open a new one; a cloudy stock invalidates any result that depends on concentration.

## The Author's Take

**Position.** I would not accept a liquid carnitine certificate that reports only "500 mg/mL" without density and assay method. The concentration claim is the product, and a calculated value is not a measurement.

**Reasoning.** A concentrated solution is easy to make and hard to verify. Dissolve 50 g of carnitine in water to 100 mL and the label writes itself; whether the powder was anhydrous, whether the volume was exact, and whether the density was measured are separate questions. The failure modes are quiet: a fill short by 3%, a density assumed at 20°C when the vial is sampled at 8°C, a dilution error that compounds through a dose-response series. None of them produces a visible defect. A supplier who reports density, assay, pH, and osmolarity is telling you the product was tested as a solution rather than as a label.
