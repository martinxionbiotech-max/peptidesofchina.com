Livagen is a four-amino-acid peptide — lysine, glutamic acid, aspartic acid, alanine — sold here as a lyophilized research powder in 20 mg vials. It belongs to the Khavinson tetrapeptide family, a group of short sequences that Russian and Baltic research groups have studied for decades under the broad label of peptide bioregulators. The sequence is short enough to synthesise cheaply and analytically difficult enough to be sold badly, which is the central tension of this page.

Most catalogue copy about Livagen repeats the same phrases: tissue-specific, chromatin-active, geroprotective. Those words carry more narrative than data. What a laboratory actually receives is a tetrapeptide with no chromophore, a low molecular weight, and a strong tendency to be under-characterised by suppliers who rely on a single HPLC peak. The useful questions are different: is the sequence correct, what counterion is present, how much water is in the vial, and what method was used to see a molecule that barely absorbs at 214 nm.

## Why This Compound

The Khavinson series is built on a simple premise: short peptides can enter cells and influence chromatin-associated processes. Livagen is one of the sequences assigned to that programme, and the published work around it is mostly from a small number of laboratories using lymphocyte cultures and chromatin assays. Khavinson and colleagues reported chromatin activation in lymphocytes from older donors, and later work from the same group extended the argument to short-peptide regulation of gene expression more generally.

That literature is worth reading, but it is not a product specification. The papers describe biological experiments with particular peptide preparations, particular donors and particular assays. They do not tell you that any 20 mg vial labelled Livagen will behave the same way. The gap between “a peptide was active in a chromatin assay” and “this batch is suitable for your experiment” is exactly where sourcing quality lives.

For a research lab, Livagen is interesting for three reasons. First, it is a tractable model for studying how very short peptides interact with cells and whether sequence-specific effects survive rigorous controls. Second, it is a useful test case for analytical method development: a tetrapeptide with no aromatic residues forces you to think about detection, counterions and quantification. Third, it sits at the centre of a category — bioregulators — where the commercial language is far ahead of the peer-reviewed evidence. Working with it responsibly means being explicit about which of those three interests you are pursuing.

## What the Batch Record Should Show

Identity for a tetrapeptide should be established by mass spectrometry and, where possible, by MS/MS fragmentation that covers the full sequence. A single nominal mass is weak evidence: many four-residue combinations share the same mass within instrument error. The record should show the observed mass, the calculated mass, the adduct pattern and the fragment ions that assign the order of residues. If the supplier cannot provide fragmentation, the sequence is an assertion rather than a measurement.

Purity is the second pillar, and it is harder than it looks. Livagen has no tryptophan or tyrosine, so 280 nm is useless. The method should run at 214 nm or use charged-aerosol, ELSD or mass detection. The chromatogram should show the main peak, the integration baseline and the threshold used to call impurities. A “≥98%” figure derived from a single wavelength with no orthogonal detection is a number, not a characterisation.

Counterion and water content are the third pillar. Short peptides are often isolated as acetate or trifluoroacetate salts, and the counterion changes the mass balance, the solubility and the pH of the reconstituted solution. Karl Fischer water content matters because a lyophilized cake can be 5-10% water and still look dry. If the certificate reports peptide purity but not counterion and water, the actual peptide content per vial is unknown.

The fourth pillar is the biological-assay footprint. Endotoxin and bioburden should be reported even for a peptide that is not intended for sterile work, because chromatin and cell-culture assays are sensitive to lipopolysaccharide. Residual solvents and synthesis reagents matter for the same reason. A short peptide is cheap to make; a clean short peptide is not. The [characterisation reporting checklist](/blog/peptide-characterization-reporting-checklist/) on this site lists the fields we expect to see.

## Sourcing & Verification

Livagen is a custom-synthesis product, not a commodity with a pharmacopoeial monograph. That single fact explains most of the variation in the market. A supplier who sells it as a stock item is usually buying from a Chinese synthesis house and repackaging; a supplier who commissions it can tell you the synthesis route, the resin, the cleavage cocktail and the purification method. Neither model is automatically better, but the documentation burden is different.

Start with the sequence. Ask for the calculated and observed mass, the MS/MS coverage and the purification gradient. Then ask for the counterion. Livagen is commonly supplied as an acetate salt; if the certificate says “peptide” without naming the salt form, the number is incomplete. Then ask for water content and, if the product is intended for cell work, endotoxin. A supplier who answers all four questions is probably working from a real batch record.

The second verification layer is the physical form. A good lyophilized cake is white to off-white, consistent in appearance and free of visible cracks or melt-back. A collapsed cake or a sticky powder suggests a poor freeze-drying cycle or moisture ingress. Take a photograph of the vial before reconstitution and keep it with the lot record. The [lyophilization cycle guide](/blog/lyophilization-cycle-peptides/) explains what the cake appearance can and cannot tell you.

The third layer is the supplier's own traceability. Ask who synthesised the batch, where it was purified, and whether the same site made the previous lot. If the answer changes between orders, your assay may be measuring a process change rather than a biological effect. The [manufacturer verification protocol](/blog/china-peptide-manufacturer-verification-a-2026-b2b-sourcing-protocol/) sets out the questions to ask; the [COA guide](/blog/how-to-read-a-peptide-coa-a-researchers-guide-to-hplc-and-ms-reports/) explains how to read the answers.

Finally, be sceptical of the category narrative. A supplier who leads with “bioregulator” and cannot produce a mass spectrum is selling a story. A supplier who leads with the analytical package and mentions the category second is easier to audit. The [quality-testing hub](/peptide-quality-testing/) is the right place to start if you are building a qualification file for this class of compound.

## Handling & Storage

Store lyophilized Livagen at −20 °C, protected from moisture and light. The vial should be allowed to equilibrate to room temperature before opening to prevent condensation on the cake. Use a clean, dry instrument to weigh or transfer the powder; static is a real problem with fine peptide powders, and a charged spatula can scatter material that you cannot recover.

For reconstitution, start with water or a neutral buffer and check solubility before adding salts. Short peptides can be surprisingly slow to dissolve; swirl rather than vortex, and avoid heating. If the solution is hazy, centrifuge at low speed and inspect the pellet under a light source. Do not filter through a membrane that adsorbs peptides unless you have validated recovery. The [reconstitution guide](/blog/peptide-lyophilization-reconstitution-guide-for-researchers/) covers the practical steps.

Aliquot the reconstituted solution into low-binding tubes and freeze only if the assay tolerates it. Repeated freeze-thaw is the most common cause of a working stock that stops performing. Record the concentration, the buffer, the date and the operator. For cell-based work, prepare fresh dilutions from a single stock rather than serially diluting in the culture medium, because serum proteins and plastic surfaces compete for the peptide.

Chemical compatibility is the last consideration. Avoid strong oxidants, reducing agents and high pH unless the experiment requires them. Livagen has no cysteine, so disulfide chemistry is not a concern, but the lysine side chain can react with amine-reactive crosslinkers. If you are labelling the peptide, check the labelling efficiency rather than assuming it.

## How It Compares

The nearest comparator is [Epithalon](/catalog/epithalon/), the alanine-glutamic-aspartic-glycine tetrapeptide that has a much larger commercial footprint and a similarly thin mechanistic literature. [Ovagen](/catalog/ovagen/) is another four-residue sequence in the same family, with a different nominal tissue assignment. [Cardiogen](/catalog/cardiogen/) and [Vesugen](/catalog/vesugen/) complete the set that buyers most often compare. Chemically, they are all short, hydrophilic, non-aromatic peptides; analytically, they present the same detection problem. The differences that matter are sequence, counterion and the quality of the batch record, not the tissue name in the marketing copy.

Against a longer peptide such as [BPC-157](/catalog/bpc-157/), Livagen is cheaper per gram and much easier to synthesise, but it is also harder to characterise by UV. Against a cosmetic tetrapeptide such as [Matrixyl](/catalog/matrixyl/), the comparison is instructive: both are short sequences, but Matrixyl carries a lipophilic palmitoyl group that changes solubility and assay design. The lesson is that “short peptide” is not a category with a single handling protocol.

If your question is mechanistic, compare Livagen with a scrambled sequence of the same composition. That control is more valuable than any catalogue comparison, because it tests whether the effect is sequence-specific. If your question is sourcing, compare suppliers on the analytical package rather than on the price per vial.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is Livagen's sequence?**

The catalogue sequence is Lys-Glu-Asp-Ala, often abbreviated KEDA. It is a four-residue synthetic peptide supplied as a lyophilized powder. The sequence should be confirmed by mass spectrometry and MS/MS on the actual batch, not assumed from the label.

**Why is Livagen hard to analyse by HPLC?**

It has no aromatic residues, so it barely absorbs at 280 nm. Useful methods run at 214 nm or use mass, ELSD or charged-aerosol detection. A purity claim based only on a 280 nm chromatogram is not meaningful for this peptide.

**Is Livagen a bioregulator?**

“Bioregulator” is a category label used for a family of short peptides, not a pharmacopoeial classification. The label describes a research tradition, not a verified mechanism. The analytical and biological evidence should be evaluated compound by compound.

**What counterion is Livagen supplied as?**

Most short peptides in this family are supplied as acetate salts, but the certificate should state the counterion explicitly. If it does not, ask. The counterion affects mass balance, solubility and the pH of the reconstituted solution.

**Can I use Livagen in cell culture?**

Only if the batch is documented for that use. Ask for endotoxin and bioburden results, and run a vehicle control at the same counterion concentration. A peptide that is pure by HPLC can still carry enough lipopolysaccharide to confound a chromatin or cytokine assay.

**How should I store the powder?**

At −20 °C, dry and protected from light. Let the vial warm to room temperature before opening. Reconstitute in water or a neutral buffer, aliquot into low-binding tubes, and avoid repeated freeze-thaw.
