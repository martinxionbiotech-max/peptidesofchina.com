Lemon Bottle is a multi-component liquid formulation rather than a single peptide. The label lists riboflavin, lecithin, bromelain, L-carnitine, artichoke extract, and a component spelled "thyidine" in the source specification. It is supplied in 10 mL vials, and it is studied as a formulation rather than as a pure compound, which changes almost everything about how a laboratory should characterise it.

Mixtures are analytically inconvenient. There is no single purity value, no molecular formula, and no mass spectrum that confirms the whole. A certificate for a formulation has to establish composition, activity where relevant, sterility, and homogeneity — four separate questions that a peptide certificate never has to answer. This page sets out what the components are studied for, what a batch record can realistically show, and where the documentation usually stops.

## Why This Compound

The formulation sits in a research tradition that began with phosphatidylcholine injections. Rotunda and Kolodney reviewed that history in *Dermatologic Surgery* in 2006, tracing how lecithin-derived phosphatidylcholine moved from a proposed solubilising agent into subcutaneous use, and how the evidence base developed around tissue response rather than around a single molecular mechanism. Their review is the most useful starting point because it separates the formulation's components from the claims made for them.

Phosphatidylcholine is an amphipathic lipid. In aqueous solution it forms bilayers and vesicles, and at sufficient concentration it can interact with cell membranes. In adipocyte culture, exposure to phosphatidylcholine has been used to study membrane disruption, lipid droplet release, and the subsequent inflammatory response. That is a laboratory observation about a lipid's physical chemistry, not a statement about what happens in a person after an injection, and the distinction matters for anyone reading marketing material.

Bromelain is a proteolytic enzyme preparation from pineapple stem. Maurer's 2001 review in *Cellular and Molecular Life Sciences* describes its biochemistry and its use as an anti-inflammatory and proteolytic agent in research settings. In this formulation it is included for its enzymatic activity against proteinaceous connective tissue, and the relevant analytical question is activity, not mass: a milligram of bromelain is not equivalent to a milligram of another bromelain preparation unless the activity units match.

Riboflavin is a FAD-dependent cofactor in fatty acid oxidation enzymes, and it is also a photosensitiser. Powers' 2003 review in the *American Journal of Clinical Nutrition* covers its chemistry and its role in redox metabolism. In a liquid formulation, the photosensitising property is a stability problem as much as a mechanistic one: riboflavin degrades under visible light, and a vial left on a bench changes composition.

L-Carnitine appears as a secondary component. Its role in the carnitine shuttle is well described, and it is supplied separately in this catalogue as a [powder](/catalog/l-carnitine/) and a [liquid](/catalog/l-carnitine-liquid/). In a mixture at low concentration, it is analytically difficult to distinguish from the background of a complex matrix.

Artichoke extract and the component listed as "thyidine" are the least characterised parts of the label. Artichoke extracts contain phenolic compounds and sesquiterpene lactones; "thyidine" is not a standard biochemical name, and a supplier who cannot define it cannot control it. That is not a reason to reject the formulation outright, but it is a reason to ask for a quantitative composition table rather than an ingredient list.

The research context, then, is adipose tissue biology and formulation behaviour: how a lipid vesicle interacts with a cell membrane, how a protease acts on extracellular matrix, how a photosensitive cofactor behaves in a sealed vial, and how a mixture can be characterised when no single assay covers all of it. Anyone looking for a clinical claim will not find one here, and that is deliberate. The product is supplied for laboratory research only.

## What the Batch Record Should Show

A formulation certificate should be read as a set of independent measurements rather than as a single release decision. The following fields are the ones worth checking.

**Quantitative composition.** An ingredient list is not a specification. The certificate should give a concentration for each declared component — mg/mL for phosphatidylcholine, riboflavin, carnitine, and the plant extract; activity units for bromelain. Without those numbers, two batches with the same label can differ by an order of magnitude in the components that matter.

**Phosphatidylcholine content and purity.** Lecithin is a mixture of phospholipids, and the phosphatidylcholine fraction is the relevant one. HPLC with evaporative light-scattering or charged aerosol detection is the usual method; the certificate should state the phosphatidylcholine percentage of the lecithin and the lecithin concentration in the formulation. A product that says "lecithin" without a phospholipid profile is under-specified.

**Bromelain activity.** Proteolytic activity is measured in gelatin digesting units or milk-clotting units, depending on the assay. The certificate should name the assay and give a value. A mass-based declaration tells you nothing about enzymatic potency, and potency is the property that degrades during storage.

**Riboflavin and carnitine.** Riboflavin can be measured by fluorescence or by UV absorbance at 445 nm; carnitine by LC-MS/MS or enzymatic assay. Both are present at concentrations that require a validated dilution scheme in a complex matrix. Spike recovery is the field that shows whether the method works in this formulation rather than in a clean standard.

**Physical parameters.** pH, osmolality, density, and fill volume. A 10 mL vial that contains 9.6 mL is within some fill tolerances and outside others; the certificate should state the acceptance range. Osmolality matters for any cell-based work and is often omitted.

**Sterility, endotoxin, and particulate matter.** A liquid that contacts cells should carry sterility and endotoxin data. Particulate matter is a particular concern for a formulation containing lipid vesicles and plant extract: precipitation and aggregation are visible failure modes. Light obscuration or membrane microscopy gives a sub-visible particle count.

**Homogeneity and phase behaviour.** A lipid-containing liquid can separate on standing. The certificate should state whether the batch was tested for homogeneity after gentle inversion, and whether any creaming or sedimentation was observed. This is a simple test that many suppliers skip.

**Batch traceability.** Lot number, fill date, expiry date, and the site of manufacture. For a formulated product, the site matters more than for a pure compound: mixing, homogenisation, and sterile filtration are process steps that can fail independently.

## Sourcing & Verification

Formulation products attract a particular kind of supplier: one who can copy a label quickly and cannot document what is inside. The questions below are designed to separate a formulator from a label printer.

- Can you provide a quantitative composition table with concentrations or activity units for every declared component?
- What is the phosphatidylcholine content of the lecithin, and how was it measured?
- What is the bromelain activity in units per millilitre, and by which assay?
- Is the product sterile, and by aseptic fill or terminal filtration? Terminal filtration of a lipid dispersion is not straightforward.
- Are endotoxin and particulate matter results available?
- How is homogeneity maintained during filling, and is it verified per batch?
- What is the source of the artichoke extract, and what marker compound is used for standardisation?
- Can you define "thyidine" and provide a specification for it?

The last two questions are the ones most suppliers cannot answer, and the answer is informative either way. A supplier who says "the extract is standardised to caffeoylquinic acids at 5%" has a specification; a supplier who says "it is a natural extract" does not. The [specification sheet note](/blog/why-peptide-specification-sheets-matter-in-procurement/) explains why a written specification is the minimum for any formulated product.

There is also a compliance dimension. Mesotherapy formulations have been the subject of regulatory attention in several markets, and the boundary between a research reagent and a medicinal product is drawn differently from country to country. The [compliance and sourcing risk hub](/peptide-compliance-risk/) sets out the general framework; for this product, the practical point is that documentation should describe a research formulation, not a treatment.

## Handling & Storage

Store at 2-8°C, protected from light, and do not freeze. Freezing a lipid dispersion can cause irreversible vesicle aggregation, and a thawed vial may not return to its original particle size distribution. Keep the vial in its carton; riboflavin is light-sensitive, and a clear vial on a windowsill is a degradation experiment.

Before sampling, let the vial reach room temperature and invert it gently ten to fifteen times. Do not vortex or shake vigorously. A lipid formulation foams, and foam changes the volume that a pipette can accurately withdraw. If visible particles or a separated layer remain after gentle inversion, do not use the vial.

Sampling from a multi-component liquid should be recorded like a small analytical procedure: temperature, inversion count, volume withdrawn, and dilution factor. Because the components have different densities and solubilities, the top and bottom of a standing vial are not identical. A laboratory that samples without mixing is measuring the vial's history rather than its composition.

For cell work, filter through a 0.22 µm membrane only if the formulation passes the filter without clogging; lipid dispersions can block a small-pore membrane quickly. A pre-filter or a larger pore size may be necessary, and the recovery of each component should be checked rather than assumed. If the formulation cannot be filtered without loss, work aseptically from the original vial and accept a shorter working life.

## How It Compares

**Phosphatidylcholine and deoxycholate formulations.** The older injectable tradition used phosphatidylcholine with sodium deoxycholate as a solubiliser. Deoxycholate is a bile acid with detergent-like membrane activity; phosphatidylcholine is a bilayer-forming lipid. The two are not interchangeable, and the literature on one does not transfer automatically to the other. Rotunda's review covers the distinction.

**Lipotropic blends.** [Lipo-C (Lipotropic Complex)](/catalog/lipo-c-lipotropic-complex/) and [Lipo-B](/catalog/lipo-b/) are aqueous vitamin and amino acid mixtures with no lipid phase. They are analytically simpler: no vesicles, no enzyme activity, no plant extract. A laboratory choosing between them should start from the analytical method it can actually run.

**L-Carnitine alone.** A single-component solution at 500 mg/mL, with a clean identity and assay story. If the research question is about carnitine transport, the single compound is the right reagent; a multi-component formulation adds variables that cannot be attributed.

**Single-component phospholipid products.** Where a supplier offers purified phosphatidylcholine, the analytical comparison is straightforward: phospholipid class profile, fatty acid composition, and vesicle size. The formulation here is broader and therefore harder to characterise, which is a trade-off rather than a defect.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is Lemon Bottle?**

A multi-component liquid research formulation containing riboflavin, lecithin (phosphatidylcholine), bromelain, L-carnitine, artichoke extract, and a component listed as thyidine. It is supplied in 10 mL vials for laboratory research only.

**Is there a single active ingredient?**

No. It is a formulation, and the research literature treats the components separately: phosphatidylcholine as a membrane-active lipid, bromelain as a protease, riboflavin as a redox cofactor, carnitine as a shuttle molecule. Any study using the mixture has to account for all of them.

**How is the composition verified?**

By a combination of assays: phospholipid content by HPLC with evaporative light-scattering or charged aerosol detection, bromelain activity in gelatin digesting units, riboflavin by fluorescence, carnitine by LC-MS/MS, plus pH, osmolality, sterility, and particulate testing. No single assay covers the whole formulation.

**Why does bromelain activity matter more than its mass?**

Because enzymatic potency varies between preparations and declines during storage. A milligram of one bromelain lot may have different activity from a milligram of another. Activity units are the only meaningful specification.

**How should the vial be stored?**

At 2-8°C, protected from light, and not frozen. Freezing can aggregate the lipid phase irreversibly. Let the vial warm to room temperature and invert gently before sampling.

**Can the formulation be filter-sterilised in the laboratory?**

Sometimes, but lipid dispersions clog small-pore membranes. Test recovery of each component after filtration before adopting the method, and use a pre-filter if necessary.

**What does "thyidine" refer to?**

It appears in the source specification but is not a standard biochemical name. Ask the supplier for a chemical identity and a specification; if neither is available, treat the component as undefined.

**Is this product intended for human use?**

## The Author's Take

**Position.** I would not buy a multi-component formulation without a quantitative composition table. An ingredient list is marketing; a table with concentrations and activity units is a product.

**Reasoning.** The analytical problem with mixtures is that everything can look acceptable while the formulation drifts. A batch with half the bromelain activity, a lecithin with a different phospholipid profile, or an extract standardised to nothing will pass a visual inspection and fail a study. The suppliers who can answer composition questions are also the ones who understand that a lipid dispersion has a particle size distribution and a phase behaviour, not just a colour and a fill volume. That understanding is what separates a formulator from a company that buys drums and fills vials.
