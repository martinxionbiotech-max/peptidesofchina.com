Lipo-C Focus is the odd one out in the Lipo family. It pairs L-carnitine and the methionine–inositol–choline complex with adenosine triphosphate, and it is supplied at 425 mg/mL in 10 mL ampoules rather than vials. The concentration is roughly double that of the 216 mg/mL blends, and the presence of ATP changes the analytical problem more than the number suggests.

ATP is a nucleotide, not a vitamin. In a sealed aqueous formulation it hydrolyses to ADP, then AMP, then adenosine and inosine, and each of those has a different ultraviolet spectrum and a different retention time. A certificate that reports "ATP" without saying how much ADP and AMP accompany it is reporting a family of compounds under one name. That is the central issue with this product, and it is worth understanding before comparing it with the other blends.

## Why This Compound

The carnitine and MIC components follow the same logic as the rest of the family. Carnitine carries long-chain acyl groups across the inner mitochondrial membrane through the CPT1–translocase–CPT2 cycle described by Bremer in 1983. Methionine supplies methyl groups via S-adenosylmethionine for phosphatidylcholine synthesis and for methylation reactions; Mato and Lu's 2008 review covers the pathway. Choline is the substrate for phosphatidylcholine synthesis via CDP-choline, and Zeisel's 2009 review sets out why it is treated as an essential nutrient. Inositol is the precursor for phosphoinositides and inositol phosphates, reviewed by Croze and Soulage in 2013.

ATP is the addition that needs explaining. Intracellular ATP is the currency of phosphorylation reactions, but a formulation containing ATP is not a delivery system for intracellular ATP: the nucleotide is charged, poorly membrane-permeant, and rapidly hydrolysed outside the cell. The research context in which ATP appears is extracellular purinergic signalling. Burnstock's 2007 review in *Cellular and Molecular Life Sciences* describes the P2X and P2Y receptor families, the ectonucleotidases that hydrolyse ATP to ADP, AMP, and adenosine, and the fact that the extracellular nucleotide concentration is a signalling variable rather than a fixed quantity.

That literature is the reason ATP appears in a research formulation at all. A laboratory studying purinergic signalling in a cell model may want a defined nucleotide source; a laboratory studying the carnitine shuttle does not. The blend therefore serves two different research interests that happen to share a vial, and the investigator should be clear about which one the experiment is testing.

There is a practical argument for the combination as well. The MIC components are cofactors and substrates for phospholipid synthesis; ATP is the phosphate donor for the kinases that activate choline to phosphocholine. In a cell-free or permeabilised system, supplying ATP alongside choline removes a variable. In an intact cell, the ATP is unlikely to cross the membrane intact, so the argument is weaker. That distinction between cell-free and intact-cell models is the one a buyer should keep in mind.

The ampoule format is part of the product's identity. Glass ampoules are sealed without a rubber closure, which reduces the risk of extractables from a stopper and eliminates the headspace exchange that occurs through an elastomer. They also require a break-open technique, produce glass particles if opened carelessly, and cannot be resealed. For a single-use concentrated reagent, that is a reasonable trade. For a laboratory that wants to sample repeatedly from one container, a vial is more practical.

At 425 mg/mL, the solution is dense and viscous. Osmolality is correspondingly high, and a small volume added to a culture well can change the medium's osmotic environment. The concentration also makes the ATP assay harder: the nucleotide must be diluted into a stabilised acidic solution quickly, because hydrolysis continues during sample preparation.

## What the Batch Record Should Show

The certificate for this product has to do something no other Lipo certificate does: separate ATP from its degradation products and quantify each.

**Adenine nucleotide profile.** Ion-pair reversed-phase HPLC with UV detection at 254 nm is the classical method for ATP, ADP, and AMP; the nucleotides are too polar to retain on a standard C18 column without an ion-pairing reagent such as tetrabutylammonium. The certificate should report ATP, ADP, and AMP separately, not as "total adenine nucleotides". A result that says "ATP 10 mg/mL" without an ADP figure is incomplete, because ADP is both a degradation product and a biologically active purinergic ligand.

**Sample handling.** ATP hydrolysis is fast at room temperature and faster at alkaline pH. The assay should quench the sample in cold acid or a chaotropic buffer immediately after dilution, and the certificate should state the quench protocol. A laboratory that receives an ATP value without that detail cannot tell whether the number reflects the vial or the sample preparation.

**Carnitine.** LC-MS/MS with a stable-isotope standard, or the enzymatic assay, with separation from the nucleotide peak. In a matrix containing ATP and choline, separation before detection is essential.

**Choline and inositol.** Choline by ion-pair HPLC with conductivity detection or LC-MS/MS; inositol by refractive index HPLC or LC-MS/MS after dilution. Both are present at concentrations high enough that a small relative error is a large absolute error.

**Methionine.** Amino acid analysis or LC-MS/MS, with the oxidation state stated. Methionine sulfoxide should be reported if the method can see it.

**Physical parameters.** pH, osmolality, density, fill volume, and particulate matter. The pH is particularly important for ATP stability: the nucleotide is most stable in mildly acidic solution and hydrolyses faster as pH rises. A certificate that reports pH without linking it to the ATP result is missing the connection.

**Sterility and endotoxin.** Ampoules are sealed glass, and the fill is aseptic or terminally filtered depending on the process. The certificate should state which.

**Container integrity.** Glass ampoules can shed particles when opened. The certificate should include a particulate matter result and, ideally, a note on the opening technique. A laboratory that snaps an ampoule without a filter may introduce glass fragments into the stock. The [quality testing hub](/peptide-quality-testing/) covers particulate testing in more detail.

## Sourcing & Verification

ATP-containing formulations are uncommon, and suppliers who offer them often cannot document the nucleotide profile. The questions below are specific to that gap.

- Does the certificate report ATP, ADP, and AMP separately, with the analytical method?
- What is the sample quench protocol, and how long between dilution and analysis?
- What is the pH of the formulation, and was it chosen for ATP stability or for the other components?
- Is the ATP sourced as the disodium salt, the free acid, or another form? The counterion changes the mass.
- Is the carnitine assay specific in the presence of ATP and choline?
- Is the osmolality reported, and at what dilution was it measured?
- Are the ampoules Type I glass, and is there a particulate matter result?
- Can the supplier provide raw chromatograms and a retained ampoule?

The ATP salt form is the field most often omitted. Disodium ATP is the common commercial form and carries a different mass from the free acid; a certificate that reports "ATP 20 mg" without the salt is ambiguous. The same logic that applies to peptide counterions applies here, in a different chemical family.

A second verification point: ask whether the ATP was added before or after sterile filtration. ATP binds to some filter materials and can be lost during filtration, so the order of operations affects the final concentration. A supplier who can answer that question has a process, not just a formula.

## Handling & Storage

Store at 2-8°C, protected from light, and do not freeze. ATP hydrolysis continues slowly even at 2-8°C, so the vial has a finite working life after opening. Record the date of first use and discard the ampoule after the working period stated by the supplier.

Open the ampoule carefully. Use a ampoule breaker or a gauze pad, snap away from the face, and inspect the neck for glass fragments before drawing the solution. Filter the working aliquot through a 0.22 µm membrane if the assay is cell-based; this also removes any glass particles introduced during opening.

Warm to room temperature and invert gently before sampling. Do not shake. The solution is viscous at 425 mg/mL, and a cold sample may not be homogeneous.

Dilute in two steps and keep the intermediate cold. A direct dilution of a concentrated ATP solution into warm medium gives the nucleotide time to hydrolyse before the experiment starts. Prepare the intermediate in a cold buffer at the assay pH, use it immediately, and record the time between dilution and addition to the cells.

For cell-free assays, the ATP concentration is the variable of interest and the other components are background. For intact-cell assays, the ATP is largely extracellular, and the relevant readout is purinergic receptor activation or ectonucleotidase activity rather than intracellular nucleotide delivery. Design the experiment around the question rather than around the label.

All handling described here is bench-scale laboratory work. Lipo-C Focus is supplied for research use only and is not intended for administration to humans or animals.

## The Author's Take

**Position.** I would not buy an ATP-containing formulation without an adenine nucleotide profile. "ATP 20 mg/mL" is a claim about a molecule that is already turning into something else in the vial.

**Reasoning.** ATP is stable enough to be sold and unstable enough to be a specification problem. The hydrolysis products are not inert: ADP activates P2Y receptors, AMP is a substrate for ecto-5'-nucleotidase, and adenosine is a potent signalling molecule in its own right. A certificate that collapses all four into one number is describing a mixture as if it were a pure compound. The suppliers who report the profile are the ones who understand that a nucleotide formulation has a shelf life measured in days at room temperature and months at 2-8°C, and that the label concentration is only true at the fill date.
