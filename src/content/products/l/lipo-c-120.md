Lipo-C 120 is the smallest blend in this product family: four components, one vial, no filler. The declared composition is L-carnitine 50 mg, methionine 15 mg, choline chloride 50 mg, and dexpanthenol 5 mg per millilitre, supplied in 10 mL vials. Where the larger Lipo-C products add arginine, inositol, and a wider B-vitamin panel, this one stays with the carnitine–choline–methionine axis and one cofactor precursor.

That restraint is the product's main selling point for research use. Every additional component in a blend is another variable that can move an endpoint, and four components can be documented more completely than eight. The trade-off is coverage: a study that needs nitric oxide signalling or B12-dependent remethylation will not find it here. This page explains what the four components do in the literature, what a four-analyte certificate should contain, and where the formulation's limits lie.

## Why This Compound

The blend is built on two connected pathways.

The first is the carnitine shuttle. Long-chain fatty acids are activated to acyl-CoA in the cytosol and cannot cross the inner mitochondrial membrane without being converted to acylcarnitine by carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1. The acylcarnitine is translocated by the carnitine-acylcarnitine translocase and reconverted by carnitine palmitoyltransferase 2 inside the matrix. Bremer's 1983 review in *Physiological Reviews* is the standard description. In this formulation, carnitine is present at 50 mg/mL, which is roughly 0.31 M — high enough that a small volume added to medium delivers a meaningful concentration without a large osmotic penalty from the carnitine itself, though the other salts still contribute.

The second is phosphatidylcholine synthesis. Choline is phosphorylated to phosphocholine, activated to CDP-choline, and combined with diacylglycerol to form phosphatidylcholine. That lipid is the main phospholipid of very-low-density lipoprotein particles, and without enough of it the liver cannot package triglycerides for export. Zeisel's 2009 review in *Nutrition Reviews* describes choline as an essential nutrient and summarises the evidence from choline-deficient diets; Corbin and Zeisel's 2012 review connects the same pathway to non-alcoholic fatty liver research. Methionine enters the same story through S-adenosylmethionine, which supplies the methyl groups for the phosphatidylethanolamine N-methyltransferase route to phosphatidylcholine. Mato and Lu's 2008 review in *Annual Review of Nutrition* covers methionine metabolism and its link to liver disease.

The two routes are complementary rather than redundant. Choline is the substrate; methionine, via SAM, is the methyl donor for the alternative synthesis route. A formulation that supplies both is testing the repletion of a pathway at two points, which is why the pair appears together in lipotropic research.

Dexpanthenol is the fourth component. It is the alcohol form of pantothenic acid, and pantothenic acid is a precursor of coenzyme A. Tahiliani and Beinlich's 1991 review covers pantothenic acid in health and disease, including its role in CoA biosynthesis. CoA is the carrier that activates acyl groups for β-oxidation, so a formulation containing carnitine and a CoA precursor is supplying both the shuttle and the activated acyl acceptor. Whether that combination produces a measurable difference in a cell model is an empirical question; the rationale is mechanistic, not clinical.

What the blend does not contain is also part of the rationale. There is no arginine, so nitric oxide synthase substrate availability is not being manipulated. There is no inositol, so phosphoinositide signalling is not being supplemented. There is no B6 or B12, so the transsulfuration and remethylation arms of one-carbon metabolism are not being pushed. A laboratory that wants those variables should choose a larger blend; a laboratory that wants a clean four-variable model should start here.

## What the Batch Record Should Show

Four analytes is a manageable analytical problem, and a competent certificate can cover all of them with named methods.

**Carnitine.** LC-MS/MS with a stable-isotope-labelled internal standard is the most specific method; enzymatic assay coupled to CoA release is the classical alternative. In a matrix that also contains choline, separation before detection is necessary because both are small polar cations. The certificate should state the assay range and the dilution factor, since 50 mg/mL requires a large dilution before analysis.

**Methionine.** Amino acid analysis by ion-exchange chromatography with ninhydrin detection, or LC-MS/MS after derivatisation. Methionine oxidises to methionine sulfoxide, and a certificate that reports only the reduced form may be underestimating total methionine or overestimating degradation depending on how the sample was handled. The oxidation state should be stated.

**Choline.** Choline is measured by LC-MS/MS or by ion-pair HPLC with conductivity detection. The salt form must be declared, and the assay should distinguish choline from acetylcholine and from phosphocholine if the method is sensitive to them. In a simple blend the interference risk is low, but the salt-form ambiguity is not.

**Dexpanthenol.** Dexpanthenol can be measured directly by HPLC with UV detection near 210 nm, or indirectly as pantothenic acid after hydrolysis. The certificate should say which species was quantified and whether the hydrolysis was complete.

**Quality panel.** pH, osmolality, density, fill volume, sterility, endotoxin, and particulate burden. At 120 mg/mL total solids, the solution is concentrated enough that osmolality is a real variable in cell work. A blend added at 1% v/v to medium contributes a small osmotic load; added at 10% v/v it does not.

**Homogeneity and fill.** A four-component blend is easier to mix than an eight-component one, but stratification during filling is still possible. The certificate should state the mixing regime and the homogeneity check.

**Batch traceability.** Lot number, fill date, expiry date, and manufacturing site. For a compounded product, the site is part of the specification: mixing and sterile filtration are process steps that can fail independently of the chemistry.

## Sourcing & Verification

Lipo-C 120 is the simplest product in the family, which makes it the easiest to verify and the easiest to counterfeit. The questions below are written for that combination.

- Is the choline declared as choline chloride, and is the 50 mg figure the salt or the base?
- Is the carnitine assay by LC-MS/MS or enzymatic method, and what is the limit of quantification?
- Is methionine reported as the free amino acid, and is methionine sulfoxide measured separately?
- Is dexpanthenol quantified directly or as pantothenic acid after hydrolysis?
- Does the sum of the four components match the stated total concentration within tolerance?
- Is the product sterile-filled, and what sterility and endotoxin results accompany the lot?
- What is the osmolality, and at what dilution was it measured?
- Can the supplier provide raw chromatograms and a retained sample?

The first question is the one that changes the arithmetic most. A supplier who reports choline chloride as "choline" is overstating the active mass by about a quarter. A supplier who reports choline base as "choline chloride" is understating the salt mass but overstating the choline. Either way, the number on the label and the number in the vial are not the same, and the certificate is the only place to find out which convention was used.

The [specification sheet note](/blog/why-peptide-specification-sheets-matter-in-procurement/) explains why a written specification should state the salt form for every component. For a four-component product, that requirement is easy to meet and therefore a fair test of the supplier.

## Handling & Storage

Store at 2-8°C, protected from light, and do not freeze. Let the vial reach room temperature before opening, then invert gently several times. The solution is less viscous than the 216 mg/mL blends but still concentrated enough to stratify if it has stood for a long time.

Sampling should be volumetric and recorded. Because the components have different molecular weights, a dilution expressed as "1:100" is unambiguous only if the stock density is known. Record the density, the temperature, and the dilution factor; if a result is anomalous later, that record is the only way to trace it.

For cell work, filter a working aliquot through a 0.22 µm membrane. Carnitine and choline are small and do not adsorb strongly, but methionine and dexpanthenol can interact with some membrane materials at low concentration. A recovery check on the component with the lowest concentration — dexpanthenol at 5 mg/mL — is the most sensitive test.

Light protection is not optional. B5 is relatively stable, but the solution as a whole should be kept in its carton. A vial left on a bench under fluorescent light for a week is not the same reagent it was when filled.

All handling described here is bench-scale laboratory work. Lipo-C 120 is supplied for research use only and is not intended for administration to humans or animals.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is Lipo-C 120?**

A four-component aqueous research blend containing L-carnitine 50 mg, methionine 15 mg, choline chloride 50 mg, and dexpanthenol 5 mg per millilitre, supplied in 10 mL vials for laboratory research only.

**Why is it called 120?**

The name refers to the total concentration of the four components, 120 mg/mL. It is a label convention, not a measure of any single component's activity.

**Is the choline in the blend choline chloride or choline base?**

The catalogue declares choline chloride. That matters for the arithmetic: choline chloride is about 75% choline by mass, so 50 mg of the salt delivers roughly 37 mg of choline.

**How does Lipo-C 120 differ from Lipo-C 216?**

Lipo-C 120 has four components and a lower total concentration. Lipo-C 216 adds L-arginine, inositol, B6, and B12, which broadens the metabolic coverage and the analytical burden. The smaller blend is easier to interpret; the larger one covers more pathways.

**How should Lipo-C 120 be kept?**

At 2-8°C, protected from light, and not frozen. Warm to room temperature and invert gently before sampling. Keep the vial in its carton.

**Can it be added directly to cell culture medium?**

Yes, but calculate the dilution and the osmotic contribution first. The blend is concentrated; a large addition to a small well changes the medium's osmolality.

**What is dexpanthenol doing in a lipotropic blend?**

Dexpanthenol is a pantothenic acid precursor, and pantothenic acid is required for coenzyme A synthesis. CoA activates acyl groups for β-oxidation, so the component is included as a cofactor precursor rather than as a lipotropic agent in the classical sense.

**What does the certificate say about sterility?**

The catalogue describes a sterile liquid formulation. If the certificate is silent on sterilization and sterility, the vial should be treated as non-sterile in the laboratory.

## The Author's Take

**Position.** Lipo-C 120 is the blend I would use to teach someone how to read a lipotropic certificate. Four components, four methods, one salt-form trap, and no place to hide.

**Reasoning.** The larger blends are impressive on paper and difficult to audit. With four analytes, a laboratory can check every number against a named method and a stated salt form. The choline chloride convention alone is a useful test: a supplier who states it has thought about mass balance, and a supplier who does not has probably copied a label. The product is also the easiest to compare across lots, because there are only four numbers to track. That simplicity is worth more in a research reagent than an extra cofactor that the study design cannot attribute.
