## Why This Compound

The Khavinson peptides are built on a tissue-extract tradition. The story is that short peptides corresponding to fragments of tissue-specific proteins can act as epigenetic regulators, turning on or off genes associated with the tissue they were derived from. The story is attractive because it is simple, and it has produced a large commercial market. The peer-reviewed evidence is uneven: some peptides in the family have been studied in chromatin and cell-differentiation assays, but the mechanism remains contested and the human data are thin.

Ovagen is the liver-associated member of the family. In the catalogues it is described as a hepatoprotective bioregulator; in the primary literature, the liver connection is mostly inferred from the tissue-extract lineage rather than demonstrated in a controlled study of the tetrapeptide itself. I am not saying the connection is false. I am saying it is not established at the level that a methods section usually requires.

That gap is what makes Ovagen useful as a research chemical. If you are studying the family, Ovagen is a negative-control candidate: a tetrapeptide with the same physical properties as Livagen but a different sequence. If you are studying chromatin, it is a short, cheap, well-defined peptide that can be used to test whether an effect is sequence-specific. If you are sourcing, it is a test of whether a supplier can distinguish a real analytical package from a narrative.

The naming convention is worth understanding before you buy. The family names are commercial labels, not chemical names: Ovagen, Livagen, Cardiogen and Vesugen each correspond to a short sequence, and the tissue word in the name is a claim about the research tradition rather than a statement about the molecule's receptor or target. Two suppliers can use the same name for the same sequence and still deliver different salts, different water content and different impurities. That is why the certificate matters more than the name on the label. A buyer who compares products by name alone is comparing marketing, not chemistry. A batch record with a mass spectrum, a water-content value and a counterion statement tells you more than a page of tissue-specific prose.

## What the Batch Record Should Show

Identity for a tetrapeptide is not established by a single mass. The calculated mass of Glu-Asp-Leu-Ala is unambiguous, but several other tetrapeptides can fall within the same nominal-mass window. The certificate should include MS/MS fragmentation that assigns the residue order, not just an intact mass. For a four-residue peptide, the b-ion and y-ion series are short and easy to read; there is no excuse for omitting them.

Purity should be measured by reversed-phase HPLC at 214 nm. Ovagen has no aromatic residues, so 280 nm is not a useful detection wavelength. The chromatogram should show the main peak and the integration baseline, and the method should resolve the peptide from deletion products and from the counterion. A “≥98%” figure derived from a single peak with no orthogonal detection is a claim, not a characterisation.

Counterion and water content are especially important for a tetrapeptide because the peptide content per vial is small. A 20 mg vial that contains 15% water and 10% counterion may contain only about 15 mg of peptide. If the certificate reports purity but not water and counterion, the actual amount is unknown. Karl Fischer and a counterion assay should be part of the release panel.

Endotoxin and bioburden should be reported for any batch intended for cell work. Short peptides are often assumed to be clean because they are small, but the synthesis and purification process can leave lipopolysaccharide and microbial contamination. A chromatin assay is sensitive to both. The [characterisation checklist](/blog/peptide-characterization-reporting-checklist/) lists the fields we expect; the [purity article](/blog/why-purity-and-concentration-tests-are-not-enough/) explains why a single number is not enough.

## Sourcing & Verification

Ovagen is a niche product, and most suppliers are repackaging material from a small number of Chinese synthesis houses. That makes the supply chain short but opaque. The first question is not “what is the price?” but “who synthesised the batch?” If the answer is a trading company, ask for the manufacturer's certificate and the synthesis site. If the answer is a synthesis house, ask for the batch record and the purification method.

The second question is the sequence. A supplier who cannot provide a mass spectrum with fragmentation is selling a label. The sequence is only four residues, so the analysis is not expensive; a supplier who skips it is skipping quality control. Ask for the calculated mass, the observed mass and the fragment table. If the table is missing, assume the sequence is unverified.

The third question is the counterion and the water content. These are the fields that determine how much peptide you actually have. A certificate that reports “20 mg per vial” without a peptide-content assay is reporting the fill weight, not the active amount. Ask for the assay and the water content, and calculate the peptide content yourself.

The fourth question is the supplier's technical literacy. A supplier who can explain the difference between Ovagen, Livagen and Epithalon, and who can provide a mass for each, is more credible than one who describes them all as “tissue-specific bioregulators.” The [peptide categories hub](/peptide-categories-mechanisms/) explains the family; the [sequence confirmation guide](/blog/what-is-sequence-confirmation-clinical-peptides/) explains the analytics.

There is one more document worth asking for: the audit trail. For a small custom batch, the supplier should be able to say when the batch was synthesised, how it was purified, who released it and whether the same site made the previous lot. An audit trail is not a quality guarantee, but its absence is a warning. A supplier who cannot reconstruct the batch history is unlikely to be able to investigate a problem later.

Finally, check the physical form and the storage history. The powder should be white to off-white and dissolve clear in water. A yellow or sticky powder suggests oxidation or moisture. If the shipment arrives warm, inspect the cake before use. The [quality-testing hub](/peptide-quality-testing/) has the broader context.

## Handling & Storage

Store the lyophilized powder at −20 °C, protected from light and moisture. Let the vial equilibrate before opening. Weigh or transfer in a dry environment; the powder is hygroscopic and static-prone.

For reconstitution, use sterile water or a neutral buffer. The peptide is hydrophilic and usually dissolves readily. Add the solvent slowly and swirl; avoid vortexing. If the solution is hazy, centrifuge at low speed and inspect the pellet. Do not filter through a membrane that binds peptides unless you have validated recovery.

Dilute into the assay buffer in steps. Keep the pH and ionic strength constant across conditions. For cell-based work, use low-binding tubes and prepare fresh dilutions from a single stock. Run a vehicle control at the same counterion concentration. If the assay is sensitive to endotoxin, use a lot with a documented LAL result.

Aliquot and freeze at −20 °C or −80 °C. Avoid repeated freeze-thaw. A working solution at 2-8 °C should be used within a few days. Record the lot, the reconstitution date and the solvent. **What is Ovagen's sequence?**

The catalogue sequence is Glu-Asp-Leu-Ala, often abbreviated EDLA. It is a four-residue synthetic peptide supplied as a lyophilized powder. The sequence should be confirmed by mass spectrometry and MS/MS on the actual batch.

**Is Ovagen the same as Livagen?**

No. Both are Khavinson-family tetrapeptides, but the sequences differ: Ovagen is Glu-Asp-Leu-Ala, Livagen is Lys-Glu-Asp-Ala. They are different molecules with different masses and should not be substituted.

**Why is there so little published research on Ovagen?**

The peptide is part of a family with a published tradition, but compound-specific primary studies are scarce. Most of the literature discusses the family or related sequences. That gap is a reason to be precise about what you are testing, not a reason to assume an effect.

**How do I verify the sequence?**

Use intact mass plus MS/MS fragmentation. A four-residue peptide is short enough for full backbone coverage. A mass match alone does not distinguish a sequence isomer.
