## Why This Compound

Oxytocin's research identity comes from its receptor. OXTR is a G-protein-coupled receptor expressed in a range of tissues, and oxytocin is the reference ligand that most OXTR-binding and signal-transduction assays are calibrated against. The peptide is also a structural model: it is small enough for NMR and crystallography, it has a well-defined disulfide framework, and its relatives — vasopressin, carbetocin, atosiban — form a family of ligands with different selectivities. That combination makes it a useful teaching molecule and a common analytical reference.

The second reason to work with oxytocin is its stability profile. The disulfide bond can be reduced, scrambled or oxidised; the C-terminal amide can be hydrolysed; the peptide can adsorb to metal surfaces and to glass. A batch that is pure at release can degrade during storage or handling, and the degradation products may co-elute with the parent in a generic HPLC method. Labs that use oxytocin as a quantitative standard need a method that separates those forms.

The third reason is regulatory. Oxytocin is a controlled or restricted substance in some jurisdictions, and its clinical use is tightly regulated. ## What the Batch Record Should Show

Identity should be confirmed by intact mass and by MS/MS or peptide mapping. The intact mass of the disulfide form is 1007.2 Da; the reduced form is about 2 Da higher. The certificate should state which form the mass corresponds to. A mass that matches the reduced peptide but a label that claims the cyclic form is a red flag.

Disulfide integrity is the most important attribute for this molecule. The certificate should include a method that distinguishes the cyclic form from the reduced and scrambled forms. Reversed-phase HPLC can do this if the gradient is shallow and the column is appropriate; mass detection is better. A single peak at 214 nm does not prove the disulfide is correctly paired, because a scrambled isomer can have a similar retention time.

Purity should be measured at 214 or 220 nm. Oxytocin has one tyrosine, so 280 nm is possible but less sensitive. The method should resolve the main peak from deamidated, oxidised and truncated forms. The certificate should state the column, mobile phase, gradient, wavelength and integration threshold. If the method is a generic gradient borrowed from another peptide, ask for a lot-specific chromatogram; the [HPLC versus LC-MS guide](/blog/hplc-vs-lc-ms-peptides/) explains why the detector choice changes the answer.

Counterion and water content are next. Oxytocin acetate is the acetate salt; the acetate content affects the mass balance and the pH of the reconstituted solution. Karl Fischer water content should be reported. For cell work, endotoxin and bioburden should be reported as well. The [characterisation checklist](/blog/peptide-characterization-reporting-checklist/) lists the fields we review.

Finally, the certificate should state the storage history and the light exposure. Oxytocin is sensitive to light and to metal ions, and a batch that has been stored in a clear vial on a bench is not the same as one stored in amber glass at −20 °C. ## Sourcing & Verification

Oxytocin is a mature peptide with many suppliers, and the market includes material intended for pharmaceutical use, for research and for veterinary use. Those grades are not interchangeable. A pharmaceutical-grade solution may contain preservatives or buffers that are not disclosed on a research certificate; a research-grade powder may be perfectly pure and still carry a counterion that interferes with your assay. The first verification step is to confirm the grade and the intended use.

The second step is to confirm the disulfide form. Ask for a method that distinguishes the cyclic, reduced and scrambled forms. If the supplier cannot provide one, the certificate is incomplete. A mass spectrum alone is not enough, because the reduced and cyclic forms differ by only 2 Da and the scrambled form has the same mass as the cyclic form.

The third step is to confirm the counterion and the water content. A 5 mg vial that contains 10% water and 8% acetate may contain less than 4.2 mg of peptide. If the certificate reports only “purity ≥98%,” the actual amount is unknown. Ask for the assay and the water content, and calculate the peptide content yourself. The [quality-testing hub](/peptide-quality-testing/) explains how these fields fit into a qualification file.

The fourth step is to inspect the vial. Oxytocin powder should be white to off-white and dissolve clear in water or dilute acetic acid. A yellow or sticky powder suggests oxidation or moisture. If the peptide is supplied as a solution, check the pH and the presence of particulates. The [solubility guide](/blog/peptide-solubility-troubleshooting/) covers the common problems.

Finally, check the supplier's compliance language. Oxytocin is a restricted substance in some markets, and a supplier who markets it with therapeutic claims is creating risk for the buyer. This listing is research-use-only. The [compliance hub](/peptide-compliance-risk/) discusses the boundary.

## Handling & Storage

Store the lyophilized powder at −20 °C, protected from light and moisture. Let the vial equilibrate before opening. Weigh or transfer in a dry environment; the powder is hygroscopic.

For reconstitution, use sterile water or 0.1% acetic acid. The peptide is soluble in water, but the disulfide bond is sensitive to metal ions; use glassware that has been rinsed with chelating agent or plastic that is certified low-metal. Avoid contact with stainless-steel needles for prolonged periods. Add the solvent slowly and swirl; avoid vortexing.

Dilute into the assay buffer in steps. Keep the pH slightly acidic if the assay tolerates it; oxytocin is more stable at pH 3-5 than at neutral pH. Avoid high pH and strong oxidants. If the solution is hazy, centrifuge at low speed and inspect the pellet. Do not filter through a membrane that binds peptides unless you have validated recovery.

Aliquot and freeze at −20 °C or −80 °C. Avoid repeated freeze-thaw. A working solution at 2-8 °C should be used within a few days. Record the lot, the reconstitution date and the solvent. ## How It Compares

Vasopressin is the closest structural relative: it differs at two residues and has a different receptor selectivity profile. In a binding assay, the two are distinguished by receptor choice and by the presence of selective antagonists. Carbetocin is a longer-acting oxytocin analogue with a different modification pattern; it is a separate product, not a formulation of oxytocin. Atosiban is an oxytocin-receptor antagonist and belongs in the same pharmacological family but has the opposite functional direction.

Within the catalogue, [Gonadorelin Acetate](/catalog/gonadorelin-acetate/) and [Sermorelin Acetate](/catalog/sermorelin-acetate/) are other lyophilized peptide hormones, but they act on different receptors and are not substitutes. The useful comparison is analytical: all three are small peptides with amide or acetate counterions, and all three benefit from a shallow HPLC gradient and a mass-confirmed identity.

If your question is receptor pharmacology, compare oxytocin with vasopressin and with a selective OXTR antagonist. If your question is analytical, compare the cyclic and reduced forms by LC-MS. The catalogue can supply the reference materials; it cannot tell you which receptor your assay is measuring.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is the difference between oxytocin and vasopressin in research?**

They are structural relatives with different receptor selectivity. Oxytocin is the reference ligand for OXTR; vasopressin is the reference for the V1a, V1b and V2 receptors. In a binding assay, the choice of receptor and the use of selective antagonists distinguish them.

**Why is oxytocin used in ASD research models?**

That question belongs to clinical neuroscience, not to a sourcing page. This listing is a research reagent, and we do not make claims about diagnostic or behavioural outcomes. If your protocol uses oxytocin as a pharmacological tool, describe the model and the controls precisely.

**What purity is your oxytocin acetate?**

The catalog specification is ≥98% by HPLC. The lot certificate states the measured value and the method. Ask for the chromatogram and the mass spectrum if you need to compare batches.

**What are the storage requirements?**

Lyophilized powder at −20 °C, dry and protected from light. Aqueous solutions at 2-8 °C, used within about two weeks. The disulfide bond is sensitive to light, metal ions and high pH, so use low-metal containers and slightly acidic buffers.

**How do I check disulfide bond integrity?**

Use a method that resolves the cyclic, reduced and scrambled forms, such as reversed-phase HPLC with mass detection or a specific thiol-labeling assay. A single 214 nm peak is not sufficient.

**Why does oxytocin adsorb to metal?**

The cysteine residues and the disulfide bond can interact with metal surfaces, and the peptide can chelate trace metals. Use plastic or passivated glass, avoid prolonged contact with stainless steel, and add a chelator only if the assay tolerates it.

**What is the difference between oxytocin and carbetocin?**

Carbetocin is a longer-acting analogue with a different modification pattern. It is a separate molecule, not a formulation of oxytocin, and it should be characterised separately.

**Is the acetate salt important?**

Yes. The acetate counterion affects the mass balance, the solubility and the pH of the reconstituted solution. A certificate that does not state the salt form is incomplete for quantitative work.
