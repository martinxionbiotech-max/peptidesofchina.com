Vasoactive intestinal peptide is a 28-residue neuropeptide with a sequence that leaves little room for ambiguity and a chemistry that leaves several ways to ruin it. It contains a methionine, an amidated C-terminus, and a tryptophan-free but tyrosine-containing chain. Those three features define the analytical agenda: oxidation of methionine adds 16 Da, deamidation of asparagine changes charge, and the amide is lost under alkaline conditions. A purity percentage measured on a fresh lot can look excellent while a reconstituted stock is already a mixture.

This page covers the compound as a receptor-pharmacology tool: how it is characterised, what a lot record should report, how it behaves in solution, and why the choice between VIP and the related PACAP peptides matters for assay interpretation. The research literature on VIP is broad; the sourcing literature is not, and that gap is where buyers get hurt.

## Why This Compound

VIP was isolated in 1970 from porcine small intestine on the strength of its vasodilatory action, and the early pharmacology treated it as a gut and vascular peptide. The receptor work that followed split the activity into three targets: VPAC1 and VPAC2, which bind VIP and PACAP with high affinity, and PAC1, which is PACAP-selective and responds poorly to VIP. That receptor map is the reason VIP is useful in assays where a VPAC response is wanted without a PAC1 confound.

The second research line is circadian. In the suprachiasmatic nucleus, VIP is released by a subset of neurons and acts on VPAC2 receptors to synchronise the molecular clocks of neighbouring cells. Disrupting the peptide or the receptor produces a desynchronised clock at the tissue level even when individual neurons keep oscillating. That is a clean, well-documented preparation-level phenotype, and it makes VIP a standard tool in circadian explant work. A study that adds VIP to an SCN slice is asking whether the peptide can reset or align the population, not whether it changes a whole animal's behaviour.

The third line is smooth muscle pharmacology. VPAC receptors couple to Gs and raise cyclic AMP, which relaxes airway, vascular, and intestinal smooth muscle in isolated-tissue preparations. The effect is measurable in organ baths and in myography, and it is one of the cleaner functional readouts for receptor activity. The fourth line is neuroendocrine and exocrine secretion, where VIP acts on secretory epithelia and on the pancreas.

The catalogue's research-areas field also lists immunomodulation and neuroinflammation. Those are legitimate research contexts in the literature, but they are context, not product claims. A cell-culture study can measure cytokine release from a macrophage line after VIP treatment; what that shows is a response in that model. It does not establish an immune effect in an organism, and it should not be described as one.

What makes VIP analytically interesting is the combination of length and liability. Twenty-eight residues is long enough that deletion sequences are a real synthesis problem, and the methionine is a oxidation site that appears in every handling step. The C-terminal amide is essential for receptor binding; a free acid form is a common impurity and a much weaker ligand. A certificate that reports one purity number without separating the oxidized and deamidated forms is not describing the material a receptor assay will see.

## What the Batch Record Should Show

**Sequence confirmation.** Intact mass by LC-MS should match the calculated mass of 3326.8 Da for the amidated peptide within a few ppm. Because the peptide is long enough for sequence scrambling to matter, MS/MS coverage of the backbone should be requested, at least for the first lot from a supplier. The b- and y-ion ladder should cover the N-terminal and C-terminal regions, including the amide.

**Purity with degradant resolution.** Reversed-phase HPLC at 214 nm is standard. The method should resolve the parent peak from the methionine sulfoxide (+16 Da), the sulfone (+32 Da), and the deamidated forms. A shallow gradient over 30-40 minutes is more informative than a fast generic run. Ask for the retention times of the known degradants, not just the percentage of the main peak. The [HPLC versus LC-MS note](/blog/hplc-vs-lc-ms-peptides/) explains which question each method can answer.

**Oxidation state at release.** The certificate should report the percentage of methionine sulfoxide for the lot, not merely the main-peak purity. A lot that is 98% pure by area and 4% oxidized is a different reagent from one that is 98% pure and 0.5% oxidized. For receptor work, the difference can matter because oxidized VIP binds less well.

**Counterion and salt form.** VIP is commonly supplied as the acetate salt. The counterion changes the mass and the pH of the reconstituted solution. The certificate should name it. The [counterion note](/blog/role-of-counterions-in-peptide-stability/) explains why a mass balance without the salt form is incomplete.

**Peptide content and water content.** A 10 mg fill is small, and a 28-residue peptide is hygroscopic. Karl Fischer water and a peptide-content assay should both be reported. Without them, the label is a nominal weight.

**Endotoxin and bioburden.** Report endotoxin in EU/mg with the method named. For anything used in cell culture or in an explant preparation, endotoxin is a primary variable. The [endotoxin primer](/blog/what-are-endotoxins-why-they-matter/) covers the assay choices.

**Lot traceability.** The lot on the certificate should match the vial, and the certificate should name the site of synthesis. For a 28-mer, the synthesis and purification are non-trivial, and a repackager is unlikely to have the analytical capability to release it properly.

## Sourcing & Verification

VIP is made by solid-phase synthesis, and a 28-residue sequence is long enough that the yield falls and the impurity profile widens. A competent facility can make it; a trading company cannot. The first question is therefore whether the seller is the manufacturer or a reseller. The second is whether the certificate was generated on the lot being sold.

Three practical checks. First, ask for the full sequence and the calculated mass before ordering, and compare them with the published sequence. A supplier who paraphrases the sequence or omits the C-terminal amide is not paying attention. Second, ask for a lot-specific chromatogram with the oxidation products labelled. Third, buy a small quantity and send an aliquot for independent LC-MS. For a peptide this size, the mass spectrum is the identity test that matters, and the oxidation profile is the stability test.

Price reflects synthesis difficulty. A 28-mer requires more coupling steps and more purification than a tetrapeptide, and the cost floor is correspondingly higher. A listing that undercuts that floor by a wide margin is usually selling a shorter fragment, a crude preparation, or a smaller fill. The [purity versus concentration note](/blog/why-purity-and-concentration-tests-are-not-enough/) explains why the three are easy to confuse in a catalogue.

Shipping is a genuine variable. Lyophilized VIP is stable at ambient temperature for short periods, but a parcel that sits in a warm depot for a week is not the same material on arrival. Ask for the shipping configuration and inspect the vial on receipt. If the powder has changed colour or collapsed into a sticky mass, the batch has taken on water.

Customs documentation should describe the product accurately and carry the research-use statement. ## Handling & Storage

Lyophilized VIP should be held at −20°C, dry and protected from light. Equilibrate the vial to room temperature in a sealed bag before opening. A cold vial opened in humid air will condense water onto the cake, and a damp cake is prone to oxidation during storage.

Reconstitute gently in water or a neutral buffer. Add the solvent down the wall and swirl; do not vortex. VIP foams readily, and foam increases the air-liquid interface where methionine oxidation occurs. If the solution is cloudy, check the pH and the salt form before adding more solvent.

The catalogue advises use within one week at 2-8°C after reconstitution. That is a reasonable default, but the more important rule is to minimise the time the peptide spends in dilute solution. Oxidation is a second-order process in peptide concentration and a first-order process in oxygen, so a dilute stock in a half-full tube oxidises faster than a concentrated stock in a full tube. Prepare the working dilution immediately before the assay, and purge the tube headspace with inert gas if the experiment is sensitive to oxidation.

Aliquot and freeze at −20°C or −80°C for longer storage. Repeated freeze-thaw cycles promote aggregation and oxidation at the ice interface. Use low-binding tubes; VIP adsorbs to polystyrene and to some polypropylene surfaces at low concentration. Adding a carrier protein such as bovine serum albumin can reduce loss if the assay buffer permits it.

Avoid alkaline pH. The C-terminal amide is stable under mild conditions but can hydrolyse at high pH, and the asparagine residues can deamidate. A buffer at pH 7.4 is a reasonable default; a carbonate buffer at pH 9 is not. Protect from light, and keep solutions on ice during the experiment rather than at room temperature. The comparison that matters is VIP against PACAP. Both bind VPAC1 and VPAC2, but PACAP also activates PAC1 with high affinity, and VIP does not. If an assay uses VIP to claim a VPAC effect, the interpretation is clean only if PAC1 is absent or blocked. If the same assay uses PACAP, the response may run through either receptor. That is the reason VIP is the better tool for a VPAC-selective question, and the reason a paper that treats the two as interchangeable is making an assumption.

Against secretin, the similarity is structural rather than pharmacological. The two peptides share the glucagon/secretin fold, but their receptor preferences differ. A supplier who lists them as alternatives is compressing two different literatures. ## The Author's Take

**Position.** I would not accept a VIP certificate that reports only a main-peak purity percentage. For this peptide, the oxidation profile and the C-terminal amide are part of the identity, not optional extras.

**Reasoning.** The molecule has a methionine that oxidises during synthesis, purification, and storage, and an amide that is easy to lose. Both changes produce species that co-elute with the parent on a careless method and bind differently at the receptor. A buyer who compares two suppliers on "purity" alone is comparing numbers that may have been generated on different methods with different integration limits. The useful comparison is a chromatogram with labelled degradants and a mass spectrum that confirms the amide.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How does VIP differ from PACAP in research applications?**

VIP and PACAP share VPAC1 and VPAC2, but PACAP also binds PAC1 with high affinity, while VIP activates PAC1 only weakly. A VPAC-selective question is therefore cleaner with VIP. If the assay uses PACAP, the response may run through PAC1, and the interpretation needs a PAC1 blocker or a PAC1-null model to separate the contributions.

**What is VIP's role in circadian biology research?**

VIP is released by a subset of neurons in the suprachiasmatic nucleus and acts on VPAC2 receptors to synchronise the molecular clocks of neighbouring cells. Disrupting VIP or VPAC2 desynchronises the population rhythm in explant preparations. That makes VIP a standard tool for studying intercellular coupling in the clock.

**What purity is your VIP?**

The catalogue specification is ≥98% by HPLC. The lot certificate should also report the methionine sulfoxide content, the counterion, water content, and peptide content. A purity figure without those values does not describe the material a receptor assay will see.

**What are the storage conditions?**

Lyophilized VIP at −20°C, dry and protected from light. Reconstituted solutions at 2-8°C for up to one week, as the catalogue states, or aliquoted and frozen for longer storage. Avoid repeated freeze-thaw cycles and alkaline buffers.

**Why does oxidized VIP matter for a binding assay?**

Methionine oxidation changes the local conformation and reduces affinity at VPAC receptors. A lot with a high sulfoxide content will produce a weaker response than a clean lot at the same nominal concentration. The certificate should report the oxidized fraction, not just the main-peak purity.

**Can VIP be used in cell-culture assays?**

Yes, in receptor-expression and primary cell models, provided the material is endotoxin-controlled and the assay buffer is compatible. The catalogue product is a research reagent, not a sterile cell-culture grade, so the user is responsible for sterility and for running vehicle controls.

**How should dilute VIP solutions be handled?**

Use low-binding tubes, keep the solution concentrated until immediately before use, avoid vortexing, and consider a carrier protein. At low concentrations, adsorption to plastic and oxidation at the air-liquid interface both reduce the amount of active peptide that reaches the assay.
