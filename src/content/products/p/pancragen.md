Pancragen is a tetrapeptide from the Khavinson series of tissue-derived bioregulators, and it is a good example of why the bioregulator category needs more careful reading than the average peptide listing. The molecule is small, the synthesis is routine, and the price is modest. The hard part is identity: the catalogue has described Pancragen as Lys–Glu–Asp–Pro, while the peer-reviewed pancreatic literature specifies Lys–Glu–Asp–Trp-NH2. Those are not the same peptide. One residue and one C-terminal amide separate them, and that is enough to change the mass, the UV spectrum and the interpretation of any binding or cell assay.

This page sets out what the published record says, what a batch certificate should show, and how a buyer can avoid receiving a different tetrapeptide under the same name.

## Why This Compound

The Khavinson bioregulator programme began in St Petersburg in the 1970s and produced a family of short peptides isolated from, or modelled on, tissue-specific extracts. Pancragen is the pancreatic member. The research premise is that short peptides can enter cells and interact with chromatin or transcription regulators in a way that is partly tissue-specific, and that this interaction can shift gene expression in aged tissue. The premise is unusual by the standards of mainstream pharmacology, and the literature is concentrated in a small number of journals, often in Russian, with modest sample sizes.

The most cited experimental paper on Pancragen describes a tetrapeptide with the sequence Lys–Glu–Asp–Trp-NH2. In streptozotocin-treated Wistar rats, oral Pancragen produced a hypoglycaemic effect, while intramuscular Pancragen normalised mesenteric capillary endothelial adhesion without changing capillary permeability. A follow-up study in pancreatic cell cultures reported that Pancragen increased expression of differentiation markers in acinar cells (Pdx1, Ptf1a) and islet cells (Pdx1, Pax6, Pax4, Foxa2, Nkx2.2) in both young and aged cultures. A separate line of work examined old rhesus monkeys and reported changes in glucose tolerance and endocrine pancreatic function.

Those are preclinical findings in animal and cell models. They do not establish a mechanism in humans, and the studies do not provide the kind of dose-response or pharmacokinetic data that would let a laboratory extrapolate to a new model. What they do provide is a set of endpoints — differentiation markers, glucose handling, capillary adhesion — that a researcher can use to design a replication. The [peptide classification guide](/blog/therapeutic-peptide-classification-a-2026-research-guide/) places the bioregulators alongside GHRH, GLP-1 and cosmetic peptides, and explains why the category is defined more by research tradition than by a shared receptor.

The naming problem is not incidental. A tetrapeptide with a tryptophan at the C-terminus has a 280 nm chromophore; the proline version does not. If a certificate reports a purity by UV at 280 nm for a peptide that has no aromatic residue, the method and the molecule are inconsistent. That single detail can tell a buyer which sequence the manufacturer actually made.

## What the Batch Record Should Show

The certificate should begin with the sequence, printed in full, including the C-terminal amide. For a tetrapeptide, the intact mass is not enough to distinguish Lys–Glu–Asp–Trp-NH2 from Lys–Glu–Asp–Pro: the two differ by a single residue, and a nominal mass to one decimal place could be read either way if the calculation is sloppy. Tandem MS sequencing, or a peptide map after trypsin or carboxypeptidase digestion, resolves the C-terminal residue directly.

The UV method should match the sequence. If the batch contains tryptophan, reverse-phase HPLC at 280 nm is informative and the certificate should show that wavelength. If the batch contains proline instead, 214 nm is the appropriate detection wavelength and a 280 nm purity claim is meaningless. Ask for the chromatogram with the wavelength printed in the method header, not just in the narrative.

The C-terminal amide deserves its own check. Amidation removes a hydroxyl relative to the free acid, a mass difference of about 0.98 Da. On a tetrapeptide, that is small in absolute terms but large relative to the total mass, and it changes the charge state and the peptide's behaviour in ion-exchange and LC-MS. A certificate that reports the free acid mass for an amidated product is internally inconsistent. The [molecular weight verification note](/blog/peptide-molecular-weight-verification-steps/) explains how to compare calculated and observed values without over-reading a single decimal place.

Purity by area normalisation is the next field. For a tetrapeptide made by solid-phase synthesis, the common impurities are truncation products, diastereomers from racemisation, and residual protecting-group adducts. The certificate should report the main peak and the significant impurities separately, with the integration method stated. A single "98.4%" line is a summary, not an impurity profile.

Counterion and water content determine how much peptide is in the vial. Pancragen is commonly supplied as the acetate salt, but TFA can remain from reversed-phase purification. The certificate should name the counterion and, where measured, give residual TFA. Karl Fischer water content and a peptide-content assay convert the 20 mg fill into peptide mass. Without them, a concentration calculated from fill weight can be off by a fifth or more.

Endotoxin testing is relevant if the material will contact primary cells. The certificate should state whether LAL was run and what limit applied. Sterility is a separate question: a lyophilized powder is not sterile, and a research-grade label is not a sterility claim. The [quality pillar](/peptide-quality-testing/) covers the distinction between release tests and a catalogue specification.

Finally, the batch record should name the synthesis site and the date. Bioregulators are often made in small batches by specialist suppliers, and the site that performs the synthesis may not be the company that sells the vial. A certificate without a site cannot be audited.

## Sourcing & Verification

Ask the sequence question first, in writing. "What is the full sequence, including terminal modifications?" A supplier who answers "Pancragen" has not answered. A supplier who answers "Lys-Glu-Asp-Trp-NH2" has given you something you can check against the literature and against the mass spectrum.

Ask why the catalogue says Pro. The discrepancy may be a copy error inherited from an older listing, or it may reflect a different product line. Either way, the answer tells you whether the supplier understands the molecule. The [manufacturer verification protocol](/blog/china-peptide-manufacturer-verification-a-2026-b2b-sourcing-protocol/) sets out the entity and site checks that should accompany the sequence question.

Ask for a batch-specific chromatogram at the wavelength that matches the sequence. A Pancragen certificate that shows only a 280 nm trace is plausible if the peptide contains tryptophan; a certificate that shows a 280 nm trace for a proline-containing peptide is a red flag. The [HPLC versus LC-MS note](/blog/hplc-vs-lc-ms-peptides/) explains what each method can and cannot establish.

Ask about the counterion and the peptide content. For a 20 mg vial, the difference between peptide mass and total powder mass is large enough to matter in cell culture. If the certificate gives only fill weight, ask for the peptide-content assay or a nitrogen determination.

Ask about the batch size and the age of the lot. Bioregulators can sit in inventory for long periods, and a lyophilized powder stored warm will slowly take up moisture. A retest date and a storage history are more useful than a purity number alone. The [sourcing pillar](/china-peptide-sourcing/) covers the document set for an international order.

One more check: the catalogue category. Pancragen is listed under Bioregulators, which is a broad shelf that includes peptides from several tissue series and several sequence families. Category membership does not confirm that two products are related. Read the sequence, not the shelf label.

## Handling & Storage

Store the dry powder at −20°C, desiccated, protected from light. Let the vial reach room temperature before opening to avoid condensation. A tetrapeptide is small and polar, and it will pick up water readily if the cap is opened while the vial is cold.

Pancragen should dissolve in water or phosphate-buffered saline. Add the solvent down the wall of the vial, then mix by gentle inversion. Avoid vortexing; a short peptide at a foam interface can adsorb to the glass and disappear from the solution. If the powder does not dissolve completely, inspect the vial against a light source before adding more solvent. Undissolved particles are easier to see than to filter out later.

For cell work, prepare a concentrated stock in water or buffer and dilute into the culture medium. Keep the final buffer concentration as low as the assay tolerates, and record the pH. Tetrapeptides have no secondary structure to denature, but they can aggregate at high concentration or at interfaces, and the aggregate may not be visible.

Aliquot before freezing. Aqueous solutions are short-term stocks at 2–8°C; repeated freeze-thaw cycles promote aggregation and adsorption. Divide into single-use portions, freeze at −20°C or below, and do not refreeze. The [reconstitution guide](/blog/peptide-lyophilization-reconstitution-guide-for-researchers/) covers the general workflow, and the [handling pillar](/peptide-handling-protocols/) covers the storage schedule.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is Pancragen the same as Prostamax?**

No. Prostamax is the prostate-series tetrapeptide Lys–Glu–Asp–Pro. Pancragen is the pancreatic-series peptide, reported in the primary literature as Lys–Glu–Asp–Trp-NH2. The similar names and the shared Khavinson origin make them easy to confuse, but they are different sequences.

**Why does the catalogue list Lys–Glu–Asp–Pro?**

The older catalogue description used the proline sequence, which appears to have been carried over from the prostate peptide. The primary pancreatic paper specifies the tryptophan version with a C-terminal amide. Buyers should ask the manufacturer which sequence the batch follows and check the certificate against the answer.

**What is a peptide bioregulator?**

A short peptide, usually two to four residues, studied for tissue-specific effects on gene expression and cell differentiation. The concept comes from Russian gerontology research and is not the same as a receptor agonist or an enzyme inhibitor. The evidence base is mostly preclinical and concentrated in a small number of laboratories.

**How should I store Pancragen?**

At −20°C, desiccated and protected from light. Bring the vial to room temperature before opening. Reconstituted solutions are short-term stocks at 2–8°C and should be aliquoted before freezing.

**What solvent should I use?**

Water or phosphate-buffered saline for most assays. Add the solvent gently and mix by inversion. Avoid vortexing and heating. If a higher concentration is needed, a small amount of DMSO can be used, but keep the final organic content low and run a vehicle control.

**How do I confirm the C-terminal amide?**

Compare the observed mass with the calculated mass for the amidated sequence and the free acid. The difference is about 0.98 Da. Tandem MS or enzymatic digestion can locate the C-terminus directly. A certificate that reports only a nominal molecular weight has not confirmed it.

**Is the product sterile?**

No. Lyophilized research peptides are not sterile by default. If sterile material is required, filter the reconstituted solution through a 0.22 µm membrane and verify that the peptide does not adsorb to the filter. The [sterility guide](/blog/sterility-testing-for-research-peptides-a-protocol-guide/) covers the options.

**Can Pancragen be used in cell culture?**

The published studies used pancreatic cell cultures and animal models. For a new cell system, run a vehicle control and a concentration-response pilot before committing to a full design. The catalogue's purity specification is not a cell-culture qualification.
