## Why This Compound

The Khavinson programme produced a series of short peptides associated with particular tissues. Prostamax is the prostate-associated member, a tetrapeptide with the sequence Lys–Glu–Asp–Pro. The research framework proposes that such peptides interact with chromatin and change the accessibility of genes in a tissue-associated way. The experiments that support the framework are mostly in vitro and often use lymphocytes rather than the tissue of origin, which is a limitation worth stating plainly.

A 2012 study in *Georgian Medical News* examined the effect of Prostamax on chromosomes in lymphocytes from donors aged 75–86. The paper reports an increase in sister chromatid exchanges, an increase in Ag-positive nucleolar organiser regions, and a reduction in large pericentromeric heterochromatin segments. The authors interpreted those changes as decondensation of chromatin, releasing genes that had been repressed by heterochromatinisation. The result is a cytogenetic observation, not a demonstration of gene-specific regulation, and the sample is small.

A 2004 study in *Biofizika* used differential scanning calorimetry to examine the effect of Prostamax on chromatin in human lymphocytes. The paper reports a redistribution of heat among endotherms and a shift of two endotherms to lower temperatures, which the authors associated with partial relaxation of the 30 nm fibre. That is a physical-chemistry measurement of chromatin organisation, not a functional assay.

A 2006 study in *Advances in Gerontology* examined the tissue-specific effect of synthetic peptides in organotypic tissue cultures from young and old rats. The endpoints were morphological, based on explant outgrowth, and the peptides were tested across several tissue types. The paper is part of the same research programme and shares its limitations: small numbers, morphological readouts, and a model system that is several steps removed from a human tissue.

The 2017 paper on peptides in long- and short-lived rodents is a comparative biology study, not a Prostamax activity study. It is useful for understanding the framework's assumptions about peptide regulation and lifespan, but it should not be read as evidence for this specific tetrapeptide.

## What the Batch Record Should Show

The certificate should print the sequence, the calculated mass and the observed mass. For a tetrapeptide, the intact mass is a useful identity check, but it is not sufficient on its own: a sequence rearrangement with the same composition can produce the same mass. Tandem MS or a peptide map is more informative, and for a peptide this small, a full sequence read is feasible.

The C-terminus deserves a specific check. The catalogue does not state whether the peptide is the free acid or the amide. The literature on the pancreatic peptide specifies an amide, and the prostate peptide may be supplied either way. The difference is about 1 Da, and it changes the charge state and the chromatographic behaviour. Ask the supplier which form the batch is, and check that the certificate's mass matches.

Purity by HPLC should be run at 214 nm. Prostamax has no aromatic residue, so a 280 nm method is inappropriate. The certificate should show the column, the gradient, the wavelength and the integration parameters. If the peptide elutes near the solvent front, the main peak may co-elute with salts and the purity may be overstated. A method with a shallow gradient or a polar-embedded column is more informative for a highly polar tetrapeptide.

Counterion and water content are the mass-balance fields. The peptide is commonly supplied as the acetate salt, and residual trifluoroacetate from purification is possible. The certificate should name the counterion and, where measured, report residual TFA. Karl Fischer water content and a peptide-content assay convert the 20 mg fill into peptide mass. Without them, a concentration calculated from fill weight can be off by a meaningful margin.

Endotoxin testing matters if the material will contact primary cells. Ask whether LAL was run and what limit applied. Sterility is a separate test; a lyophilized powder is not sterile by default. The [quality pillar](/peptide-quality-testing/) explains why these are separate release decisions.

Batch traceability closes the record. The lot number on the certificate should match the vial, and the certificate should name the synthesis site and the date. For a bioregulator sold under a name that is easily confused with another product, traceability is also a naming safeguard.

## Handling & Storage

Store the dry powder at −20°C, desiccated and protected from light. Bring the vial to room temperature before opening. A cold vial in a warm room condenses water on the glass, and a polar tetrapeptide will take up moisture quickly once the cap is removed.

Prostamax dissolves readily in water and in phosphate-buffered saline. Add the solvent down the wall of the vial and mix by gentle inversion. Avoid vortexing; a small peptide at a foam interface can adsorb to the glass, and the loss is proportionally larger when the total mass is small. If the solution is cloudy, let it stand for a few minutes before adding more solvent.

For stock solutions, use water or the buffer your assay requires. The peptide has two carboxylates and one basic residue, so its charge state changes with pH. Record the pH and the buffer. A shift of one pH unit can change the peptide's interaction with a surface or a binding partner, and the effect is larger for a short peptide with few ionisable groups.

The catalogue does not specify an aqueous storage window for Prostamax beyond the general −20°C recommendation. A reasonable default is to keep reconstituted solutions at 2–8°C for short-term use and to aliquot and freeze for longer storage. Do not refreeze. Reconstitution mechanics are covered in the [reconstitution guide](/blog/peptide-lyophilization-reconstitution-guide-for-researchers/), and the [handling pillar](/peptide-handling-protocols/) covers the wider workflow.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is the sequence of Prostamax?**

Lys–Glu–Asp–Pro, a four-residue peptide from the Khavinson prostate series. The sequence is printed in the primary literature and is the best way to distinguish Prostamax from the pancreatic peptide Pancragen.

**Is Prostamax the same as Pancragen?**

No. Prostamax is Lys–Glu–Asp–Pro. Pancragen is the pancreatic-series peptide, reported in the primary literature as Lys–Glu–Asp–Trp-NH2. The names are similar and the catalogue copy has sometimes mixed them, but they are different molecules.

**Why can't I measure Prostamax at 280 nm?**

Because the peptide has no tryptophan, tyrosine or phenylalanine. It has no aromatic chromophore, so it does not absorb at 280 nm. Purity should be measured at 214 nm or by a method that detects the peptide backbone.

**How should I store the powder?**

At −20°C, desiccated and protected from light. Bring the vial to room temperature before opening. Reconstituted solutions should be kept at 2–8°C for short-term use and aliquoted before freezing for longer storage.

**What solvent should I use?**

Water or phosphate-buffered saline. The peptide is polar and dissolves readily. Add the solvent gently, mix by inversion, and avoid vortexing. Record the pH, because the charge state changes with pH.

**Is the product sterile?**

No. Lyophilized research peptides are not sterile by default. If sterile material is required, filter the reconstituted solution through a 0.22 µm membrane and verify that the peptide does not adsorb to the filter. The [sterility guide](/blog/sterility-testing-for-research-peptides-a-protocol-guide/) covers the options.

**Can I use Prostamax in cell culture?**

The published studies used lymphocyte cultures and organotypic tissue cultures. For a new system, run a vehicle control and a concentration-response pilot before committing to a full design. The catalogue purity specification is not a cell-culture qualification.

**Why is the sequence sometimes written with a C-terminal amide?**

Some bioregulator peptides are supplied as the C-terminal amide, which is more stable to exopeptidase attack. Others are supplied as the free acid. The catalogue does not specify for Prostamax, so ask the supplier which form the batch is and check the mass.

## The Author's Take

**Position.** Prostamax is a well-defined tetrapeptide, but the name is not a specification. I would confirm the Lys–Glu–Asp–Pro sequence and the C-terminal form before ordering, especially because the catalogue has confused this sequence with Pancragen.

**Reasoning.** The bioregulator category has a naming problem: several products share a style of name, a tissue association and a research tradition, and the catalogue copy has not always kept the sequences straight. For a tetrapeptide, a one-residue error is easy to make and hard to see in a purity number. The fix is cheap: ask for the sequence and a 214 nm chromatogram, and check that the mass matches. A supplier who can provide both is selling a defined molecule. A supplier who cannot is selling a name.
