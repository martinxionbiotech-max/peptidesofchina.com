PT-141, whose international nonproprietary name is bremelanotide, is a cyclic heptapeptide and melanocortin receptor agonist supplied as a lyophilized powder for laboratory research. It is a metabolite-analogue of Melanotan-2, modified by removal of the C-terminal amide and closure of a lactam bridge between the aspartic acid and lysine side chains. Those two changes alter receptor selectivity and, more importantly for a buyer, they create specific analytical risks: a ring that can hydrolyse, a D-phenylalanine that can epimerise, and an aspartimide pathway that appears at neutral and alkaline pH. This page explains what a batch record should prove and why the cheapest vial is often the least documented.

## Why This Compound

The melanocortin system consists of five G-protein-coupled receptors, MC1R through MC5R, and a family of peptide ligands derived from pro-opiomelanocortin. Most research peptides in this space are blunt instruments: they activate several receptors at once, which makes it hard to attribute an effect to a single subtype. PT-141 is interesting because its selectivity profile is skewed toward MC3R and MC4R, with much lower MC1R activity than Melanotan-2. That skew is the basis for its use as a pharmacological tool in CNS and inflammatory research.

MC4R is expressed in hypothalamic and limbic circuits, and animal studies using receptor knockouts have been used to link MC4R activation to specific behavioural and autonomic endpoints. MC3R is expressed in macrophages, dendritic cells, and endothelial cells, and a separate literature examines melanocortin agonism in inflammatory signalling. The two lines are often conflated in supplier copy. They are not the same research programme, and a study designed around one is not automatically informative about the other.

PT-141 also has an unusually deep clinical record for a research peptide. Its development programme reached Phase 3, and the trial reports are public. That record is useful for understanding pharmacokinetics and adverse-event profiles in humans, but it is not a substitute for the preclinical literature, and it says nothing about the identity of the vial in your freezer. One widely cited 2008 trial report was retracted, which is a reminder that citation counts in this area include material that should not be counted.

For sourcing, the chemistry is the useful part. The lactam bridge is formed between the side chains of Asp and Lys, creating a cyclic core. The N-terminus is acetylated, and the sequence includes a D-phenylalanine, which is not a natural proteogenic residue in the same sense as the others. Each of those features is a potential point of failure in synthesis, purification, and storage.

The sequence has one tryptophan, which is the UV chromophore used for detection and the residue most likely to oxidise. It has one aspartic acid, which can cyclise to an aspartimide under acidic or basic stress and then hydrolyse to iso-aspartate or aspartate. And it has a D-phenylalanine, which can epimerise during synthesis or under prolonged exposure to base. The first two problems change mass; the third does not. That is why a mass spectrum alone cannot fully characterise this peptide.

## What the Batch Record Should Show

**Identity by mass.** The monoisotopic mass of the free base is near 1024.5 Da, and the singly protonated ion appears near m/z 1025.5. The certificate should show a deconvoluted mass consistent with the cyclic sequence and, ideally, MS/MS fragments that cover the ring. A linear precursor has the same mass as the cyclised product minus the elements of water, so mass alone does not prove that cyclisation occurred. The difference is 18 Da, which is large enough to see if the method resolves it, but many certificates report only the final mass.

**Ring integrity.** Lactam hydrolysis opens the cycle and adds 18 Da. The resulting linear peptide may retain receptor binding but usually loses potency and stability. Ask whether the chromatogram shows a peak at +18 Da and how it was integrated. If the purity is reported as 99% with no impurity table, the question has not been answered.

**Aspartimide and iso-aspartate.** Aspartimide formation is pH- and temperature-dependent and is a classic degradation route for Asp-containing peptides. It changes the mass by −18 Da when the succinimide forms, and the subsequent hydrolysis produces iso-aspartate with the same mass as the parent. A certificate that reports only one impurity peak may be collapsing these species.

**Diastereomer content.** D-Phe is part of the intended sequence, but epimerisation during synthesis can produce the L-Phe analogue, which has the same mass and often a close retention time. Reversed-phase HPLC can resolve some diastereomers; chiral analysis is rarely offered. For a research tool, the practical check is whether the main peak is symmetric and whether the method has a stated resolution criterion. A certificate that reports "≥98%" without a chromatogram image is not enough to judge.

**Purity, counterion, and water.** The catalogue specification is ≥98% by area at 214 nm. The counterion is usually acetate or trifluoroacetate, and the certificate should name it. Water content by Karl Fischer and peptide content by nitrogen or amino acid analysis convert fill weight into peptide mass. Without those values, "10 mg" is a statement about the vial, not the molecule. The [quality-testing overview](/peptide-quality-testing/) explains how these measurements fit together, and the [counterion note](/blog/role-of-counterions-in-peptide-stability/) covers the salt-form question.

**Batch linkage and endotoxin.** The batch number on the certificate should match the vial label, and the document should carry a synthesis date and retest date. If the material will contact cells, ask for LAL endotoxin data; sterility is a separate test.

## Sourcing & Verification

PT-141 is a peptide where the difference between a good and a poor batch is visible in the impurity profile, not in the headline purity. These questions are worth asking before a first order.

- Does the certificate print the full cyclic sequence, including the lactam bridge and the D-Phe configuration? A supplier who omits stereochemistry is not describing the molecule precisely.
- Is the reported mass the free base or a salt, and does the spectrum show the cyclic product rather than the linear precursor?
- Is there an impurity table with retention times and relative areas, or only a single purity figure?
- Was the method capable of resolving the +18 Da ring-opened species and the diastereomer?
- What is the peptide content per vial, and by which method?
- Is residual TFA or residual solvent reported? Lactam cyclisation and purification can leave both.
- Is there LAL endotoxin data for this lot, if cells will be exposed?
- Can the supplier provide a certificate for a lot that has already shipped, not a specimen document?

Names and synonyms cause confusion here. PT-141, bremelanotide, and PL-6983 refer to the same compound in different contexts. Melanotan-2 is a different molecule with a different sequence and a different receptor profile; a listing that treats them as interchangeable is wrong on chemistry. The [Melanotan-2 page](/catalog/melanotan-2/) sets out that comparison.

One documentation habit is worth adopting: ask for the certificate and the label of the same lot, then compare the peptide content with the fill weight. If the label says 10 mg and the certificate accounts for 7 mg of peptide plus 2 mg of counterion and 1 mg of water, the numbers are coherent. If the certificate has no peptide-content value, the label is unverifiable. ## Handling & Storage

Lyophilized PT-141 is stored at −20°C, protected from light and moisture. The reconstituted solution is less forgiving than the powder.

Allow the sealed vial to reach room temperature before opening. The powder is hygroscopic, and condensation introduces water that can drive hydrolysis. Weigh or reconstitute promptly and reseal the vial if it is not used in one session.

Choose the solvent for the assay rather than by habit. Water and phosphate-buffered saline are common; avoid alkaline buffers, because the aspartimide pathway is base-catalysed. If the assay requires a pH outside the mild range, run a short stability check first rather than assuming the peptide will tolerate it. Add solvent gently down the wall and swirl; do not vortex, since the cyclic peptide can aggregate at the air-liquid interface.

Freeze single-use portions at −80°C when the stock will be kept beyond a few days. Working solutions at 2–8°C should be used within days, not weeks. Repeated freeze-thaw cycles promote aggregation and ring opening. Protect from light, because tryptophan is photo-oxidisable.

If the assay needs a sterile solution, filter through a 0.22 µm membrane and check recovery. Cyclic peptides can adsorb to some filter materials, and the loss may be compound-specific. Run a vehicle control with the same buffer and filter so that the buffer is not confused with the peptide effect. The [solubility troubleshooting note](/blog/peptide-solubility-troubleshooting/) covers the common failure modes, and the [handling hub](/peptide-handling-protocols/) covers the wider bench workflow.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is PT-141?**

PT-141 is the research name for bremelanotide, a cyclic heptapeptide that acts as an agonist at melanocortin receptors, with higher activity at MC3R and MC4R than at MC1R. It is supplied as a lyophilized powder for laboratory research.

**How does PT-141 differ from Melanotan-2?**

Melanotan-2 is a cyclic heptapeptide with a C-terminal amide and broader melanocortin receptor activity, including MC1R. PT-141 lacks the C-terminal amide and has a lactam bridge between Asp and Lys, which changes its selectivity and its analytical profile. They are different molecules, not different grades of the same product.

**What purity should a PT-141 batch meet?**

The catalogue floor is ≥98% by area at 214 nm. For this peptide, the impurity profile matters as much as the headline number: ring-opened species at +18 Da and diastereomers can be present without appearing in a single purity figure.

**Why is the lactam bridge important?**

The bridge closes the peptide into a ring and is part of the intended structure. Hydrolysis opens the ring and adds 18 Da, which can reduce stability and change receptor behaviour. A certificate should show whether the ring-opened species was resolved.

**How should PT-141 be stored?**

Lyophilized powder at −20°C, protected from light and moisture. Reconstituted solution at 4°C for short-term use, or aliquoted at −80°C for longer storage. Avoid alkaline pH and repeated freeze-thaw cycles.

**Is PT-141 soluble in water?**

It is sufficiently soluble for typical assay work, but solubility depends on pH and buffer. Prepare a concentrated stock in water or buffer, inspect for cloudiness, and dilute into the assay medium. Do not assume that a clear solution at one pH will remain clear at another.

**Can PT-141 be used in cell-based assays?**

It is supplied for laboratory research, and cell-based receptor assays are a common application. Endotoxin and sterility are separate release tests; confirm whether they were performed before planning a primary-cell experiment.

**Why do some citations for PT-141 look inconsistent?**

The literature includes retracted work, and citation counts can include it. The 2008 retraction is one example. Read the trial reports and the preclinical papers directly rather than relying on a supplier summary or a citation count.

## The Author's Take

**Position.** I would not buy PT-141 from a supplier who cannot show an impurity table. For a cyclic peptide with a D-amino acid and an aspartic acid in the sequence, the headline purity is the least informative number on the certificate.

**Reasoning.** The molecule has three failure modes that a single HPLC percentage can hide: a hydrolysed lactam at +18 Da, an aspartimide/iso-aspartate pathway that changes mass by −18 Da, and a diastereomer that has the same mass as the intended product. A supplier who understands the chemistry will show the impurity profile and the method. A supplier who does not will show a clean number and a generic chromatogram. The difference matters most when an assay is repeated months later with a new lot and the baseline has moved. Documentation is how you find out whether the move is biological or chemical.
