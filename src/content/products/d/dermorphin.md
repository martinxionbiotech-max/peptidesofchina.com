## Why This Compound

The peptide was isolated in 1981 from the skin of *Phyllomedusa sauvagei*, a South American tree frog, by Montecucchi and colleagues. Frog skin is a chemical warehouse: it contains host-defence peptides, antimicrobial sequences, and a family of opioid-like peptides that bind mammalian receptors with unusual selectivity. Dermorphin was the first of that family to be sequenced, and the D-alanine was immediately conspicuous. A vertebrate ribosome does not normally produce D-amino acids; the frog makes the peptide through a post-translational step that epimerises the residue.

That detail explains the compound's research career. Peptidases in tissue homogenates and plasma recognise L-amino acid bonds. A D-alanine at position two blocks the first cut, so dermorphin survives in biological media for far longer than an equivalent all-L sequence. For a binding assay that is convenient. For a cell-culture experiment it changes the exposure profile, and for anyone comparing literature results it is a variable that has to be controlled.

The receptor target is the μ-opioid receptor, a class A G-protein-coupled receptor. Dermorphin behaves as a high-affinity agonist in radioligand competition assays and in functional readouts such as GTPγS binding and cAMP inhibition. It is used as a reference ligand when a study needs a MOR-selective peptide that is not one of the standard synthetic enkephalin analogues. The published literature also uses it in biased-agonism work, where the question is whether a ligand favours G-protein signalling over β-arrestin recruitment, and in receptor-internalisation studies where the fate of the occupied receptor is the endpoint.

Two cautions belong in the same paragraph. First, most of the mechanistic work is in recombinant cell lines and rodent tissue; the concentration of evidence is not the same as breadth of evidence. Second, the compound's name is sometimes used loosely for synthetic analogues with modified C-termini or substituted residues. A catalog entry that says "dermorphin" and a paper that says "[D-Arg², Lys⁴]dermorphin-(1-4) amide" are describing different molecules. The [peptide categories overview](/peptide-categories-mechanisms/) is a useful map of where this ligand sits relative to other receptor-targeted peptides.

The C-terminal amide is part of the molecule, not a purification artefact. It removes the terminal carboxylate charge and is one of the features that distinguishes the natural peptide from a truncated fragment. A supplier who ships the free acid instead of the amide has shipped a different compound with a mass 1 Da lower and a different charge state, and the error will not be obvious on a label.

The sequence has three aromatic residues — two tyrosines and one phenylalanine — so it absorbs at 280 nm as well as at 214 nm. That is convenient for detection but not for purity assessment: at 280 nm the signal comes from the aromatics, and a deletion sequence that removes one of them will look like a large impurity while a sequence that keeps all three may co-elute unnoticed. Purity for this peptide should be reported at 214 nm, where the peptide bond absorbs, with the wavelength stated on the certificate.

Dermorphin is moderately hydrophobic for a seven-residue peptide because of the aromatic side chains and the proline. It dissolves in water at modest concentrations, better in dilute acetic acid, and is often prepared as a stock in water or buffer containing a small amount of acetonitrile for binding assays. The exact solvent matters less than consistency: changing from water to 10% acetonitrile can shift the apparent concentration if the peptide is not fully dissolved, and undissolved peptide is a common source of "failed" experiments that are really preparation errors. ## What the Batch Record Should Show

A dermorphin certificate has to answer a harder question than most peptide certificates. Identity is not just the mass; it is the mass plus the stereochemistry.

**Identity by mass.** The average mass of the free peptide is 802.9 Da. In electrospray ionisation the singly protonated ion appears near m/z 804, with the doubly charged ion near m/z 402. Sodium and potassium adducts are common and should be visible rather than suppressed. A certificate that prints the theoretical mass without a spectrum has restated the catalogue, not measured the batch. The [CoA reading guide](/blog/how-to-read-a-peptide-coa-a-researchers-guide-to-hplc-and-ms-reports/) and the [molecular-weight verification note](/blog/peptide-molecular-weight-verification-steps/) explain how to check the arithmetic.

**Stereochemistry.** This is the field most certificates omit. Mass spectrometry cannot distinguish D-alanine from L-alanine: the two are isobaric and fragment identically. Reversed-phase HPLC usually cannot resolve them either, because the chiral centre is remote from the residues that dominate hydrophobic interaction. To establish that the D-residue is present, a supplier needs either chiral amino acid analysis after hydrolysis — derivatisation with a chiral reagent followed by GC or LC — or a technique such as NMR with a chiral shift reagent. Very few catalog certificates include this. A buyer who cares about the distinction should ask for it explicitly and expect a separate report rather than a line on a standard CoA.

**Purity by HPLC with method detail.** Ask for column chemistry, mobile phase, gradient, wavelength, and integration threshold. At 214 nm, area normalisation reports the main peak as a share of UV-absorbing material; it does not see acetate, trifluoroacetate, water, or inorganic salts. That limitation is the subject of the note on [why purity and concentration tests are not enough](/blog/why-purity-and-concentration-tests-are-not-enough/). For a peptide this size, the common impurities are deletion sequences, which differ by one residue and may elute close to the main peak. A shallow gradient over a C18 column resolves more of them than a ten-minute generic run.

**Counterion and residual acid.** Dermorphin is typically supplied as the acetate or trifluoroacetate salt. The counterion changes the mass of material in the vial and can matter in cell-based work; residual TFA in particular is biologically active at sufficient concentration. The certificate should name the salt form and, where available, report residual TFA. The [counterion note](/blog/role-of-counterions-in-peptide-stability/) explains why this is a formulation variable rather than a housekeeping detail.

**Peptide content, water, and appearance.** Karl Fischer titration gives water content. Nitrogen analysis or amino acid analysis gives peptide content. Both are needed to convert a fill weight into a molar amount, and both drift if the vial has been opened. The cake should be a white to off-white plug with no shrinkage or discoloration, and the batch number on the certificate must match the label.

**Endotoxin and bioburden.** If the peptide will contact primary cells or be used in an animal model, endotoxin is a relevant specification. A standard chemical-purity CoA does not cover it. Ask for a limulus amoebocyte lysate result in EU/mg and a bioburden count, and treat a certificate that omits both as incomplete for that use. ## Handling & Storage

Lyophilized dermorphin is stable for long periods at −20°C when kept dry and away from light. The practical risks begin when the vial is opened.

Let the vial reach room temperature before breaking the seal. A cold surface condenses water from the air, and the powder is hygroscopic; moisture taken up at the bench is moisture that was not in the release certificate. Wipe the outside, wait twenty minutes, and work in the driest space available.

For binding assays, dissolve in water or the assay buffer, using dilute acetic acid if dissolution is slow. Avoid vortexing at high speed. Peptides with aromatic residues and a proline can aggregate at the air-liquid interface, and foam is a signal that the solution has been handled too roughly. Add solvent down the wall and swirl.

Dermorphin adsorbs to plastic and to some filter membranes, and the effect is proportionally larger at low concentrations. Use low-binding tubes for dilute stocks, avoid transferring through multiple containers, and check recovery if you filter. A 0.22 µm polyethersulfone filter may retain a meaningful fraction of a dilute peptide solution; a cellulose acetate membrane often performs differently, which is why filter choice should be fixed in a protocol rather than changed ad hoc.

Aliquot before freezing. Aqueous solutions are short-term working stocks at 2–8°C; repeated freeze-thaw cycles promote aggregation and adsorption. Divide into single-use portions and freeze at −80°C for long-term storage. Do not refreeze a thawed aliquot. Protect solutions from light, particularly if they will sit on a bench for hours; tyrosine and tryptophan side chains are photo-oxidisable, and dermorphin has two tyrosines.

## How It Compares

**DAMGO.** The standard synthetic MOR agonist in pharmacology. DAMGO is an enkephalin analogue with a modified N-terminus and a C-terminal alcohol; it is commercially available in high purity and behaves predictably in recombinant assays. Dermorphin is the natural-product alternative, with a different off-rate and a different literature. For a first binding assay, DAMGO is the easier reference; for a study that needs a peptide closer to a natural ligand, dermorphin is the more interesting choice. The two are not interchangeable in a curve-fitting exercise without re-deriving the constants.

**Deltorphins.** A second frog-skin peptide family, selective for the δ-opioid receptor rather than μ. The deltorphins also carry D-amino acids, and comparing them with dermorphin is a useful way to see how a single residue change redirects receptor selectivity. A supplier who lists "dermorphin/deltorphin" as one product is compressing two different pharmacology programmes.

**Endomorphin-1 and endomorphin-2.** Endogenous mammalian tetrapeptides with high MOR affinity. They are shorter than dermorphin, lack the D-alanine, and are degraded more quickly in tissue preparations. The contrast is instructive: the frog peptide's stability comes from a stereochemical trick that the mammalian peptides do not use.

**β-Endorphin.** A much larger opioid peptide derived from pro-opiomelanocortin, with broader receptor promiscuity. It is not a substitute for dermorphin in a MOR-selective assay, and the two are rarely compared directly in modern binding work.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is dermorphin the same as [D-Ala²]-dermorphin?**

Yes, in the sense that the natural sequence already contains D-alanine at position two, and the bracketed name simply makes the stereochemistry explicit. It is not the same as the many truncated or substituted dermorphin analogues in the literature, which have different sequences and different masses.

**Why does the sequence contain a D-amino acid?**

The frog produces the peptide through a post-translational epimerisation step, and the D-configuration at position two blocks the first cut by aminopeptidases. That gives the peptide a longer lifetime in tissue preparations than an all-L sequence, which is one reason it became a useful pharmacological tool.

**Can HPLC tell me whether the D-alanine is present?**

No. Reversed-phase HPLC separates molecules by hydrophobicity, and D- and L-alanine are isobaric and nearly identical in that respect. Establishing the configuration needs chiral amino acid analysis after hydrolysis, or another stereochemistry-sensitive method. A standard CoA almost never includes this, so ask for it separately if the distinction matters to your study.

**What should the mass spectrum show?**

The singly protonated ion near m/z 804 and the doubly charged ion near m/z 402, with sodium and potassium adducts visible. The exact values depend on the instrument and calibration, so a certificate should include the observed spectrum rather than a calculated number.

**What purity should the batch meet?**

The catalog standard is ≥98% by area normalisation at 214 nm. For this peptide the more useful question is whether the certificate includes a mass spectrum and any statement about stereochemistry, because purity alone does not establish identity.

**How is dermorphin supplied?**

As a lyophilized powder in sealed vials, 5 mg per vial in packs of ten for the current catalog format. The certificate should state peptide content rather than only fill weight.

**How should a stock solution be stored?**

Prepare a single-use stock, aliquot it, and freeze at −80°C. Keep working solutions at 2–8°C for short periods, use low-binding tubes, and avoid repeated freeze-thaw cycles.

**Is the research-use label enough for customs?**

No. Import rules for opioid-receptor ligands vary by country, and some authorities apply controls regardless of the label. Check local requirements before ordering.

## The Author's Take

**Position.** I would not buy dermorphin on the strength of a purity percentage. For this peptide, the certificate's most important line is the one that is usually missing: whether anyone checked the stereochemistry.

**Reasoning.** The entire research value of dermorphin rests on a D-alanine that mass spectrometry cannot see and reversed-phase HPLC cannot resolve. A supplier can synthesise an all-L sequence, purify it to 98%, and produce a CoA that looks identical to the real thing. The biological consequence would be large — the L-form is degraded quickly and binds differently — but the document would not reveal it. That asymmetry is unusual. For most peptides, a mass spectrum and a chromatogram together are strong evidence of identity. Here they are necessary but not sufficient. A supplier who understands the compound will mention the D-residue without being prompted, and will know that chiral analysis is a separate test. A supplier who answers "purity 99%, mass confirmed" has answered a different question.
