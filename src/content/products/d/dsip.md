DSIP, or delta sleep-inducing peptide, is a nine-amino-acid neuropeptide supplied as a lyophilized powder for laboratory research. It was isolated in 1977 from rabbit cerebral venous blood during slow-wave sleep, and the name has followed it ever since. The name is also the problem: it promises a function that later work has not settled, and supplier pages tend to repeat the promise while omitting the replication history. This page separates what the literature supports from what the catalogue copy implies, and sets out the batch data and handling conditions that matter for a nonapeptide with one tryptophan, one aspartic acid, and no positive charge.

## Why This Compound

The original observation was straightforward. Perfusing cerebral venous blood from sleeping rabbits into awake recipients produced behavioural and EEG signs of sleep, and a fraction of that blood was eventually reduced to a nonapeptide with the sequence Trp-Ala-Gly-Gly-Asp-Ala-Ser-Gly-Glu. Early papers reported increased sleep duration and altered EEG activity after peripheral or central administration in rabbits, rats, and mice. The peptide was also reported to cross the blood-brain barrier in animal models, which was unusual for a molecule of its size and polarity.

Then the story becomes less tidy. Replication was inconsistent, doses and routes varied between laboratories, and some effects were small or absent in controlled designs. A 2006 review by Kovalzon and Strekalova titled DSIP "a still unresolved riddle" is the clearest statement of the problem: the peptide exists, the early experiments are real, and the physiological role is still not established. Later work has examined neuroendocrine endpoints, including corticotropin and luteinising hormone release in stressed rodent models, and antioxidant or cytoprotective markers in cell culture. Those are separate lines of inquiry, and none of them turns DSIP into a characterised sleep regulator.

For a research buyer, the history is relevant because it shapes the market. DSIP is inexpensive to synthesize, has a memorable name, and carries a literature that is easy to cite selectively. A supplier can quote the 1977 paper and the 1983 sleep study without mentioning the replication problems. A researcher who knows the difference will design a study with appropriate controls and will not expect the peptide to behave like a validated drug. The [categories and mechanisms hub](/peptide-categories-mechanisms/) describes where DSIP sits among the other neuropeptides in this catalogue.

The chemistry is more tractable than the biology. The sequence has no cysteine, so there are no disulfide bonds to form or scramble. It has one tryptophan at the N-terminus, which is the UV chromophore and the main oxidation site. It has one aspartic acid followed by alanine, a motif that can isomerise to iso-aspartate under acidic or thermal stress. It has two acidic residues and no basic residues, so the net charge at neutral pH is negative, and solubility falls as the pH is lowered. Those features drive the analytical and handling notes below.

A 9-residue peptide is small enough that the counterion and water content can be a substantial fraction of the fill. It is also small enough that a single amino-acid substitution changes the mass by a large relative amount, which makes mass spectrometry a strong identity check. The weak point is not identity but degradation: oxidation at tryptophan and isomerisation at aspartate both produce species that can co-elute with the parent if the method is not designed to separate them.

## What the Batch Record Should Show

**Identity by mass.** The monoisotopic mass of the free base is near 848.3 Da, and the singly protonated ion appears near m/z 849.3. The certificate should show a deconvoluted mass and, ideally, a fragment pattern that covers the N-terminal tryptophan and the C-terminal glutamate. A reported "MW 848.9" without a spectrum is a catalogue value, not a measurement.

**Oxidation at tryptophan.** Trp oxidation adds 16 Da and is promoted by light, oxygen, and trace metals. The oxidised species often elutes just before the parent on a reversed-phase column, so a method with a steep gradient can merge them. Ask whether the impurity table includes a +16 species and how it was integrated.

**Aspartate isomerisation.** The Asp-Ala bond can form a succinimide intermediate, which hydrolyses to iso-aspartate or back to aspartate. The iso form has the same mass but a different retention time and can be difficult to separate. Stress conditions include heat, acidic pH, and prolonged storage in solution. A single impurity peak on the certificate may represent more than one species.

**Purity by HPLC, with the method attached.** The catalogue minimum is 98% by peak area at 214 nm. DSIP has one tryptophan, so 280 nm is possible but less sensitive; 214 nm is the better choice for a nonapeptide. Ask for the column, mobile phase, gradient, and integration parameters. Peak-area normalisation is blind to counterions, water, and inorganic salts.

**Counterion, water, and peptide content.** DSIP is commonly supplied as the acetate salt, and trifluoroacetate can remain from purification. The certificate should name the counterion and report water content by Karl Fischer and peptide content by nitrogen or amino acid analysis. For a 5 mg vial, the difference between fill and peptide content can be a milligram or more. The [counterion note](/blog/role-of-counterions-in-peptide-stability/) explains why the salt form is part of the specification rather than a footnote.

**Appearance and batch linkage.** The cake should be a white to off-white plug with no shrinkage or discoloration. The lot number on the certificate has to match the label on the vial, and the record should give a synthesis date plus a retest date. If the same document could ship with any DSIP lot, it is not a batch record.

## Sourcing & Verification

DSIP is sold in a part of the market where the name does a lot of work. The list below is short on purpose; each item has a documentary answer.

- Does the certificate print the full nine-residue sequence, or only "DSIP"? A supplier who cannot show the sequence is not describing a batch.
- Is the reported mass the free base or a salt? The counterion changes the fill and the mass.
- Was the chromatogram run on a gradient that resolves oxidised and iso-aspartate forms? Ask for the method and the impurity table.
- Does the certificate state peptide content per vial, and how was it measured?
- Is residual TFA reported? For cell-based assays, this is a real variable.
- Has the lot been tested for endotoxin if it will contact cells?
- Does the supplier describe DSIP as a sleep aid or a research reagent? The first description is outside the research-use frame and is a warning sign about the documentation.
- Can you see a certificate for a shipped lot rather than a sample?

The naming is relatively clean: DSIP, delta sleep-inducing peptide, and Deltaran refer to the same nonapeptide. The risk is not synonym confusion but category confusion. DSIP is sometimes grouped with selank, semax, and other neuropeptides under a "cognitive and neuro" heading, as if they were interchangeable tools. They are not. Selank and semax are different sequences with different research histories, and a study designed around one does not transfer to another.

## Handling & Storage

Lyophilized DSIP is stored at −20°C for long-term use, protected from light and moisture. The powder is hygroscopic, and the peptide is more stable dry than in solution.

Bring the sealed vial to room temperature before breaking the seal. A cold surface collects condensation, and that moisture drives both oxidation and aspartate isomerisation. Work quickly in dry air, and purge the headspace if the vial will be reopened.

For assay work, dissolve in sterile water or a neutral buffer. The peptide has two acidic residues and no basic residues, so it carries a net negative charge at neutral pH and its solubility falls as the pH is lowered toward the carboxyl pKa. A buffer at pH 6.5–7.5 is usually easier than water adjusted to an acidic pH. Add solvent gently and swirl; avoid vortexing, which creates an air-liquid interface that promotes aggregation.

Split the solution into single-use portions and hold them at −80°C. Working solutions at 2–8°C should be used within days to weeks, and the exact window should be established for the assay rather than assumed from a label. Repeated freeze-thaw cycles increase oxidation and can shift the iso-aspartate fraction. Keep the solution dark; the indole ring is light-sensitive.

If the assay requires sterile solution, filter through a 0.22 µm membrane and check recovery. Small anionic peptides can adsorb to some filter materials and to glass at low concentration; low-binding tubes reduce the loss. Run a vehicle control with the same buffer and filter so that buffer effects are not attributed to the peptide.

All handling described here is laboratory bench work. ## How It Compares

**Selank.** A synthetic heptapeptide derived from tuftsin, studied in stress-related and immune research models. It is not a DSIP analogue and does not share its sequence. The two appear together in neuropeptide catalogues because both are short and both have CNS research literatures, not because they are substitutes. See the [Selank page](/catalog/selank/).

**Semax.** A heptapeptide derived from ACTH(4-10), studied in neurotrophic and CNS research. Its sequence and proposed mechanisms differ from DSIP. A researcher comparing them should start from the receptor or pathway, not from the catalogue category. The [Semax page](/catalog/semax/) sets out its profile.

**PE-22-28.** A synthetic peptide studied in neuropathic pain and neuronal signalling models. It is a different molecule with a different literature. The comparison is useful mainly as a reminder that "neuropeptide" covers a wide range of sequences and mechanisms. See the [PE-22-28 page](/catalog/pe-22-28/).

**Melatonin.** Not a peptide, but often discussed alongside DSIP in circadian and sleep research. Melatonin has a defined receptor pharmacology and a large clinical literature; DSIP does not. A study that uses one as a positive control for the other is making an assumption that the literature does not support.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is DSIP?**

DSIP is a nine-amino-acid neuropeptide with the sequence Trp-Ala-Gly-Gly-Asp-Ala-Ser-Gly-Glu. It was isolated in 1977 from rabbit cerebral venous blood and has been studied in sleep neurophysiology, neuroendocrine, and antioxidant research models.

**Does DSIP cross the blood-brain barrier?**

Preclinical studies in the 1980s reported that radiolabelled DSIP crossed the blood-brain barrier in animal models, and the peptide's amphiphilic character is consistent with that observation. This is a research finding, not a property that has been established for every formulation or batch.

**Why is the sleep literature inconsistent?**

Early studies used different species, routes, doses, and EEG scoring methods, and later controlled designs did not always reproduce the original effects. A 2006 review described DSIP as an unresolved question. The peptide is real; the physiological role is not settled.

**What purity does a research-grade batch carry?**

The catalogue floor is 98% by area at 214 nm. For this peptide, the impurity profile matters: oxidised tryptophan and iso-aspartate can co-elute with the main peak if the gradient is not optimised.

**How should DSIP be stored?**

Lyophilized powder at −20°C, protected from light and moisture. Aqueous solutions at 2–8°C for short-term use, or aliquoted at −80°C for longer storage. Avoid repeated freeze-thaw cycles.

**Is DSIP soluble in water?**

It is soluble at neutral pH, where the two acidic residues carry negative charge. Solubility falls at acidic pH as the carboxylates are protonated. Prepare a stock at neutral pH, inspect for cloudiness, and match the vehicle in the control.

**How is DSIP supplied?**

As a lyophilized powder in sealed vials, typically 5 mg, 10 mg, or 15 mg per vial in 10-vial packs. The catalogue lists current formats and availability.

**Can DSIP be used in cell culture?**

It is supplied for laboratory research, and cell-based work is a common research application. Both are separate release tests, and both should be confirmed before a primary-cell study is designed.
