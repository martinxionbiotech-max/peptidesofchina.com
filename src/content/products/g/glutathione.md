## Why This Compound

Glutathione is the most abundant low-molecular-weight thiol in mammalian cells, present at millimolar concentrations in the cytosol, mitochondria, and nucleus. It is synthesised in two ATP-dependent steps: glutamate-cysteine ligase joins glutamate and cysteine through the γ-carboxyl, and glutathione synthetase adds glycine. The first step is rate-limiting and is inhibited by buthionine sulfoximine, which is why BSO is the standard tool for depleting cellular glutathione in research models.

The cysteine thiol is the functional centre. Glutathione donates electrons to glutathione peroxidase, which reduces hydrogen peroxide and lipid hydroperoxides to water and alcohols. It conjugates electrophiles through glutathione-S-transferases, a major route for xenobiotic detoxification. It maintains protein thiols in a reduced state, either directly or through glutaredoxin. And it is a cofactor for glyoxalase and for several prostaglandin and leukotriene pathways.

The oxidised form, GSSG, is a disulfide-linked dimer. The GSH/GSSG ratio is the standard redox indicator in cell and tissue research: a fall in the ratio indicates oxidative stress, and the ratio is used to compare conditions, genotypes, or treatments. The ratio is also a stability marker for the reagent itself. A vial of GSH that has oxidised during storage is not the same reagent as a fresh one, even if the peptide purity by area normalisation looks unchanged.

Research applications cluster in four areas. In oxidative-stress biology, GSH is used to buffer or deplete redox capacity and to measure the consequences. In hepatotoxicity models, the liver's high glutathione content and its role in detoxifying reactive metabolites make GSH a central variable; acetaminophen metabolism to NAPQI, which depletes glutathione, is the textbook example. In immunology, T-cell activation and proliferation are sensitive to intracellular glutathione, and macrophage oxidative burst is studied in relation to it. In neuroscience, glutathione depletion in the substantia nigra is an early biochemical finding in Parkinson's disease models.

None of that is a medical claim. The literature describes mechanisms and model systems; the reagent is a tool for interrogating them.

## Compound Profile

Chemical Class: Tripeptide thiol antioxidant (γ-glutamyl peptide); Synonyms: GSH, Glutathione reduced, γ-L-Glutamyl-L-cysteinyl-glycine, L-Glutathione; Molecular Formula: C₁₀H₁₇N₃O₆S; Molecular Weight: 307.3 Da; CAS Number: 70-18-8; PubChem CID: 124886; Form: Lyophilized powder (reduced form, GSH); Storage: −20°C long-term; protect from air and oxygen; 2-8°C aqueous (prepare fresh — GSH oxidises rapidly)

The sequence is γ-Glu-Cys-Gly, with a non-standard γ-peptide bond at glutamate rather than the usual α-linkage.

The γ-linkage is not a cosmetic variation. It means the peptide cannot be sequenced by standard Edman degradation, because the backbone does not present the free α-amino group in the expected position. It also means that proteases which cleave α-peptide bonds do not recognise glutathione; the enzyme that degrades it is γ-glutamyl transpeptidase, which removes the γ-glutamyl group and transfers it to an acceptor. For a supplier, the practical consequence is that identity is established by mass, by amino acid composition after hydrolysis, and by enzymatic assay rather than by N-terminal sequencing.

Glutathione has no aromatic residues, so it does not absorb at 280 nm. Purity must be measured at 214 nm, where the peptide bond absorbs, or by a thiol-specific method such as Ellman's reagent or HPLC with electrochemical detection. A certificate that reports a 280 nm purity for glutathione is measuring noise.

The molecule is highly water-soluble and poorly soluble in organic solvents. It is most stable at acidic pH, where the thiol is less ionised and less reactive; at neutral or alkaline pH, oxidation accelerates. Trace transition metals — copper, iron, manganese — catalyse the oxidation, which is why chelating agents are sometimes included in glutathione buffers.

## What the Batch Record Should Show

A glutathione certificate has one question at its centre: how much of the material is the reduced monomer, and how much has oxidised?

**Purity by HPLC at 214 nm, with the method described.** Column, mobile phase, gradient, wavelength, and integration parameters. A generic gradient can resolve GSH from GSSG, but the separation depends on pH and on the ion-pairing agent. The [CoA reading guide](/blog/how-to-read-a-peptide-coa-a-researchers-guide-to-hplc-and-ms-reports/) explains which fields to check.

**GSSG content as a specified impurity.** This is the item most certificates omit. Reporting "purity 98%" while not reporting the oxidised dimer is like reporting a protein concentration without saying whether it is aggregated. Ask for the GSSG value, or at least for a statement that the method measures it. The [HPLC versus LC-MS note](/blog/hplc-vs-lc-ms-peptides/) explains why a single-wavelength method is weak evidence for a mixture of redox states.

**Identity by mass and by amino acid composition.** The singly protonated ion appears near m/z 308, and the dimer near m/z 613. A mass spectrum confirms the molecular weight but not the γ-linkage; amino acid analysis after hydrolysis confirms the three residues but not the linkage either. The linkage is established by enzymatic digestion with γ-glutamyl transpeptidase or by NMR. A certificate that reports only a mass has confirmed the formula, not the connectivity.

**Water content and peptide content.** Glutathione is hygroscopic, and the fill weight includes water and counterion. Karl Fischer titration and nitrogen analysis or amino acid analysis convert the fill into a molar amount. A 1,500 mg vial that contains 1,300 mg of glutathione is a 13% error in every calculation.

**Counterion and pH.** Glutathione is often supplied as the free acid or as a sodium salt. The form matters for solubility and for cell-based work. The certificate should state it, along with the pH of a standard solution.

**Heavy metals.** Because trace metals catalyse oxidation, a heavy-metals specification is more relevant here than for a typical peptide. A certificate that reports lead, arsenic, and copper at low ppm is giving you information about the stability of the batch, not just its safety.

**Appearance and batch linkage.** A white to off-white powder with no discoloration. A yellow tint suggests oxidation. The batch number should match the label, and the certificate should carry a manufacture and retest date.

**Endotoxin and bioburden.** For cell-culture work, endotoxin is a relevant specification. A chemical-purity certificate does not cover it.

## Sourcing & Verification

Glutathione is a commodity chemical with a large global market, a long history of food and cosmetic use, and a wide range of grades. The research grade is a small segment of that market, and the documentation quality varies accordingly.

- Does the certificate report GSSG as a separate value, or only a total purity?
- Was the assay run at 214 nm, and is the method described?
- Is the identity confirmed by mass and by amino acid analysis?
- Is the γ-linkage addressed at all, or only the molecular formula?
- Is water content reported, and is peptide content given in mg per vial?
- Is the counterion named, and is the pH of a standard solution stated?
- Are heavy metals reported?
- Is endotoxin reported for cell-culture use?
- Is the certificate tied to the vial lot and to a retest date?
- Does the supplier distinguish reduced glutathione from oxidised glutathione and from glutathione esters?

That last question is important. GSH, GSSG, S-acetyl glutathione, and glutathione ethyl ester are different molecules with different membrane permeability and different assay behaviour. A listing that says "glutathione" without specifying the form is not a specification. The catalogue entry here is for the reduced form, and the certificate should say so.

The two variants in the listing — one with a purity figure and one without — are worth asking about. A variant without a purity specification may be a different grade, a different fill, or simply an incomplete listing. The buyer should not assume the two are interchangeable.

## Handling & Storage

Store the lyophilized powder at −20°C, protected from moisture and air. Equilibrate the vial to room temperature before opening; condensation is the main route to water uptake.

Prepare solutions fresh. GSH oxidises in aqueous solution, and the rate increases with pH, temperature, and trace metal contamination. Use degassed buffer where possible, and keep the solution on ice during use. If the assay runs for hours, prepare a fresh dilution rather than leaving the stock on the bench.

Avoid metal contact. Stainless-steel spatulas, metal-backed filters, and trace-metal-contaminated water can all accelerate oxidation. Use plastic labware and high-purity water. If a chelator is compatible with the assay, consider adding a small amount of EDTA to the buffer; if it is not, minimise the exposure time instead.

Do not vortex aggressively. Glutathione is small and does not aggregate like a protein, but foaming increases the air-liquid interface and promotes oxidation. Swirl to dissolve.

For cell-based work, prepare the stock at a concentration that allows a small dilution into the medium, and check the final pH and osmolality. Glutathione is acidic in solution, and a concentrated stock can shift the medium pH if it is added without buffering.

Aliquot dry powder only if the balance and the humidity allow it; otherwise keep the vial sealed and take the whole contents into solution at once. Repeated opening of a hygroscopic powder is a slow route to a concentration error.

The steps above are bench procedure. Glutathione is sold for laboratory research, not for administration to humans or animals. ## How It Compares

**GSSG (oxidised glutathione).** The dimeric form. It is used as a reference standard for the GSH/GSSG ratio and as a substrate in glutathione reductase assays. It is not a substitute for GSH in a reducing system, and a listing that does not distinguish the two is incomplete. See the note on [why purity and concentration tests are not enough](/blog/why-purity-and-concentration-tests-are-not-enough/).

**Glutathione ethyl ester.** A membrane-permeable derivative used when intracellular delivery is the goal. Extracellular GSH is taken up poorly by most cell types, so the ester is often preferred for cell-loading experiments. It is a different molecule with a different mass and a different stability profile.

**N-acetylcysteine.** A thiol antioxidant and a glutathione precursor. It is not glutathione, but it is often used in the same experiments as a comparator. The mechanisms overlap without being identical.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is the difference between reduced and oxidised glutathione?**

Reduced glutathione (GSH) has a free cysteine thiol and is the form that donates electrons. Oxidised glutathione (GSSG) is the disulfide-linked dimer formed after GSH has been oxidised. The GSH/GSSG ratio is the standard redox indicator, and the two forms are not interchangeable.

**Why is the purity measured at 214 nm?**

Because glutathione has no aromatic residues and does not absorb at 280 nm. The peptide bond absorbs at 214 nm, and thiol-specific methods such as Ellman's reagent or electrochemical detection can be used as orthogonal checks.

**Why does the certificate need a GSSG value?**

Because a purity percentage can stay high while a meaningful fraction of the material has oxidised to the dimer. The GSSG value is the direct measure of that change, and it is the specification that matters for redox experiments.

**Why must solutions be prepared fresh?**

GSH oxidises in aqueous solution, especially at neutral or alkaline pH and in the presence of trace metals. A solution left on the bench will not have the same GSH/GSSG ratio as one prepared immediately before use.

**Is glutathione a peptide?**

It is a tripeptide with a γ-glutamyl linkage rather than the usual α-peptide bonds. That structure makes it unrecognisable to standard proteases and to Edman sequencing, which is why its identity tests differ from those of a conventional peptide.

**How is it supplied?**

As a lyophilized powder in 1,500 mg vials, ten vials per pack. Two variants are listed; one carries a ≥98% HPLC specification and the other does not, so buyers should confirm the grade before ordering.

**How should it be stored?**

Lyophilized powder at −20°C, protected from moisture and air. Aqueous solutions are prepared fresh and kept cold and protected from light; they are not stored long-term.

**Is it appropriate for cell-culture experiments?**

It is supplied for laboratory research only. Suitability depends on endotoxin, counterion, pH, and the assay system. Extracellular GSH is taken up poorly by many cell types, so the choice of form matters for the experimental question.

## The Author's Take

**Position.** For glutathione, I would rather see a GSSG value than a purity percentage. The percentage is the least informative number on the certificate.

**Reasoning.** Glutathione's research value depends on its redox state. A batch that is 98% pure by area normalisation can still contain a meaningful fraction of the oxidised dimer, and the dimer is not a trace impurity in a redox experiment — it is the variable the experiment is trying to control. The analytical method that reports "purity" is usually measuring the main peak as a share of UV-absorbing material; it can miss the dimer if the gradient is short or the integration window is narrow. A supplier who reports GSSG, water content, heavy metals, and the counterion is describing the reagent. A supplier who reports one percentage is describing a label.
