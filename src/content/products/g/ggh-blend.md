GGH Blend is a five-component aqueous formulation supplied at 402 mg/mL in 10 mL ampoules. The label composition is GHK-Cu, glutathione, NADH, histidine, and glycine. It is filed under Skin, Hair & Nails and Vitamins & Nutrients, which is a merchandising decision; the research interest is the chemistry that happens when a copper-binding tripeptide, a thiol antioxidant, and a reduced pyridine nucleotide sit in the same solution.

That chemistry is not passive. Copper(II) oxidises thiols. Glutathione reduces copper(II) to copper(I) and is itself oxidised to glutathione disulfide. NADH can reduce both copper and oxidised glutathione. The vial therefore contains a redox system that will drift toward a new equilibrium after mixing, and the drift continues during storage. A certificate that reports the ingredients at fill is describing the starting point, not the state of the solution when you open it.

This page explains the components, the analytical panel that can characterise such a mixture, and the handling decisions that follow from its instability.

## Why This Compound

GHK-Cu is a tripeptide — glycyl-L-histidyl-L-lysine — complexed with copper(II). It was isolated from human plasma in the 1970s and has been studied for its effects on extracellular matrix turnover, fibroblast behaviour, and wound repair models. The copper is not a contaminant; it is part of the complex, and the peptide's affinity for Cu(II) is the reason the molecule is interesting. The histidine imidazole and the N-terminal amine provide the coordination geometry, and the resulting complex has a distinctive blue colour.

Glutathione is the cell's principal low-molecular-weight thiol. Its cysteine residue is a strong nucleophile and a reducing agent, and it exists in a dynamic equilibrium with its disulfide dimer, GSSG. In a mixture containing Cu(II), glutathione will reduce the metal and form GSSG. The reaction is fast enough to matter on the timescale of sample preparation, which is why copper–glutathione mixtures are used as model systems for oxidative stress.

NADH is the reduced form of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide. It carries electrons to the respiratory chain and is a cofactor for many dehydrogenases. In solution it oxidises to NAD⁺, particularly at acidic pH, in the presence of light, or in the presence of metal catalysts. Its absorption at 340 nm makes it easy to measure and easy to mistake for something else in a mixture.

Histidine and glycine are free amino acids. They contribute buffering capacity and metal coordination — histidine especially — and they are also breakdown products of peptide hydrolysis. Their presence in the formulation is not incidental: free histidine can compete with GHK for copper, and free glycine can affect osmolality and pH.

The name "GGH" invites a question. It could be read as the tripeptide Gly-Gly-His, which is itself a copper-binding sequence, or as an abbreviation of the formulation's components. The label composition is the authoritative description: GHK-Cu, glutathione, NADH, histidine, and glycine. A buyer who assumes the name refers to a single peptide is buying something different from what the label describes.

## What the Batch Record Should Show

A five-component formulation with a redox-active metal needs a panel that addresses both composition and speciation. The [quality-testing hub](/peptide-quality-testing/) explains why the usual single-analyte certificate does not apply, and the [CoA reading guide](/blog/how-to-read-a-peptide-coa-a-researchers-guide-to-hplc-and-ms-reports/) shows how the individual fields fit together.

**GHK-Cu identity and integrity.** The certificate should confirm the peptide sequence (Gly-His-Lys), the copper content, and the ratio of copper to peptide. A formulation that reports "GHK-Cu" without a copper assay has not shown that the complex is intact. Copper:peptide ratio is the key number; a ratio below 1:1 suggests free peptide or lost metal.

**Copper speciation.** Total copper by ICP-MS is necessary but not sufficient. The interesting question is how much copper is bound to the peptide and how much is free or bound to glutathione. Methods exist — UV-visible spectroscopy, circular dichroism, and HPLC with element-specific detection — but they are not routine. A supplier who can at least report total copper and the visible spectrum is ahead of one who reports neither.

**Glutathione with oxidation state.** GSH and GSSG should be reported separately. In a copper-containing solution, the GSH fraction will fall over time, and the rate depends on the copper speciation. A total-glutathione result hides the change that matters most. The [HPLC versus LC-MS note](/blog/hplc-vs-lc-ms-peptides/) explains why a single-wavelength method is weak evidence here.

**NADH and NAD⁺.** NADH absorbs at 340 nm and NAD⁺ does not, so the ratio is measurable by HPLC or by enzymatic assay. The certificate should state which species was measured. "NADH" reported as a weighed amount is a label claim, not an analytical result.

**Histidine and glycine.** Free amino acids can be measured by amino acid analysis or LC-MS. Their presence is also a marker of peptide hydrolysis: if free glycine and histidine are elevated relative to the label, some of the GHK may have degraded.

**pH and osmolality.** Copper speciation, thiol oxidation, and NADH stability all depend on pH. Osmolality matters for any cell-based dilution. Both should be measured.

**Appearance, colour, and particulates.** A blue solution is expected; a brown one is not. The certificate should describe the colour at release and state whether the fill was filtered.

**Sterility and endotoxin.** For cell-based work, endotoxin is the more relevant specification, but a multi-use aqueous ampoule should also carry a bioburden or sterility result.

**Batch linkage and fill volume.** The certificate must match the ampoule, and the fill volume should be stated.

The last question is the one that separates a formulator from a blender. Adding copper sulfate to a GHK solution produces the complex; adding that complex to a glutathione solution produces a redox reaction. ## Handling & Storage

Store at 2–8°C, protected from light and oxygen. Do not freeze. A concentrated formulation with a metal complex can precipitate on freezing, and the redissolved solution may not be homogeneous.

Ampoules are single-use. Inspect the solution before opening: a clear blue solution is expected; a brown, green-brown, or turbid solution indicates a chemical change. Discard rather than filter.

Open immediately before use and minimise headspace exposure. Glutathione and NADH both react with dissolved oxygen, and the copper in the formulation catalyses the reaction. A solution left open on the bench will not be the same solution an hour later.

For cell-based work, dilute in a buffer that matches the assay pH and check the final osmolality. Copper is toxic to cells above a threshold, and the threshold depends on the cell type and on how much of the copper is bound to the peptide. A dilution that is safe for one formulation may not be safe for another with a different copper speciation.

Do not autoclave. The components are heat-labile, and the copper–thiol system will react. If sterile solution is required, filter through a membrane that has been tested for peptide and copper recovery.

No. It is a five-component aqueous formulation. The peptide component is GHK-Cu, a copper-binding tripeptide complex.

**Why does copper matter in this formulation?**

Copper(II) oxidises thiols, and glutathione is a thiol. The copper–glutathione reaction changes both the metal speciation and the glutathione redox state. That is the central analytical challenge of the product.

## The Author's Take

**Position.** I would not buy a copper–thiol formulation from a supplier who cannot state the copper:peptide ratio. Everything else on the certificate is secondary to that number.

**Reasoning.** GHK-Cu is defined by its metal. A peptide solution with the right sequence but the wrong copper content is not the same reagent, and a formulation that also contains glutathione will redistribute the copper over time. The analytical panel needed to describe that system — total copper, bound copper, GSH/GSSG, NADH/NAD⁺ — is more demanding than the panel for a simple peptide, and most certificates do not attempt it. The result is a product whose label describes the ingredients at fill while the vial contains a different equilibrium at opening. A supplier who understands the chemistry will volunteer the copper ratio and the colour specification. A supplier who does not will describe the product as "GHK-Cu blend" and leave it there.
