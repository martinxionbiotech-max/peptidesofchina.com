GHK Basic is the copper-free form of the tripeptide glycyl-L-histidyl-L-lysine, supplied as a lyophilized powder for laboratory research. Catalogue pages often present GHK and GHK-Cu as two versions of one product. In solution they behave as two different materials: the copper complex has its own colour, stability profile and literature, and the apo-peptide is the control condition rather than a weaker version of the same thing. This page is about the copper-free material — how to confirm that a vial is actually copper-free, what the batch record should contain, and where the research use of GHK ends and the copper-peptide literature begins.

## Why This Compound

GHK is a three-residue peptide — glycine, histidine, lysine — with a molecular weight of 340.38 Da. It has no tryptophan, tyrosine or phenylalanine, so it has almost no useful absorbance at 280 nm. It carries two basic side chains and no acidic side chain, which makes it strongly cationic at neutral pH and gives it a tendency to associate with glass, plastic and anionic surfaces. Those two facts, low mass and positive charge, shape nearly every analytical and handling decision on this page.

The peptide was identified in human plasma and later studied for its ability to bind copper(II) and for its effects on connective-tissue cells in culture. The copper complex, GHK-Cu, became the better-known material. It is the form used in most of the fibroblast and extracellular-matrix experiments, and it is the form that appears most often in the formulation literature. The free tripeptide is what remains when the copper is removed. It is still a defined molecule with a defined sequence, but it is not the same reagent as the complex.

That distinction has practical consequences for study design. In a fibroblast culture, GHK-Cu introduces copper along with the peptide. Copper itself affects oxidative status, enzyme activity and matrix gene expression. A study that wants to attribute an effect to the peptide rather than to the metal needs a copper-free control, and GHK Basic is that control. A study that uses GHK Basic but cites copper-peptide results has left the main variable uncontrolled. The reverse error is equally common: a lab orders GHK-Cu expecting the apo-peptide and then wonders why the powder is blue.

The published work on the apo-peptide is smaller than the work on the complex. It includes binding studies, solution-structure work and a set of cell-culture experiments in which the free peptide is compared with the copper complex. The results are not uniform, and the differences between models are large enough that a single claim about what GHK "does" is not supportable. What the literature does provide is a clear chemical identity, a well-characterised copper-binding equilibrium, and a set of in vitro observations that can be reproduced or contradicted with a defined batch. That is the appropriate basis for a research listing.

For a sourcing decision, the important point is that "GHK Basic" describes a purity claim about the absence of copper as much as a purity claim about the peptide. A certificate that reports 98% peptide content by HPLC but says nothing about copper has not established that the material is the apo form. ## What the Batch Record Should Show

A GHK Basic batch record has to answer five questions, and the order matters because the first one is the one most often skipped.

**Copper content.** This is the defining assay for the product. Inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) or ICP optical emission spectroscopy can quantify copper at low parts-per-million levels in a peptide sample. The certificate should state the method, the sample preparation, and the result in µg per vial or mg/kg. A material sold as copper-free should be low enough that the copper contribution is negligible relative to the peptide concentration used in the experiment. If the certificate has no copper result, "Basic" is a label rather than a specification. A blue or green tint in the cake or solution is a crude visual warning, but colour is not an assay: a low level of copper can be invisible.

**Identity by mass.** The average mass of the free peptide is 340.38 Da. Electrospray ionisation in positive mode gives a protonated ion near m/z 341.4, with a sodium adduct near m/z 363.4 and, depending on the solvent, a small doubly charged signal near m/z 170.7. The isotope pattern for a molecule with six nitrogen atoms is distinctive. A certificate that prints the formula and molecular weight without a spectrum has quoted the catalogue, not measured the batch.

**Purity by HPLC at 214 nm.** The peptide bond absorbs at 214 nm, and that is the practical wavelength for a molecule with no aromatic chromophore. Request the column chemistry, mobile phase, gradient table and integration parameters. Because GHK is small and highly polar, it elutes early on a conventional C18 column unless an ion-pairing reagent is used. Deletion sequences missing one residue differ by 57 to 137 Da and may not be resolved by a short generic gradient. A single area-percent figure without the chromatogram is a weak specification.

**Counterion, water and peptide content.** GHK is usually supplied as the acetate salt. Trifluoroacetate can remain from reversed-phase purification. Both contribute mass, and both can affect cell-culture behaviour. Karl Fischer titration gives water content; nitrogen analysis or amino acid analysis gives peptide content. The fill weight on the label is not the peptide mass. For a 50 mg vial, the difference between fill weight and peptide content can be large enough to change a molar calculation.

**Appearance and batch linkage.** The lyophilized plug should be white to off-white and free of shrinkage or melting. The certificate's batch number should be the same one printed on the vial, and the document should carry a synthesis date plus a retest date. A certificate that could describe any GHK lot is a template.

## Sourcing & Verification

The first question for a GHK Basic quote is not the price per vial. It is whether the supplier can show a copper assay. A tripeptide this short is inexpensive to synthesize, and the market contains material that is chemically plausible but incompletely characterised. The common failure modes are a copper complex sold under a "GHK" label, a free peptide with residual copper from synthesis or containers, and a certificate that reports peptide purity while leaving the metal unmeasured.

Ask for the sequence in writing. Gly-His-Lys is short enough that a single-residue error produces a molecule with a different mass, and the certificate should show the sequence and the observed mass, not just the catalogue name. Ask for the counterion. Ask whether the batch was purified by preparative HPLC and whether the column or containers could have introduced copper. A supplier who cannot answer the metal question is not selling a characterised apo-peptide.

The second question is packaging. GHK is hygroscopic and strongly cationic. A 50 mg fill in a vial with a large headspace will pick up moisture each time it is opened, and the peptide will adsorb to some filter membranes and plasticware. Suppliers who understand the material ship it in sealed vials with desiccant and print a retest date. The third question is whether the product is being compared honestly with GHK-Cu. If the study needs the copper complex, buy the [GHK-Cu listing](/catalog/ghk-cu/) rather than adding copper to a basic batch. If the study needs the apo-peptide, keep copper out of the workflow: metal spatulas, stainless-steel contact and copper-containing buffers can all change the result. ## Handling & Storage

Keep the lyophilized powder at −20°C, away from light and moisture. Let the vial reach room temperature before opening it; condensation on a cold cake is a source of water uptake that no certificate can correct. Between uses, return the vial to a desiccator; repeated warming does the material no favours. A working aliquot can be prepared once and discarded rather than returned to the freezer.

Reconstitution should be slow. Run the solvent down the inside of the vial and rotate it gently; a vortex will whip air into the solution. GHK is highly soluble in water, but a cationic peptide can foam at an air-liquid interface, and foam is a sign that part of the material has unfolded or aggregated at the surface. If the solution is hazy, centrifuge at low speed and inspect before filtering. A 0.22 µm low-protein-binding filter is suitable, but check recovery with a UV assay if the concentration is quantitative; some membranes adsorb basic peptides.

Prepare aqueous solutions fresh. If a solution must be held, keep it at 2-8°C for the shortest possible time and protect it from light. If a stock must be kept, split it into single-use aliquots and freeze at −20°C or colder; thaw one aliquot per session. Avoid repeated freeze-thaw. Use low-bind tubes and avoid metal contact: a stainless-steel needle or spatula is a plausible source of trace copper, which is exactly the variable a copper-free study is trying to control.

**KPV.** A different tripeptide with a different sequence and a different research literature. It is often paired with GHK-Cu in catalogue blends, but it is not a copper-binding peptide and does not substitute for GHK in a metal-binding study. The KPV page lists the formats.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is GHK Basic the same as GHK-Cu?**

No. GHK Basic is the free tripeptide glycyl-L-histidyl-L-lysine. GHK-Cu is the copper(II) complex of the same peptide. They have different molecular masses, different colours and different solution behaviour, and the research literature rarely treats them as interchangeable.

**What sequence and molecular weight should the certificate show?**

The sequence is Gly-His-Lys, the free-base formula is C₁₄H₂₄N₆O₄, and the average molecular weight is 340.38 Da. The protonated ion in electrospray should appear near m/z 341.4.

**Why does copper content matter in a product called "Basic"?**

Because the name refers to the copper-free form. A peptide purity figure does not measure copper. If the certificate has no metal assay, the buyer has no evidence that the material is the apo-peptide rather than a partially copper-loaded batch.

**What purity does the catalogue list?**

The page states ≥98% by HPLC. The useful question is how that number was obtained: column, gradient, wavelength and integration. Ask for the chromatogram rather than the summary figure.

**Can I measure GHK at 280 nm?**

No. GHK has no tryptophan, tyrosine or phenylalanine, so it has no meaningful absorbance at 280 nm. Use 214 nm, where the peptide bond absorbs, or a charged-aerosol detector. The choice of wavelength belongs in the certificate.

**How should the lyophilized powder be stored?**

Keep the vial at −20°C in a desiccator and out of the light. Let it come to room temperature before you open it. Make working solutions on the day; if you must hold one briefly, keep it cold and dark.

**Is the product supplied as acetate or trifluoroacetate?**

The catalogue material is normally the acetate salt, but the counterion should be confirmed on the batch certificate. Residual trifluoroacetate from purification changes the mass and can affect cell-culture results.

**What pack sizes are available?**

The catalogue lists 50 mg per vial in 10-vial packs. Confirm the peptide content per vial, not just the fill weight, before preparing a nominal concentration.

## The Author's Take

**Position.** For a copper-free study, I would not accept an HPLC purity certificate as the only document. The copper assay is the product identity. A 98% pure peptide that contains trace copper is not the reagent the study needs.

**Reasoning.** Most of the well-known GHK literature uses the copper complex, and copper is not a passive passenger in those experiments. It changes redox chemistry, enzyme activity and matrix gene expression. A lab that orders GHK Basic to avoid that variable, then receives a batch with unmeasured copper, has imported the variable back into the study. The test is cheap and the result is unambiguous. The visual check is useful but incomplete: a blue cake is obviously wrong, while a low copper level can be invisible. Mass spectrometry confirms the peptide and says nothing about the metal. A supplier who can provide both a mass spectrum and a copper result is demonstrating control of the actual product; a supplier who provides only a purity number is demonstrating control of the label.
