## Why This Compound

Gonadorelin is a ten-amino-acid peptide with a pyroglutamate at the N-terminus, an amidated C-terminus, and the sequence pGlu-His-Trp-Ser-Tyr-Gly-Leu-Arg-Pro-Gly-NH₂. The free-base formula is C₅₅H₇₅N₁₇O₁₃ and the average molecular weight is 1182.3 Da. Both terminal modifications matter. The pyroglutamate closes the N-terminus against aminopeptidase attack, and the C-terminal amide removes a negative charge that would otherwise sit near the receptor-binding surface. A batch that has lost either modification is a different molecule with a different mass and a different receptor profile.

The peptide is the hypothalamic signal that drives gonadotroph cells in the anterior pituitary. Its physiological release is pulsatile, and the pituitary reads the frequency of those pulses rather than a single concentration. The classic experiments made the point directly: continuous delivery of GnRH to the rhesus monkey suppressed gonadotropin output, while intermittent delivery maintained it. The difference was not the total amount delivered but the pattern. Later work mapped how pulse frequency and amplitude change the ratio of the two gonadotropins, and how receptor number and coupling change under sustained exposure.

That history explains why gonadorelin is a research tool for a specific kind of question. In pituitary cell culture, it is used to study receptor binding, Gq-coupled calcium signalling and gonadotropin release. In rodent models, programmable infusion or intermittent delivery is used to examine how the pituitary decodes pulse frequency. In receptor pharmacology, it is the reference agonist against which modified analogues are compared. Each of those uses depends on the native sequence, because the analogues were designed to escape the short lifetime and the pulsatile requirement.

The short half-life is not a defect in the molecule. It is the property that makes pulsatile physiology possible. A long-acting analogue that stays on the receptor produces sustained stimulation, receptor downregulation and a suppression of output that looks like the opposite of the acute response. Researchers who use the two interchangeably generate results that cannot be compared. The [peptide selectivity overview](/blog/peptide-selectivity-explained-for-researchers/) explains how receptor residence time changes the readout, and the [peptide modification note](/blog/how-peptide-modifications-affect-activity/) covers the position-6 and position-10 changes that turn a pulse signal into a sustained one.

For analytical work, the tryptophan at position 3 is the main chemical liability. Oxidation adds 16 Da and converts the indole side chain to a mixture of products that can co-elute with the parent under a shallow gradient. The molecule has no disulfide bond and no methionine, so the oxidation story is simpler than for larger peptides, but it is not absent. Light, trace metals and dissolved oxygen all accelerate it. A certificate that reports purity without saying whether the tryptophan is intact has described the peak, not the molecule.

## What the Batch Record Should Show

A ten-residue peptide is easy to synthesize and easy to mischaracterise. The batch record should close five gaps.

**Mass and terminal modifications.** The average mass of the free base is 1182.3 Da. Electrospray ionisation gives multiply charged ions — roughly m/z 592 for the doubly charged species and m/z 395 for the triply charged species — so the certificate should show a deconvoluted mass, not just a peak list. The amidated C-terminus is the modification most likely to be incomplete. The free-acid form is about 1 Da heavier and can be a minor component that a low-resolution spectrum will not resolve. A peptide map or a tandem mass spectrum can locate the C-terminal peptide. If the certificate reports only "1182.3" without a spectrum, it has repeated the catalogue value.

**Purity by HPLC at 214 nm.** The peptide bond absorbs at 214 nm; the single tryptophan also contributes. The method description should name the column, the mobile phase, the gradient and the integration settings. The main impurities to resolve are oxidised tryptophan (+16 Da), des-amidated peptide (+1 Da), and deletion sequences. A short generic gradient can hide the oxidised species under the main peak. The chromatogram should show the main peak and the impurity profile, not a single area-percent number.

**Acetate content.** The product is the acetate salt. Acetate contributes mass and changes the pH of a concentrated solution. Ion chromatography gives the acetate content; a titration gives the acid content. If the certificate reports peptide content without the counterion, the "2 mg" on the label may be fill weight rather than peptide mass. The [counterion note](/blog/role-of-counterions-in-peptide-stability/) explains how the salt form affects both weighing and solution behaviour.

**Water content and peptide content.** Karl Fischer titration gives water. Nitrogen analysis or amino acid analysis gives peptide content. Both are needed to convert a fill weight into a molar amount. A lyophilized decapeptide is hygroscopic, and the water content at release may not describe the vial after repeated opening.

**Endotoxin and appearance.** Endotoxin matters for pituitary cell cultures and for any preparation that contacts primary cells. The certificate should state whether the limulus amebocyte lysate test was run and what the result was. A sound cake is white to off-white and fills the vial without shrinking away from the glass. The vial label and the certificate should carry the same lot number, together with a synthesis date and a retest date.

The catalogue specification is ≥98% by HPLC. Marketing text sometimes rounds that to "99%+". The certificate is the document of record, and the measured value, the method and the impurity profile matter more than the rounded claim.

## Sourcing & Verification

The first question for a gonadorelin quote is which molecule is being sold. Native gonadorelin has the sequence above. Triptorelin, leuprolide, buserelin and histrelin are analogues with substitutions at position 6 or position 10, designed for longer receptor occupancy. They are not interchangeable with the native peptide, and a supplier who uses the names loosely is not selling a defined research reagent.

Ask for the sequence and the observed mass before the quotation. Ask whether the C-terminus is amidated and whether the certificate shows the free-acid impurity. Ask for the acetate content and the water content. A supplier who can answer those questions has characterised the batch; a supplier who sends a catalogue number and a purity figure has not.

## Handling & Storage

The lyophilized powder belongs at −20°C, protected from light and moisture. Tryptophan is the reason for the light protection: exposure to ultraviolet light and to trace oxidants converts the indole ring to oxindole and related products. Let the vial warm to room temperature before opening, and keep it in a desiccator between uses.

Reconstitution should be gentle. Let the solvent run down the glass and mix by slow rotation; shear from a vortex is unnecessary. Gonadorelin is freely soluble in water, but a cationic peptide can adsorb to glass and to some plastics. Use low-bind tubes for dilute solutions, and avoid leaving a dilute stock on a bench for longer than the experiment requires. If the solution is filtered, use a low-protein-binding membrane and check recovery; a 0.22 µm filter can remove aggregates but can also remove a measurable fraction of the peptide.

Aqueous solutions are short-lived. The catalogue guidance is 2-8°C for short-term use, and that is an outer limit rather than a target. Prepare the working solution fresh, keep it on ice during the experiment, and discard the remainder. For archival storage, divide the solution into single-use tubes and freeze at −20°C or lower; do not refreeze. Repeated freeze-thaw concentrates the peptide at the ice interface and promotes aggregation.

Pulse protocols deserve their own note. The vial does not create a pulse; the pump, the tubing and the timing do. Peptide adsorption to the infusion line can blunt the delivered concentration at the start of a run and release it later, which changes the shape of the pulse without changing the programmed schedule. Prime the line with the working solution, use low-bind tubing where possible, and verify the delivered concentration at the outlet if the experiment depends on the amplitude. The [handling and protocols pillar](/peptide-handling-protocols/) covers the general laboratory workflow.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is gonadorelin the same as leuprolide or triptorelin?**

No. Gonadorelin is the native decapeptide. Leuprolide, triptorelin, buserelin and histrelin are analogues with substitutions that extend receptor occupancy. The native peptide is used for pulsatile studies; the analogues are used when sustained exposure is the variable under study.

**Why is the half-life so short?**

The native peptide is rapidly cleared and degraded in plasma, with a half-life measured in minutes. That short lifetime is part of its physiology: the pituitary responds to pulses, and a signal that persists becomes a different stimulus. The catalogue storage guidance reflects the same instability in aqueous solution.

**What sequence and mass should the certificate show?**

The sequence is pGlu-His-Trp-Ser-Tyr-Gly-Leu-Arg-Pro-Gly-NH₂. The free-base formula is C₅₅H₇₅N₁₇O₁₃ and the average mass is 1182.3 Da. The certificate should also show a deconvoluted mass consistent with the amidated C-terminus.

**How is the acetate salt confirmed?**

Ion chromatography can quantify acetate, and acid titration gives the total acid content. The certificate should report the counterion and the peptide content separately. A fill weight without a counterion value is not a peptide mass.

**What purity does the catalogue list?**

The page gives ≥98% by HPLC. The useful question is how the chromatogram was run and whether it resolves oxidised tryptophan and des-amidated peptide. Ask for the method and the impurity profile.

**How should the lyophilized powder be stored?**

Keep the vial at −20°C, desiccated and dark, and let it warm before opening. Aqueous stocks do not keep; make them for the day and keep them cold while in use.

**Why does a dilute solution lose activity overnight?**

Adsorption to container surfaces, oxidation of tryptophan and aggregation at the air-liquid interface all remove peptide from solution. Use low-bind tubes, avoid foaming, and prepare the working solution close to the time of use.

**Can the same vial be used for a continuous infusion and a pulse protocol?**

The vial is the same, but the experiment is not. Continuous exposure and pulsatile exposure produce different receptor states. The choice belongs to the study design, and the delivered concentration at the outlet should be verified for either protocol.
