## Compound Profile

GHK-Cu (Glycyl-L-Histidyl-L-Lysine copper(II)) is a naturally occurring copper chelating tripeptide first isolated from human plasma by Loren Pickart in 1973. The complex consists of the tripeptide GHK (Gly-His-Lys) coordinated to a copper(II) ion — a configuration that enables cellular uptake via copper chelation and interaction with a broad range of extracellular matrix and intracellular receptors. Plasma concentrations of GHK-Cu decline significantly with age: from approximately 200 ng/mL at age 20 to under 80 ng/mL by age 60, making it a subject of regenerative biology and aging research. Preclinical and in vitro research has examined GHK-Cu's influence on fibroblast proliferation, collagen and glycosaminoglycan synthesis, matrix metalloproteinase regulation, wound remodeling, and stem cell gene expression. Its small molecular size and copper-chelating structure enable tissue penetration that larger peptides cannot achieve. ## Why This Compound

The research literature on GHK-Cu is unusual in one respect: the molecule has been studied for more than fifty years, yet most experimental work remains at the level of cultured cells and animal injury models. That makes it a useful tool for mechanistic questions and a poor candidate for claiming that a particular outcome has been established in humans. A researcher who keeps that boundary in view can use the compound well. A buyer who does not is an easy mark for anything blue.

GHK was identified in human plasma in 1973, and the copper complex was later characterised as the form that carries the metal into cells. The tripeptide has a high affinity for Cu(II), and the complex is small enough to move through tissue compartments that larger proteins do not reach easily. In fibroblast cultures, GHK-Cu has been reported to affect collagen and glycosaminoglycan synthesis, matrix metalloproteinase activity, and genes associated with extracellular matrix turnover. Reviews by Pickart and colleagues describe these effects across several cell types. The 2018 gene-expression paper reviews datasets suggesting that GHK-Cu influences a broad set of genes involved in tissue organisation. That is a hypothesis-generating result, not a clinical finding.

Two mechanistic details matter for sourcing. First, the copper is not an impurity or an ordinary counterion; it is part of the active complex. A vial of GHK peptide without copper — sold separately as GHK Basic — is a different research material, and the two should not be used interchangeably in a study that attributes an effect to copper delivery. Second, the redox state of the copper matters. Cu(II) is the specified form, but free or loosely bound copper can participate in Fenton chemistry and generate reactive oxygen species in solution. A preparation with poorly chelated copper may behave differently in culture. That is a chemical question, and it is answerable with an assay.

The published work also includes animal models of cutaneous injury, where GHK-Cu has been applied topically or delivered systemically. Those studies are worth reading for design ideas, but they do not establish human efficacy. For cosmetic-formulation research, the relevant questions are different again: solubility in the vehicle, compatibility with preservatives, pH, and whether the copper remains chelated in the finished base. None of those is answered by a purity number.

The compound does not have a large, independent body of human trial data. The 2020 review by Dou and colleagues surveys the mechanistic and preclinical case; it does not add a large human trial base. A supplier who presents GHK-Cu as a proven intervention is either misinformed or selling to people who will not check. The defensible position is narrower: a well-characterised copper-binding tripeptide with reproducible in vitro activity and a long history of laboratory use.

## What the Batch Record Should Show

A GHK-Cu certificate of analysis should let a reviewer reconstruct the batch without calling the supplier. In practice, most certificates do not. The common failure is a single purity figure, generated by HPLC, presented as if it described the whole product. It does not. The peptide content, the copper content, the counterion, and the water content are separate measurements, and each one changes the mass balance of what is in the vial.

Identity confirmation comes first. Electrospray ionisation mass spectrometry of GHK-Cu should show a protonated molecular ion near m/z 405, consistent with the formula mass of 403.9 Da. A spectrum that shows only free GHK, at roughly m/z 341, suggests the copper is missing or was lost during sample preparation. Acidic HPLC mobile phases can strip copper from the peptide, so a method developed for free GHK may not report the complex correctly. The certificate should state the solvent and the ion mode.

Purity is usually measured by reverse-phase HPLC with UV detection at 214 or 220 nm. The certificate should give the column, mobile phase, gradient, injection volume, and integration method. It should also distinguish the main peak from related substances: free GHK, oxidised peptide, and copper-free fragments can elute near the main peak depending on the method. If the chromatogram is not supplied, the number is an assertion rather than a measurement.

Copper content is the measurement that separates a serious GHK-Cu supplier from a reseller. The theoretical copper fraction of the complex is about 15.7% by weight, calculated from the formula mass and the atomic mass of copper. A batch that reports 98% peptide purity but only 11% copper is not a 98% GHK-Cu complex; it is a mixture of copper-loaded and copper-free peptide, or a hydrate with a different mass balance. ICP-OES or ICP-MS will quantify the metal. A colorimetric assay can screen, but it is less specific. The certificate should give the measured copper percentage and the calculated copper-to-peptide molar ratio.

Counterion and water content are the next two fields. GHK-Cu is commonly supplied as the acetate salt, though chloride and other forms appear in the market. The counterion affects solubility, pH in solution, and the mass of powder that corresponds to a given amount of peptide. Ion chromatography or titration can quantify acetate. Karl Fischer titration gives water content, which is not trivial for a hygroscopic lyophilised powder: a batch with 8% water has less peptide per milligram than one with 2%, even if both say 98% purity. The certificate should also state fill mass and vial overfill, because a 50 mg label is not a guarantee of 50 mg of powder.

Appearance belongs in the record as an observation, not a specification. GHK-Cu is typically a blue to blue-green powder, and the colour comes from the copper d-d transitions. Colour intensity varies with particle size, moisture, and lighting. A pale batch is not automatically under-copper-loaded, and a vivid batch is not automatically correct. The assay decides.

Finally, the record should carry traceability: batch number, manufacture date, retest date, site of manufacture, and a statement of whether the material is sterile or non-sterile. Endotoxin and bioburden testing matter for cell-culture work, but they do not substitute for chemical characterisation.

## Sourcing & Verification

The first question to ask a GHK-Cu supplier is whether copper is assayed on the same lot that ships. Not on a representative batch, not on a previous production run, and not by a subcontractor who never saw the vial. The answer should be a lot number that appears on the certificate and on the label. If the supplier cannot connect the two, the documentation is decorative.

The second question is what the purity figure describes. A certificate that says "purity 99%" without specifying the method could be reporting peptide content by HPLC, complex content by titration, or simply a number copied from a synthesis report. Ask for the chromatogram and the mass spectrum. Ask what the integration excludes. Ask whether the method was developed for GHK or for GHK-Cu. A supplier who understands the product will have answers; one who does not will offer a discount.

The third question is the copper source and the complexation step. Copper(II) acetate and copper(II) sulfate are both used, and the residual counterion differs. Complexation pH and drying method affect the ratio of bound to free copper. A manufacturer should be able to describe these at a high level without disclosing proprietary parameters. A trader who cannot name the site of complexation is not in control of the variable that matters most.

The fourth question is counterion and water content. These are boring fields, which is why they are useful. A supplier who includes them is likely to have a real QC release process. The same logic applies to fill mass: ask whether the label weight is peptide mass, complex mass, or total powder mass including counterion and water.

A fifth question concerns grade. GHK-Cu appears in cosmetic ingredient catalogs, research catalogs, and grey-market vial lists, and the specifications are not equivalent. Cosmetic-grade material may be a solution with a declared percentage, often without batch-level copper data. Research-grade material should be a lyophilised solid with a certificate that reports the complex. If a supplier offers both from the same lot without explaining the difference, treat that as a gap.

None of this requires a laboratory visit. It requires a supplier who can produce four documents: a lot-specific certificate, the raw HPLC trace, the mass spectrum, and a copper assay. If any is missing, the buyer is pricing an unknown. ## Handling & Storage

Lyophilised GHK-Cu should be stored at −20°C for long-term holding and at 2–8°C for short-term work. The powder is hygroscopic, so let the vial equilibrate to room temperature before opening it; condensation on a cold vial will introduce water and can promote copper-mediated degradation. Keep it in a desiccator with fresh desiccant, protect it from light, and avoid repeated warming cycles. A working aliquot can stay at 2–8°C for the duration of an experiment, but the stock should return to −20°C.

Weighing small quantities of a statically charged powder is a common source of error. Use a balance with a draft shield, an anti-static device if available, and a clean spatula. For cell-culture work, prepare the stock in a suitable buffer or medium, filter sterilise if the assay requires it, and record the actual concentration rather than the nominal one. GHK-Cu dissolves readily in water and aqueous buffers; a cloudy solution usually points to pH or counterion, not insufficient mixing.

Solution stability is assay-dependent and should be established for the specific matrix. Light, temperature, and reducing agents all affect the copper centre. Amber tubes or foil wrap are cheap insurance. Avoid vigorous vortexing, which can denature the peptide and introduce bubbles that interfere with spectrophotometric readings. Aliquot the stock to avoid repeated freeze-thaw cycles.

This is laboratory material. It is not for human or veterinary use, and nothing on this page is a dosing instruction. Reconstitution volumes, concentrations, and exposure times belong in a study protocol reviewed by the institution, not in a product listing.

## How It Compares

[GHK Basic](/catalog/ghk-basic/) is the same tripeptide without the copper. It is the right comparator when a study needs to separate copper-dependent effects from peptide-sequence effects. If a response appears with GHK-Cu but not with GHK Basic, the copper is a plausible contributor; if both produce the same response, the sequence is doing the work. Buying both from the same manufacturer, in the same batch series if possible, reduces the risk that a difference is actually a supplier difference.

[AHK-Cu](/catalog/ahk-cu/) is a different copper-binding tripeptide, Ala-His-Lys, with a similar design logic but a different sequence and a different copper affinity. It is useful for structure-activity comparisons, and it is not a substitute for GHK-Cu in a study that cites GHK-specific literature. The two are sometimes marketed as interchangeable "copper peptides"; they are not.

[Matrixyl](/catalog/matrixyl/) belongs to a different class. It is a palmitoylated peptide developed for cosmetic formulation research, and its proposed mechanism centres on matrix-derived signalling rather than copper delivery. ## The Author's Take

**Position.** If I were designing a GHK-Cu study, I would pay for a lot with a measured copper assay and a lot-specific chromatogram, and I would walk away from a certificate that reports peptide purity alone. The extra cost is small relative to repeating an experiment with an uncharacterised reagent.

**Reasoning.** GHK-Cu is a two-variable material. The peptide sequence determines receptor and matrix interactions; the copper determines redox chemistry and delivery. A purity number captures neither variable completely. Because GHK is a short, cheap sequence to synthesise, purity is also the least differentiating specification in the market — most reputable producers can make a clean tripeptide. The differentiator is whether the copper is bound, at what ratio, and with what counterion. Those are the fields that predict whether two vials will behave the same way in culture.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is copper content tested on every batch?**

It should be. A useful certificate reports copper by ICP-OES or ICP-MS and gives the copper-to-peptide ratio. A certificate that reports only HPLC purity does not establish that the complex is correctly loaded. Ask for the copper assay on the same lot number that appears on the vial.

**Why does the powder look blue or green?**

The colour comes from the copper(II) centre. Intensity varies with particle size, moisture, and lighting, so colour is a weak quality indicator. A pale powder can still meet the copper specification, and a strongly coloured powder can still be under-pure. The assay decides.

**How should GHK-Cu be stored?**

Store lyophilised powder at −20°C long-term and 2–8°C short-term, protected from light and moisture. Equilibrate the vial before opening, keep it dry, and avoid repeated freeze-thaw cycles of reconstituted solution. Use amber or foil-wrapped tubes for working solutions.

**Can GHK-Cu be used in cosmetic formulation research?**

It can be studied in formulation systems, but the questions change: solubility in the vehicle, pH compatibility, preservative interactions, and whether the copper stays chelated in the finished base. A lyophilised research-grade solid and a cosmetic-grade solution are not interchangeable specifications. This catalog supplies the former for laboratory use.

**What is the difference between GHK-Cu and GHK Basic?**

GHK Basic is the tripeptide without copper. It is the appropriate negative comparator when a study aims to isolate copper-dependent effects. The two should not be substituted for one another without saying so in the methods.

**What documentation should come with a batch?**

A lot-specific certificate of analysis, the raw HPLC trace, the mass spectrum, a copper assay, and traceability fields linking the lot number to the label. Counterion and water content should be reported. Endotoxin and sterility data are useful for cell work but do not replace chemical characterisation.
