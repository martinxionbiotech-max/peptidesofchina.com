A research peptide label is a control document, not a sticker. It has to identify the material, the lot, the quantity, the storage condition, the hazard classification, and the research-use limitation in a way that matches the certificate, the packing list, and the commercial invoice. When those four documents disagree, the label becomes the weakest link in an otherwise defensible shipment.

This guide is written for suppliers, brand owners, and procurement teams who review labels for research peptides. It covers the fields that should appear, the hazard communication elements that apply to chemical products, the customs documents that must agree with the label, and the claims that create regulatory risk. It is a documentation guide, not legal advice; the requirements of the destination market always control.

## What a research peptide label needs to contain

A label has limited space, so the design question is which fields are essential and which belong on the carton, the certificate, or the safety data sheet. The vial label should carry the minimum needed to identify the material at the bench. The carton can carry the rest.

| Field | Example | Control question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Product name | BPC-157, research grade | Does it match the catalog and invoice? |
| Sequence or identifier | 15-residue sequence or catalog code | Can the user confirm identity without opening the certificate? |
| Lot number | Site code, year, sequence | Does it match the certificate and packing list? |
| Quantity | 5 mg per vial | Is the unit metric and unambiguous? |
| Purity or grade | ≥98% by RP-HPLC | Is the method named on the certificate? |
| Storage condition | Store at −20 °C, protect from light | Is the condition supported by stability data? |
| Research-use statement | For research use only. Not for human or veterinary use. | Is it legible at the final label size? |
| Manufacturer or seller | Legal entity and address | Does it match the invoice entity? |
| Hazard symbols and signal word | Per GHS classification | Does the SDS carry the same classification? |
| Expiry or retest date | Month and year | Is the date supported by stability data? |

Three fields cause most receiving queries: the lot number, the quantity, and the storage condition. A lot number that differs by one character between vial and certificate is a traceability break. A quantity stated as "5 mg" without clarifying whether that is peptide mass or vial fill mass creates a weighing error. A storage condition that contradicts the certificate forces the receiver to choose which document to believe.

## Identity fields: name, sequence, lot, and mass

The product name alone is not enough. Many peptide names are used for different sequences, salt forms, or purity grades. A label that says "BPC-157" without a sequence, salt form, or catalog identifier leaves the identity ambiguous.

A practical minimum is the product name, the catalog code, the lot number, and the quantity. The sequence can go on the carton or the certificate if the vial label is too small. For custom peptides, include the batch-specific sequence or a reference to the custom order number.

The lot number is the most important field for traceability. It should encode enough information to retrieve the batch record: site code, production year, and a sequence number are common. The same lot number must appear on the vial, the carton, the certificate, the packing list, and the commercial invoice. If the supplier uses a different internal lot number for the same material, the relationship should be stated in writing.

Mass deserves its own line. A vial labeled "5 mg" may contain 5 mg of total powder, 5 mg of peptide by net content, or 5 mg of a specific salt form. The certificate should state which convention applies. A label that does not distinguish these is a specification gap, not a printing error. ## The research-use-only statement

The phrase "For research use only. Not for human or veterinary use" is a boundary statement. It tells the buyer that the material is not intended for diagnostic, therapeutic, or veterinary use, and it supports the supplier's position that the product is sold as a research reagent.

The statement does not, by itself, make a product compliant with every market's rules. It also does not transfer responsibility to the buyer for unlawful use. It is one element of a labeling and distribution posture, not a legal shield.

For the label to do its job, the statement should be:

- **Legible.** At least 6-point type on the vial label, and larger on the carton.
- **Unambiguous.** "Research use only" rather than "not for sale" or "laboratory sample."
- **Consistent.** The same wording on the vial, carton, certificate, and website.
- **Separate from claims.** A label that says "research use only" and also implies a therapeutic benefit sends contradictory signals.

## Hazard communication: GHS, CLP, and HazCom

Peptides are chemicals. Even when the hazard is low, the label and safety data sheet should follow the hazard communication system of the destination market. The three systems most relevant to research peptide shipments are the UN GHS model, the EU CLP Regulation, and the US OSHA Hazard Communication Standard.

| Element | Requirement | Common label error |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Product identifier | Name matching the SDS | Abbreviation only |
| Signal word | "Danger" or "Warning" | Missing when a hazard class applies |
| Hazard statements | Standard phrases for each class | Free-text descriptions instead of H-statements |
| Precautionary statements | Prevention, response, storage, disposal | Generic "handle with care" |
| Pictograms | Black symbol on white with red border | Low-resolution or missing border |
| Supplier identification | Name, address, telephone | Website only, no legal entity |

Under the EU CLP Regulation, the label elements are defined in Article 17 and the safety data sheet in Article 31. Under the US Hazard Communication Standard, 29 CFR 1910.1200 requires a 16-section safety data sheet and labels with the GHS elements. China's GB 30000 series adapts the GHS criteria, and GB 15258 sets the label content requirements.

A peptide with no classified hazard still needs a product identifier and supplier information, and it still needs an SDS if the destination market requires one. "Not hazardous" is a classification outcome, not an exemption from documentation.

The SDS and the label must agree. A label with no pictogram but an SDS listing a skin irritant is an inconsistency that a customs or safety reviewer will notice. The [documentation package guide](/blog/peptide-documentation-package) lists the documents that should be cross-checked before shipment.

## Customs documents that must agree with the label

Customs review compares the label, the commercial invoice, the packing list, and the transport document. The most common discrepancies are description, quantity, value, and origin.

- **Commercial invoice.** Product description, quantity, unit value, HS code, and country of origin should match the label and packing list.
- **Packing list.** Vial count, net weight, gross weight, and dimensions should match the invoice and the physical carton.
- **Air waybill or bill of lading.** Piece count, weight, and description should match the packing list.
- **Certificate of analysis.** Lot number, specification, and result should match the vial label.
- **Safety data sheet.** Product name, hazards, and transport information should match the label.

The HS code is a classification decision, not a label field, but it drives duty and documentary review. Peptides are generally classified in Chapter 29, and synthetic research peptides are often entered under subheading 2937.19, but the correct code depends on the specific product and the importing country's tariff schedule. The [customs clearance guide](/blog/research-peptide-customs-clearance-a-professional-b2b-sourcing-guide-2026) covers classification and importer-of-record questions.

Incoterms determine who arranges carriage, insurance, and import clearance. A DDP shipment puts the seller in the importer role for customs purposes; an FOB shipment leaves that role with the buyer. The label and documents should be consistent with the agreed term. The [shipping documentation guide](/blog/why-legitimate-documentation-matters-in-peptide-shipping) explains the documents a consignee should expect at each step.

## Destination-market differences

Labeling requirements vary by market, and a single global label is usually a compromise. The table below summarizes the main differences that affect research peptide shipments.

| Market | Core framework | Language | Notable requirement |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| United States | OSHA HazCom, 29 CFR 1910.1200; FDA rules for drugs and foods if applicable | English | 16-section SDS; GHS label elements |
| European Union | CLP Regulation (EC) 1272/2008; REACH for substances | Official language(s) of the member state | Label elements under Article 17; SDS under Article 31 |
| China | GB 30000 series for classification; GB 15258 for labels | Chinese | Chinese-language label and SDS for domestic distribution |
| Japan | Industrial Safety and Health Act; GHS classification | Japanese | Container labeling and SDS for specified substances |
| United Kingdom | GB CLP, retained EU CLP as amended | English | UK-specific label and SDS duties |

A shipment that crosses a border without the destination-language label may be held even when the product is correctly classified. For China-bound research shipments, a Chinese product name and hazard information on the carton reduce the chance of a documentation query. For EU shipments, the label should use the language of the member state where the product is placed on the market, unless the buyer has agreed otherwise in writing.

## Claims that create regulatory risk

The fastest way to turn a research reagent into a regulatory problem is to put a medical claim on the label or the website. Phrases such as "treats," "prevents," "heals," "anti-aging," "weight loss," or "boosts recovery" move the product toward a drug, supplement, or cosmetic claim, depending on the market.

Labels should avoid:

- **Disease or symptom claims.** Any statement that the product diagnoses, treats, cures, or prevents a disease.
- **Dosage or administration instructions.** Even a suggested amount implies a therapeutic use.
- **"Pharma grade" or "GMP" without evidence.** These terms imply a manufacturing standard that must be documented, not just printed.
- **"Guaranteed customs clearance" or "guaranteed purity."** A guarantee that cannot be supported by a certificate is a commercial risk.
- **Comparative claims against approved drugs.** These invite regulatory attention and are difficult to substantiate.

A label can state the product name, grade, purity method, quantity, storage condition, research-use limitation, and hazard information. That is enough for a research supplier. Anything beyond that should be reviewed by someone who knows the destination market's rules.

## Label change control and versioning

A label is a controlled document. Changes should be versioned, approved, and linked to the batch record. The version that applies to a lot should be retrievable after shipment.

A practical control scheme:

- Assign a label part number and a revision letter.
- Record the effective date and the reason for each change.
- Keep a proof of the printed label with the batch record.
- Retain one labeled vial and one carton from each lot for the retention period.
- Reconcile the label version with the specification version at release.

Under ICH Q7, batch records should be retained for at least one year after the expiry date. Label proofs and specification versions should follow the same retention schedule. A supplier that cannot produce the label proof for a lot cannot prove what the buyer received.

## A label review checklist before print

Before approving a label, check the following:

1. Product name matches the catalog, invoice, and certificate.
2. Lot number field is present and matches the batch record format.
3. Quantity and unit are unambiguous.
4. Purity grade is stated with the method or a certificate reference.
5. Storage condition matches the stability data and the certificate.
6. Research-use statement is present and legible.
7. Hazard pictograms, signal word, and statements match the SDS.
8. Supplier legal name and address match the invoice entity.
9. Destination-language requirements are met.
10. No disease, dosage, or efficacy claims appear.
11. Label part number and revision are recorded.
12. A proof is retained with the batch record.

A label that passes this checklist is not automatically compliant with every market. It is, however, internally consistent, which is the first thing a customs officer, safety auditor, or receiving laboratory will test.

## The Author's Take

**Position:** Labeling problems are usually document-control problems, not design problems. The supplier that cannot reconcile a lot number across the vial, certificate, and invoice will also struggle to answer a customs query or a customer complaint.

**Reasoning:** I have seen labels that looked professional and still failed at receiving because the quantity convention was unclear or the certificate carried a different lot suffix. The fix is not a better printer; it is a controlled field list and a cross-check before the label is applied. The research-use statement matters, but it cannot compensate for an identity field that does not match the paperwork. A label is credible when every field can be traced to a source document, and that standard is achievable at any scale.
