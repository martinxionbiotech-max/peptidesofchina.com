Verify a China peptide manufacturer by separating four questions: which legal entity is responsible for the material, which site and batch produced it, what the analytical record actually measures, and whether the quality system can reproduce that result. A certificate answers none of those until its issuer, sample identity, and batch number are tied to the goods you will receive. The review ends with a written qualification that names the product, site, batch, specification version, and evidence reviewed.

This protocol is for research procurement teams reviewing a source before a first order or a repeat purchase. It does not cover clinical supply or personal use. Peptides of China coordinates sourcing, orders, supplier communication, and delivery support; it does not manufacture peptides or operate an independent analytical testing laboratory. Product specifications, manufacturer records, and buyer-commissioned reports remain separate evidence sources throughout the review.

## 1. Start with the legal manufacturer, not the storefront

A supplier can sell a peptide without making it. That is normal, and not a quality failure by itself. The problem is an unclear role: a trading company presented as a factory, a brand owner that cannot name the synthesis site, or a certificate issued by an entity with no connection to the batch.

Map four roles for the material under review:

- **Brand owner or seller.** The entity that invoices the buyer and owns the catalog listing.
- **Synthesis site.** The facility that runs the coupling, cleavage, purification, and lyophilization.
- **Fill and finish site.** The facility that aliquots the material into vials, if that is a separate operation.
- **Intermediate holder.** A trader, distributor, or sourcing coordinator that holds stock or places the order.

Ask for the legal name, registered address, and business registration of the seller. Then ask for the site address where the batch was synthesized and the site where it was filled. If the seller is not the manufacturer, request a written statement that it is authorized to share the manufacturer's batch records and certificate. A statement on letterhead is a start; it is not a substitute for a batch record that names the same lot.

Check that the invoice entity, the certificate issuer, and the business registration agree. A mismatch is not automatically improper, but it needs an explanation in writing. Common legitimate cases include a brand owner that releases product made by a toll synthesizer, or a sales entity that invoices for an affiliated manufacturing site.

Facility photographs, a catalog headed "manufacturer," and a logo do not establish which site made a lot. The batch record does. If the supplier cannot provide a batch record extract, the production site remains unverified.

## 2. Fix the material definition and the specification version

A supplier review cannot be more precise than the product definition. Record the catalog identifier, compound name, sequence, modifications, salt or counterion, presentation, quantity, and the analytical methods used to state purity and content. Do not infer these details from an abbreviated product name.

The specification should state, at minimum:

1. Amino acid sequence and any terminal modifications.
2. Salt or counterion form.
3. Presentation and fill mass or fill volume.
4. Identity method and acceptance rule.
5. Purity method, wavelength, gradient, and integration threshold.
6. Peptide content method and acceptance range.
7. Water content limit.
8. Residual solvent or counterion limits where relevant.
9. Endotoxin limit, if the intended research use requires one.
10. Storage condition and retest period.

Give the specification a version number and a date. Keep a signed or dated copy with the purchase record. If the supplier later changes a method or a limit, the earlier version remains available for comparison. A specification that exists only as a web page is difficult to freeze; export it to a dated PDF and attach it to the order.

The salt form deserves separate attention. A peptide supplied as a trifluoroacetate salt and the same peptide supplied as an acetate salt have different mass contributions from the counterion. Trifluoroacetic acid has a nominal mass of 114 Da, and trifluoroacetate adducts can appear in mass spectra. A certificate that reports a molecular weight without stating the salt form leaves the buyer unable to reconcile the number with the sequence. Ask which form was manufactured, which form the certificate describes, and whether the label states it.

## 3. Build a four-layer evidence chain

Verification improves when each document is assigned the question it can answer. Four layers cover most supplier reviews.

| Evidence layer | Issuer | What it can establish | What it cannot establish |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Specification | Buyer and supplier, agreed and dated | The material, methods, and numeric limits | That any batch met the limits |
| Manufacturer certificate of analysis | Manufacturer or its quality unit | Results attributed to a stated batch | That the sample tested was drawn from the shipped vials |
| Independent laboratory report | Laboratory commissioned by the buyer or supplier | Results for the submitted sample under the stated method | That the sample represents the whole lot |
| Batch production record extract | Manufacturer | How the lot was made, tested, and released | That the analytical method was suitable for the buyer's purpose |

The chain holds only when one identifier connects the layers. Check the lot code on the vial label, the certificate, the invoice or packing list, and the batch record extract. If the label uses an internal code that differs from the certificate, ask for a cross-reference letter that maps the codes and names the batch. Keep the letter with the original documents. Do not overwrite a mismatched identifier to make the file look consistent.

Document provenance questions are straightforward and worth asking every time:

1. Who issued the document, and can the issuer be contacted independently?
2. Which sample or batch does it identify?
3. Does that identifier agree with the purchase record and the label?
4. Are the sample description, dates, methods, results, and units complete?
5. Are limitations, missing pages, or unsupported corrections visible?

A certificate for bulk material does not automatically establish the content of a filled vial. A certificate for a different batch is background information. An independent report can be genuine and still fail to connect to the goods in your warehouse. The [COA reading guide](/blog/how-to-read-a-peptide-coa-a-researchers-guide-to-hplc-and-ms-reports/) walks through the fields, and the [documentation package](/blog/peptide-documentation-package/) checklist covers the file structure.

## 4. Read each analytical test at its own scope

Analytical results answer narrow questions. A purity percentage from reversed-phase high-performance liquid chromatography is an area percentage at a stated wavelength, usually 214 nm for the peptide bond, under a stated gradient and integration rule. It is not a mass fraction. Water, counterions, residual solvents, and inorganic salts do not appear in the same UV response, so a lot can show 98% chromatographic purity and still contain substantially less peptide by mass.

| Test | Question answered | Method detail to request | Common misreading |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| RP-HPLC | How much UV-absorbing material elutes as the main peak? | Column, gradient, flow, temperature, wavelength, injection volume, integration threshold | Treating area percent as peptide content |
| LC-MS intact mass | Does the observed mass match the expected structure? | Ionization mode, adducts, deconvolution method, mass accuracy | Treating a mass match as sequence confirmation |
| MS/MS or sequencing | Is the amino acid order consistent with the structure? | Fragmentation method, coverage, software and score threshold | Assuming full sequence coverage from a partial spectrum |
| Amino acid analysis | What is the peptide mass fraction? | Hydrolysis conditions, derivatization, internal standard, calculation | Comparing results from different hydrolysis protocols |
| Ion chromatography | Which counterion is present, and at what level? | Column, eluent, detection, calibration | Assuming the declared salt form without a result |
| Karl Fischer titration | How much water is in the sample? | Sample size, oven or direct injection, endpoint | Reporting loss on drying as water content |
| LAL endotoxin test | Is endotoxin below the stated limit? | Method, control standard, inhibition and enhancement validation | Treating a pass as a sterility result |
| Sterility test | Is the sample free of growth under the method? | Membrane filtration or direct inoculation, media, incubation | Treating absence of growth as proof of sterility in use |

Method validation is the next question. ICH Q2(R2) sets out the characteristics that a validated analytical procedure should address, including specificity, linearity, range, accuracy, precision, detection limit, quantitation limit, and tolerance to deliberate method variation. A certificate that reports a result without naming a validated method gives the buyer a number without a basis for comparing it to another lot or another laboratory. The [quality testing page](/peptide-quality-testing/) explains how HPLC, mass spectrometry, counterion analysis, and endotoxin testing fit together.

Impurity limits need their own framework. ICH Q3A(R2) sets reporting, identification, and qualification thresholds for impurities in new drug substances: a reporting threshold of 0.05%, an identification threshold of 0.10%, and a qualification threshold of 0.15%, with daily-intake alternatives where the guideline applies them. Those thresholds are a reference for how impurity limits are structured, not a release specification for a research peptide. Applying them without saying so overstates the regulatory status of a catalog product.

A mass-balance view helps: purity by HPLC, water by Karl Fischer, counterion by ion chromatography, and residual solvents by gas chromatography should reconcile. If they do not, ask which assay is missing rather than treating the highest percentage as the truest one.

## 5. Audit the batch record and the quality system

A certificate is a summary. An audit tests whether the summary came from a controlled process. For a desk review, request a batch record extract for the last three lots of the same product. For an on-site or remote audit, sample the same three lots and trace each one from raw material receipt to release.

| Audit area | Records to sample | Acceptance signal | Red flag |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Batch production record | Synthesis, cleavage, purification, lyophilization, yield, in-process tests, sign-offs | Complete entries with equipment IDs and no unexplained gaps | Summary sheet with no batch-specific process data |
| Raw materials | Resin, amino acid derivatives, solvents, cleavage reagents, lot numbers, supplier qualification | Each input traceable to a released lot | "Pharmaceutical grade" with no supplier or lot record |
| Equipment | HPLC, balance, pH meter, freeze-dryer, calibration and qualification records | Calibration current at the run date | Calibration after the batch, or no equipment ID |
| Deviations and CAPA | Deviation log, investigation reports, corrective actions, closure dates | Deviations investigated and closed before release | Repeated deviations with no root cause |
| Change control | Changes to route, resin, purification, fill site, or specification | Change assessed and approved before implementation | Retroactive approval, or no change record |
| Training and access | Operator training files, electronic record audit trails, user permissions | Training current; audit trails enabled | Shared logins, disabled audit trail, no review |
| Retention samples | Sample log, storage condition, inventory | Retention samples match released lots | No retention program, or samples without lot identity |

Data integrity belongs in the same review. If the quality unit keeps electronic records, ask whether audit trails are enabled and reviewed, whether users have unique accounts, and whether the system is controlled under 21 CFR Part 11 or an equivalent national requirement. A PDF certificate with a typed name is easy to produce; a controlled record with an audit trail is harder to fabricate and easier to verify.

The audit sample should be bounded. Asking for every record invites a document dump that no one reviews. Ask for the last three batches, twelve months of deviation and complaint records, and all change controls that affect the product or site.

## 6. Sample, retain, and document chain of custody

Sampling is where a good document chain can break. Record who drew the sample, when, from which vial or container, and under what conditions. Use a unique sample ID that is not the supplier's lot code, and keep a mapping between the two in the procurement file. If the sample travels to a laboratory, include a chain-of-custody form with each handover.

A workable retention rule is two vials per lot in addition to the vial tested: one unopened for confirmatory testing, one working aliquot. Store them at the labeled condition, typically -20 °C and protected from light, and retain them for the study duration plus 12 months, or longer under the sponsor's quality system. Label each vial with the lot, the date received, the study code, and the sample ID. A retention sample without a location and a custodian is only a stored vial.

For a new supplier or high-value lot, test one vial, retain two, and keep a fourth for a repeat assay. Record the sample size and acceptance rule before testing; choosing the rule after seeing the result turns an assay into a negotiation.

The laboratory report should identify the sample, the method, the instrument or platform, the raw data file, the analyst, the reviewer, and the date. Ask for the chromatogram and the mass spectrum, not just the summary table. A summary can be transcribed; a raw file with an audit trail is harder to reconstruct after the fact.

## 7. Score the evidence and issue a bounded qualification

A qualification decision should describe its scope. "Approved supplier" is too broad to be useful. A better record states: product, site, batch or batches reviewed, specification version, evidence received, tests the buyer will run, open limitations, approver, and review date.

A simple 100-point model makes the decision comparable across suppliers:

- **Documentation, 30 points.** Complete certificate, batch record extract, specification, and label proof.
- **Analytical concordance, 25 points.** Identity and purity results agree across the certificate, the independent report, and the buyer's test.
- **Audit evidence, 25 points.** Batch records, deviations, change control, equipment calibration, and data integrity.
- **Logistics, 10 points.** Label, packing, temperature control, and receipt condition.
- **Commercial record, 10 points.** Invoice, origin statement, and correspondence that match the legal entity.

Set decision bands in advance. For example: 85–100 approved for the stated scope; 70–84 approved with conditions such as lot-by-lot testing; below 70 hold or reject. The bands are a management tool, not a scientific standard. The important part is that the score, the evidence, and the conditions are written down before the first purchase order.

A bounded qualification names what was not reviewed. If the audit covered one product but not the site's other lines, or the certificate covered identity and purity but not endotoxin, say so. The [supplier quality assurance guide](/blog/peptide-quality-assurance-process-a-researchers-guide-to-sourcing-integrity/) describes how this record fits into a larger quality process.

## 8. Re-verify at change events and on a calendar

Verification ages. A site can change a synthesis route, a purification column, a lyophilization cycle, or a fill provider without changing the catalog listing. Set triggers and a calendar review.

Re-verify when:

- The production or fill site changes.
- The synthesis route, resin, cleavage cocktail, or purification method changes.
- The presentation, vial size, or counterion changes.
- The specification or an analytical method is revised.
- Two consecutive lots show an out-of-trend result.
- A label, certificate, or batch record identifier does not reconcile.
- Twelve months have passed since the last document review.

Keep the supplier file for at least three years after the last order, or longer if the sponsor's record-retention policy requires it. The file should contain the specification versions, certificates, independent reports, audit notes, qualification decision, and the correspondence that resolved each discrepancy.

Traceability is the thread through all of it. A lot number is useful only when it connects the vial to the batch record, the analytical sample, and the release decision. ## The Author's Take

**Position.** Manufacturer verification is a document-linkage exercise before it is a laboratory exercise.

**Reasoning.** Most disputes I see do not turn on whether an HPLC method can detect an impurity. They turn on whether the certificate describes the vial in the buyer's hand. A supplier with a clean batch record, a consistent lot code, and an honest answer about its role is easier to qualify than a supplier with a polished certificate and no traceable batch. The laboratory test confirms the material; the document chain establishes what the material is supposed to be.
