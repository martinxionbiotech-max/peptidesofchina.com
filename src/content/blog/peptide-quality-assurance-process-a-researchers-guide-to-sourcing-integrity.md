Reading an HPLC Chromatogram High-Performance Liquid Chromatography (HPLC) is the industry standard for determining purity. It works by separating the peptide from impurities based on its interaction with a stationary phase. The report displays various peaks; the largest peak represents the target peptide, while smaller peaks indicate residual solvents, truncated sequences, or salts. Purity is calculated using the "area under the curve" method. If the main peak accounts for 99% of the total area, the purity is reported as 99%. However, these percentages are easily faked on paper. High-volume buyers should always perform independent peptide testing to validate these claims. Small deviations in the impurity profile, as noted in FDA guidance on peptide impurities, can significantly alter research outcomes. Confirming Identity with Mass Spectrometry While HPLC tells you how pure a substance is, Mass Spectrometry (MS) tells you what it actually is. It verifies the identity of the peptide sequence by measuring its molecular weight. In Electrospray Ionization (ESI-MS) reports, you'll typically look for the (M+H)+ peak, which represents the molecular weight plus one hydrogen ion. If the observed mass peak doesn't match the theoretical molecular weight of the sequence, the batch is either the wrong peptide or contains significant synthesis errors. This step is vital in the peptide quality assurance process to prevent the "wrong peptide" error, which is common when resellers handle multiple similar-looking compounds like BPC-157 or TB-500. Frequently Asked Questions How do I verify a peptide Certificate of Analysis (COA) is authentic? Authenticity is verified by checking for specific batch numbers, synthesis dates, and high-resolution chromatograms that aren't blurred or recycled. Authentic reports should correlate with current production timelines rather than appearing as third-generation photocopies. Contacting the issuing lab or synthesis facility directly to verify the report ID is the most reliable method for confirming that the document hasn't been altered or reused by a reseller. What is the minimum purity required for research-grade peptides? Research-grade peptides typically require a minimum purity of 98% to ensure experimental accuracy and minimize the impact of synthesis byproducts. While some applications may tolerate lower thresholds, high-purity levels are necessary to prevent impurities from interfering with biological assays. Maintaining a strict peptide quality assurance process helps identify batches that fall below these technical requirements before they enter a professional laboratory workflow. Why do HPLC reports sometimes show different results for the same batch? Variance in HPLC results often stems from differences in equipment calibration, mobile phase composition, or the specific column used during analysis. A variance of ±1-2% is standard across different laboratories and doesn't necessarily indicate fraud. However, significant deviations may suggest that the samples were taken from different parts of a non-homogeneous bulk batch or that chemical degradation occurred during trans-shipment through uncontrolled environments.
