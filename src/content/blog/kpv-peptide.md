KPV is the tripeptide Lys-Pro-Val, a three-residue fragment from the C-terminal end of alpha-melanocyte-stimulating hormone. The published research on it is preclinical: cultured intestinal and skin cells, antimicrobial assays, and mouse colitis models. No human trial establishes a dose, an administration route, or a clinical benefit.

That leaves two separate questions, and buyers often merge them. The first is what the biology literature reports. The second is whether the powder in a vial is actually KPV, at the stated quantity, with the impurities and counterions described on the label. The first question is settled by reading papers. The second is settled by analytical documents tied to a batch number.

This page covers both, in that order.

## What KPV Is, and Why the Name Proves Little

KPV corresponds to residues 11–13 of alpha-MSH. Its formula is C16H30N4O4 and its average molecular mass is 342.4 Da. The sequence has no aromatic residue, which has practical consequences for analysis: there is no tyrosine or tryptophan to absorb at 280 nm, so purity and concentration methods must rely on the peptide bond absorbance near 214 nm, on mass spectrometry, or on amino acid analysis.

The molecule is small and strongly polar. At pH 7.4 the free peptide carries a net positive charge, contributed by the N-terminal amino group and the lysine side chain, partly offset by the C-terminal carboxylate. That charge affects everything downstream: retention on a reversed-phase column, solubility, and the choice of counterion in the finished salt.

Three naming problems appear repeatedly in supplier catalogs.

The first is sequence ambiguity. "KPV" is unambiguous as a sequence, but a listing that says only "KPV peptide" does not state whether the material is the free base, the acetate salt, or a trifluoroacetate salt. Those forms have different masses per milligram of powder, and a buyer calculating molarity from vial weight will be wrong if the salt form is unknown.

The second is positional isomerism. A tripeptide with three different residues can be assembled in six orders. Mass spectrometry alone cannot distinguish Lys-Pro-Val from Lys-Val-Pro, because both have the same elemental composition. Sequence confirmation needs fragmentation data or a reference standard, not just a molecular weight.

The third is grade. A research-grade batch, a "cosmetic grade" batch, and a batch intended for further manufacturing are not interchangeable, even when the HPLC area percentage is similar. The difference sits in documentation, control of residual solvents, bioburden, and traceability.

## The PepT1 Study: Transport, Signaling, and Mouse Colitis

The most cited mechanistic paper on KPV is Dalmasso et al., published in *Gastroenterology* in 2008. It is useful because it connects three things that are usually studied separately: how the peptide enters a cell, what happens to inflammatory signaling once it is inside, and whether an animal model responds.

The in-vitro work used Caco2-BBE and HT29-Cl.19A human intestinal epithelial cell lines and Jurkat T cells. The investigators stimulated the cells with pro-inflammatory cytokines and measured NF-κB activation with a luciferase reporter, then confirmed pathway changes by Western blot, real-time RT-PCR, and ELISA. Nanomolar concentrations of KPV reduced NF-κB and MAP kinase activation and lowered pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion.

Uptake was measured two ways: cold KPV competed with a radiolabeled PepT1 substrate, and tritiated KPV was used to derive uptake kinetics. The result linked transport to the di/tripeptide transporter PepT1, which is expressed in the small intestine and induced in the colon during inflammatory bowel disease.

The in-vivo arm used DSS- and TNBS-induced colitis in mice. KPV was added to drinking water, and inflammation was scored histologically and by cytokine mRNA expression. Both models showed a reduction in colitis incidence and inflammatory markers.

What this paper establishes is a plausible transport-and-signaling mechanism, tested in human cell lines and in two rodent colitis models. What it does not establish is a human dose, a human route, or a treatment effect. The drinking-water administration is a mouse study design, not a protocol that transfers to people. The paper's own conclusion describes KPV as a possible therapeutic candidate for IBD, which is a hypothesis, not a finding in patients.

## Antimicrobial and Skin-Model Findings

Cutuli et al. examined alpha-MSH and its C-terminal tripeptide against *Staphylococcus aureus* and *Candida albicans* in 2000. KPV inhibited staphylococcal colony formation and reduced yeast viability and germ-tube formation. The authors reported activity across a broad concentration range, including picomolar concentrations, and proposed a cAMP-mediated mechanism based on the reversal of killing by an adenylyl cyclase inhibitor.

Two details in that paper deserve attention. The first is that the assays were performed in culture, not in an infected animal or a human. The second is that the concentrations reported as active include values far below what a plasma or tissue exposure model would predict. An in-vitro effect at picomolar concentration is a signal about mechanism; it is not a pharmacokinetic claim.

For a buyer, the antimicrobial literature matters mainly because it explains why KPV appears in wound-care and skin-care research lists. It does not support a claim that a KPV vial treats or prevents infection, and a supplier that markets it that way is writing outside the evidence.

Elliott et al. studied alpha-MSH, the KPV fragment, and adrenocorticotropic hormone signaling in human keratinocytes, published in the *Journal of Investigative Dermatology* in 2004. The paper belongs to a body of work on melanocortin signaling in skin cells, where KPV is used as a minimal active fragment rather than as a drug candidate.

A more recent study by Sung et al., published in *Tissue and Cell* in 2025, exposed HaCaT keratinocytes and a three-dimensional skin model to fine dust particles. The authors reported reduced apoptosis and inflammatory markers in KPV-treated systems, with changes attributed to oxidative-stress regulation and Nrf2/HO-1 signaling.

These are laboratory models. A three-dimensional skin construct is more informative than a monolayer, but it is still not human skin with an immune system, a circulation, and a stratum corneum barrier. The result supports a research direction; it does not support a cosmetic efficacy claim or a clinical outcome.

Brzoska et al. reviewed the alpha-MSH tripeptide literature in *Endocrine Reviews* in 2008, covering anti-inflammatory and protective effects in vitro and in vivo. Reviews of this kind are useful for mapping the field, and they also show how much of the evidence sits in animal models and cell culture rather than controlled human studies.

## Where the Evidence Stops

The published record supports a narrow set of statements. KPV is transported into intestinal epithelial cells in a PepT1-dependent manner in cell culture. It reduces NF-κB and MAP kinase signaling in cytokine-stimulated cells at nanomolar concentrations. It reduces colitis markers in DSS- and TNBS-treated mice when given in drinking water. It shows antimicrobial activity against two organisms in vitro. It changes inflammatory and apoptotic markers in keratinocyte and skin-model experiments.

The record does not support the following, and these are the claims a buyer should discount immediately:

- a proven benefit for inflammatory bowel disease, psoriasis, eczema, wound healing, or infection in people;
- an effective or safe human dose, cycle, or route;
- equivalence between a research vial and any pharmaceutical product;
- synergy between KPV and BPC-157, TB-500, or any other peptide in a blend;
- long-term safety data in humans;
- a therapeutic index or a no-observed-adverse-effect level that applies to human use.

A supplier page that states or implies any of the above is not summarizing the literature. It is extrapolating from it.

## Identity Testing and Certificate Review

Small peptides are harder to control than their size suggests. A three-residue molecule has few fragments to work with, a weak chromophore, and a high chance of carrying counterions and water that inflate the powder weight. The analytical package should therefore combine separation, identity, and content measurements rather than relying on a single purity number.

| Attribute | Typical method | What it establishes | What it can miss |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Identity | ESI-LC-MS/MS, positive mode, [M+H]+ near m/z 343.2 | Molecular mass and fragmentation pattern consistent with Lys-Pro-Val | A positional isomer with the same mass, unless fragmentation or a reference standard is used |
| Purity | RP-HPLC-UV at 214 nm, C18, water/acetonitrile with 0.1% TFA | Area percentage of the main peak | Non-UV-active impurities, salts, water, and anything that co-elutes |
| Sequence | MS/MS b- and y-ion series, or co-elution with a certified standard | Residue order | Modified residues that preserve mass |
| Counterion | Ion chromatography | Acetate or trifluoroacetate content | Counterions not included in the method |
| Water content | Karl Fischer titration | Moisture in the vialed powder | Residual solvent bound in the cake |
| Endotoxin | LAL assay per USP <85> or equivalent | Endotoxin burden in EU/mg | Non-endotoxin pyrogens |
| Bioburden | USP <61>/<62> methods | Viable microbial count | Organisms that do not grow under the chosen conditions |

The 214 nm wavelength is not a detail. Peptide bonds absorb there, and the absorbance is weak but usable at the concentrations used in HPLC. A certificate that reports "purity 99%" without stating the wavelength, column, gradient, and injection amount is not reproducible. Two labs can report different purity for the same batch when the method differs, especially for a hydrophilic tripeptide that elutes near the solvent front.

Mass tolerance also matters. For a 342 Da molecule, a 0.5 Da window is loose enough to admit some isobaric interferences. A report that gives the observed mass to two decimal places, the calculated mass, and the charge state is more useful than one that states "mass confirmed" without values.

A certificate is a batch record, not a marketing document. The useful ones share a structure: batch identity, methods, acceptance criteria, results, and a release decision. The table below shows the fields that matter for a small peptide and an example specification range used in research-grade supply. The ranges are examples, not universal limits; the buyer's own acceptance criteria should govern.

| Field | Method | Example specification | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Batch number and manufacture date | Record | Unique, traceable to a synthesis lot | Ties the vial to the analytical work |
| Appearance | Visual | White to off-white lyophilized powder | A collapsed or discolored cake suggests a process or storage problem |
| Identity | LC-MS, [M+H]+ | 343.2 ± 0.5 m/z | Confirms the expected molecular mass |
| Purity | RP-HPLC-UV 214 nm | ≥ 98.0% area | Measures chromatographic purity, not biological activity |
| Peptide content | Nitrogen analysis or amino acid analysis | ≥ 80% by weight | Converts powder weight into actual peptide mass |
| Acetate content | Ion chromatography | 5–15% w/w | Explains part of the mass balance and affects solubility |
| Water content | Karl Fischer | ≤ 8.0% | Water inflates weight and reduces stability |
| Endotoxin | LAL, USP <85> | ≤ 10 EU/mg | Relevant to any cell-based work sensitive to pyrogens |
| Retest date | Stability protocol | Typically 24–36 months at −20 °C | Sets the usable window under stated storage |

The peptide content line is the one buyers skip most often. A 10 mg vial label is a nominal fill. If the powder is 85% peptide and 8% water, the actual peptide in the vial is roughly 8.5 mg before any transfer losses. A researcher preparing a 1 mM stock from the label weight will be off by the difference. This is not a supplier defect; it is a documentation question that the certificate should answer.

## Solubility, Salt Form, and Handling

KPV is freely water-soluble, which makes stock preparation straightforward but also creates a false sense of control. Three variables still need attention.

The first is the salt form. An acetate salt dissolves readily and is the common research form. A trifluoroacetate salt also dissolves, but residual TFA can affect cell assays at higher concentrations. The certificate should state which form was supplied.

The second is adsorption. Small, positively charged peptides can bind to glass and to some plastics. Low-binding tubes and a protein-free blocking step reduce losses at low concentrations. For cell work at nanomolar concentrations, the difference between a nominal and a delivered concentration can be large.

The third is freeze-thaw. A reconstituted solution kept at 4 °C for days will accumulate degradation products, and repeated freezing concentrates the peptide at the ice interface. Aliquoting before freezing is the standard control. The [solubility troubleshooting guide](/blog/peptide-solubility-troubleshooting/) covers buffer selection and the appearance of undissolved material.

Lyophilized powder should be stored dry, dark, and cold. The exact storage temperature belongs in the certificate and the label; a supplier that gives no storage condition is leaving the buyer to guess.

## Supplier Verification Questions

The following questions are answerable by any supplier that controls its own documentation. Vague answers are information.

1. **Who synthesized this batch, and at which site?** A trading company that cannot name the manufacturing site cannot answer questions about process changes.
2. **What is the exact sequence and expected mass?** The certificate should state Lys-Pro-Val and the calculated mass, not just the trade name.
3. **Which HPLC method was used, and at what wavelength?** Ask for the chromatogram itself, not just the percentage.
4. **Was identity confirmed by mass spectrometry, and what were the observed and calculated masses?**
5. **What is the net peptide content, and how was it measured?**
6. **Which counterion is present, and at what percentage?**
7. **What are the endotoxin and bioburden results, and which methods were used?**
8. **What is the retest date, and what stability data support it?**

## FAQ

### What is KPV?

KPV is the tripeptide Lys-Pro-Val, corresponding to residues 11–13 of alpha-melanocyte-stimulating hormone. Its average mass is 342.4 Da.

### Is KPV clinically proven?

No. The directly relevant studies are cell-culture, antimicrobial, skin-model, and mouse colitis experiments. They do not establish clinical efficacy.

### Does KPV have an established human dose?

No human dose is established by the published literature. This page provides no dosing or administration instructions.

### Why does PepT1 matter in KPV research?

The 2008 Gastroenterology study reported that KPV uptake into intestinal epithelial and immune cells depends on the PepT1 di/tripeptide transporter, and that this uptake is linked to reduced NF-κB and MAP kinase signaling.

### Can mass spectrometry alone confirm a KPV sequence?

Mass alone confirms elemental composition, not residue order. A positional isomer such as Lys-Val-Pro has the same mass. Sequence confirmation needs fragmentation data or a certified reference standard.

### What should a KPV certificate of analysis include?

Batch identity, sequence and calculated mass, LC-MS result, HPLC method and purity, net peptide content, counterion, water content, endotoxin result, storage condition, and retest date.

## The Author's Take

**Position.** KPV is a well-characterized small peptide with a plausible mechanism and a thin clinical record. Buyers should treat it as a research reagent whose value depends on identity and content documentation, not as a compound with established human benefits.

**Reasoning.** The mechanistic work is credible and reproducible in the systems studied: PepT1-mediated uptake, NF-κB and MAP kinase suppression, and reduced colitis markers in two mouse models. None of that survives translation into a human protocol without pharmacokinetic and safety work that has not been published. On the supply side, a tripeptide is easy to synthesize and easy to mislabel. The absence of an aromatic chromophore makes 280 nm quantification useless, and the presence of three possible positional isomers makes mass-only identity checks weak. A certificate that states the method, the observed mass, the net peptide content, and the counterion is doing real work; one that states a purity percentage alone is not.
