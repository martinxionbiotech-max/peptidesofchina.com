BPC-157 and TB-500 are often discussed as if they were two versions of the same research tool. They are not. BPC-157 is a synthetic 15-residue peptide with a molecular weight of about 1,419.5 Da. TB-500 is a label most often used for a 17-residue N-terminal fragment of thymosin beta-4, a 43-residue protein. The two differ in sequence, size, proposed mechanism, and the analytical risks that matter at release.

The comparison is still useful, but it should be made on the right axes: what the molecule is, which assay it fits, how it is tested, and what the certificate can prove. A page that ranks them by anecdotal “strength” is not a procurement tool.

This guide compares the two compounds from a buyer’s side of the bench: identity, purity, stability, documentation, and the economics of a vial that may contain less peptide than the label implies.

## What Each Compound Is

**BPC-157** is a pentadecapeptide with the sequence Gly-Glu-Pro-Pro-Pro-Gly-Lys-Pro-Ala-Asp-Asp-Ala-Gly-Leu-Val. It is also catalogued as PL 14736 or stable gastric pentadecapeptide. Its molecular weight is about 1,419.5 Da as the free base, and its CAS number is 137525-51-0. The sequence is short, linear, and contains no disulfide bond.

**TB-500** is not a single chemical entity. In the research market it usually refers to the acetylated N-terminal 17-residue fragment of thymosin beta-4: Ac-SDKPDMAEIEKFDKSKLK, with a mass of roughly 2 kDa. The full protein is 43 residues, about 4.9 kDa, and is also sold under the same family label by some suppliers. The sequence on the certificate is the only reliable identifier.

The length difference is the first practical distinction. A 15-mer and a 17-mer are both accessible to solid-phase synthesis, but they are not equally easy to purify, and they do not behave the same in reversed-phase chromatography. The full 43-mer is a different manufacturing problem entirely.

## Two Different Research Stories

BPC-157’s literature is dominated by rodent studies in gastrointestinal, vascular, and connective-tissue models. The proposed mechanisms include nitric-oxide signalling, VEGFR-2–dependent angiogenesis, EGR-1 induction, and changes in growth-factor expression. The reviews by Sikiric and colleagues summarize a large preclinical body of work and are candid about the gap between animal data and clinical evidence [1][2].

TB-500’s literature is anchored in thymosin beta-4 biology. The full protein sequesters actin monomers and influences filament dynamics, which makes it a tool for studying cell migration, cytoskeletal rearrangement, and tissue remodelling [3]. The 17-mer is a fragment of that protein; its research rationale is usually tied to the actin-binding region, but the evidence for the fragment is not the same as the evidence for the full protein.

Neither compound has a substantial head-to-head clinical literature against the other. That is not a criticism of either; it is a limitation of the comparison. A buyer who wants to choose between them for a study should choose based on the assay, not on a ranking.

## Where the Comparison Breaks Down

Three assumptions are common and wrong.

**Assumption 1: “They both do repair.”** That is a category description, not a mechanism. BPC-157’s proposed pathways are largely vascular and inflammatory; thymosin beta-4’s core biology is actin sequestration. A repair assay that measures one may not measure the other.

**Assumption 2: “A mg is a mg.”** A 10 mg vial of BPC-157 and a 10 mg vial of TB-500 can contain different amounts of peptide because the water content, counterion, and fill accuracy differ. The label is a nominal fill, not a net peptide measurement.

**Assumption 3: “The certificate is the product.”** A certificate can be accurate for a batch that is not the batch in the box, or accurate for a method that does not resolve the impurity that matters. The batch number and the sample point are part of the evidence.

| Question | BPC-157 | TB-500 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sequence length | 15 residues | Usually 17 residues; full protein is 43 |
| Approximate mass | 1,419.5 Da | About 2 kDa for the fragment; about 4.9 kDa for the protein |
| Terminal modification | Free N-terminus in the common form | N-terminal acetyl on the fragment and the mature protein |
| Main identity risk | Sequence variants and salt form | Fragment-versus-protein confusion; missing acetylation |
| Main purity risk | Related peptides and counterion | Truncation products and protein aggregates |
| Common assay fit | GI, vascular, connective-tissue models | Actin dynamics, cell migration, cytoskeletal models |

## Purity and Identity: Different Analytical Risks

Both products need identity, purity, and content testing, but the failure modes differ.

For BPC-157, the sequence is short enough that a competent lab can make it without unusual difficulty. The risks are a wrong sequence variant, an undeclared counterion, and a purity result that ignores water and salt. A 15-mer also has a relatively simple mass spectrum, so intact LC-MS is usually decisive for identity. The [BPC-157 COA guide](/blog/bpc-157-certificate-of-analysis-explained-a-researchers-guide-to-verification-2026/) walks through the fields.

For TB-500, the central risk is label ambiguity. A supplier may sell the 17-mer, the 43-mer, or a shorter fragment under the same name. Intact mass resolves the size; MS/MS or amino-acid analysis resolves the sequence; acetylation adds about 42 Da and should be visible in the mass. The [TB-500 versus thymosin beta-4 guide](/blog/tb-500-vs-thymosin-beta-4-what-you-re-actually-getting/) covers the naming problem in detail.

Both products should be tested by a method with a stated wavelength, gradient, and system-suitability result. A purity number without a method is not comparable across suppliers. The [HPLC versus LC-MS guide](/blog/hplc-vs-lc-ms-peptides/) explains why the two methods answer different questions.

## Stability and Handling Differences

Both compounds are normally supplied as lyophilized powders and stored at −20 °C for long-term holding, with 2–8 °C for short-term handling. The similarities end there.

BPC-157 has an unusual acid stability profile in the literature, which is part of its research history. That does not mean it is stable in every buffer or at every pH. The peptide contains multiple proline residues, and proline isomerization can occur under harsh conditions. A lyophilized cake with high residual moisture is still at risk of hydrolysis.

TB-500, as a fragment or a full protein, is more sensitive to aggregation and surface adsorption than a short peptide. The full protein has a larger hydrophobic surface and a more complex folding problem. A 17-mer is less complex, but it can still adsorb to filters and plastic at low concentrations.

For both, the practical controls are the same: sealed vials, dry storage, limited freeze–thaw cycles, protection from light, and a written excursion policy. The [lyophilized stability review](/blog/peptide-stability-why-lyophilized-peptides-are-far-more-stable-than-you-think/) explains the water and glass-transition variables.

## COA Fields for Each Compound

A certificate for either product should answer the same core questions, but the acceptable values differ.

| Field | BPC-157 | TB-500 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sequence | 15-residue sequence stated | 17-mer or 43-mer sequence stated, with residue range |
| Theoretical mass | About 1,419.5 Da | About 2.0 kDa or 4.9 kDa, depending on product |
| Observed mass | LC-MS with error | LC-MS with error; MS/MS for sequence |
| Purity | RP-HPLC area %, method stated | RP-HPLC area %, method stated |
| Counterion | Acetate, TFA, or chloride | Acetate, TFA, or chloride |
| Water | Karl Fischer, % w/w | Karl Fischer, % w/w |
| Net peptide content | Calculated from fill, water, counterion | Calculated from fill, water, counterion |
| Endotoxin | LAL result, if claimed | LAL result, if claimed |
| Storage | Label condition and retest date | Label condition and retest date |

## Sourcing Economics: Why Price per Vial Misleads

A price list usually quotes dollars per vial. The number that matters is dollars per milligram of net peptide, adjusted for purity and content. The two are not the same.

Consider an illustrative comparison. Vial A is a 10 mg fill at 80% net peptide content and sells for $50. Vial B is a 10 mg fill at 95% net peptide content and sells for $60. The sticker price favors A. The cost per milligram of peptide favors B:

- Vial A: 10 mg × 0.80 = 8.0 mg peptide; $50 ÷ 8.0 mg = $6.25 per mg.
- Vial B: 10 mg × 0.95 = 9.5 mg peptide; $60 ÷ 9.5 mg = $6.32 per mg.

The difference is small in this example, but it widens when water content and counterion are counted. A vial with 5% water and 12% counterion contains about 8.36 mg of peptide in a 10 mg fill, not 10 mg. The label is a fill target, not a peptide assay.

## A Decision Framework for Research Buyers

The choice between BPC-157 and TB-500 should follow the assay, not a popularity ranking.

1. **Define the biological question.** If the study is about actin dynamics or cell migration, the TB-4/thymosin literature is the relevant background. If it is about gastrointestinal or vascular models, BPC-157 has the denser preclinical literature.
2. **Choose the molecular form.** For TB-500, decide whether the 17-mer or the full protein is the right tool, and require the sequence on the certificate. For BPC-157, confirm the salt form and the counterion.
3. **Set the specification.** State purity, water, counterion, net peptide content, endotoxin (if needed), and the test methods.
4. **Verify the batch.** Ask for the certificate, the chromatogram, the mass spectrum, and the batch record before release.
5. **Plan the handling.** Confirm the storage condition, the reconstitution solvent, and the freeze–thaw policy.
6. **Compare cost per milligram of peptide.** Use the net content, not the label fill.

## The Author's Take

**Position.** Do not compare BPC-157 and TB-500 as if they were competing brands of the same product. Compare the molecule, the assay fit, and the batch evidence. A buyer who starts with “which is stronger?” will end up with a vial that cannot answer the study question.

**Reasoning.** The two compounds differ in length, sequence, terminal chemistry, and proposed mechanism. The analytical risks differ too: BPC-157 is mainly a sequence-and-salt problem, while TB-500 is mainly a fragment-identity problem. A supplier can be excellent on one and weak on the other. The buyer’s defense is the same in both cases: a written specification, a batch-linked certificate, a method-backed purity result, and a net peptide calculation. Those checks are cheap relative to a failed study, and they make the price comparison honest.
