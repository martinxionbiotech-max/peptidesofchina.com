Tesamorelin is a 44-amino-acid growth hormone-releasing factor analog with a molecular mass of 5,135.9 Da. Prescription tesamorelin is FDA-approved for one narrow indication: reducing excess abdominal fat in HIV-infected adults with lipodystrophy. The label explicitly states that it is not indicated for weight-loss management and that long-term cardiovascular safety has not been established.

The trial record behind that approval is specific. It shows reductions in visceral adipose tissue in adults with HIV-associated abdominal fat accumulation, measured by imaging, over six and twelve months. It does not show a general fat-loss effect, a cosmetic benefit, an anti-aging effect, or a reason to use a research vial in place of a prescribed product.

That distinction — population, endpoint, product — is the whole argument of this page.

## What Tesamorelin Is, and What Approval Covers

Tesamorelin is a synthetic analog of human growth hormone-releasing factor. The molecule comprises the 44-amino-acid sequence of human GRF with a hexenoyl moiety, a six-carbon chain with a double bond, attached at the N-terminus. The free-base molecular weight is 5,135.9 Da.

The approved product exists in two formulations that are not substitutable. EGRIFTA WR is supplied as an 11.6 mg lyophilized powder per vial; EGRIFTA SV is a 2 mg presentation. The label states that the two differ in dosage, number of vials per dose, reconstitution instructions, and storage requirements, and that they cannot be used interchangeably.

Approval is tied to the named product: its formulation, manufacturing controls, release testing, labeling, and indicated population. A research-grade tesamorelin vial is not the approved drug even if the sequence is identical, because the batch has not been made under the same control system and has not been shown to meet the same specification.

A buyer who reads "FDA approved" on a supplier page and concludes that the vial is pharmaceutical-grade has skipped that step.

## The Trial Record: 412 and 404 Adults

Falutz et al. published the pivotal trial in the *New England Journal of Medicine* in 2007. The study randomized 412 adults with HIV and abdominal fat accumulation to daily subcutaneous tesamorelin or placebo for 26 weeks. The primary endpoint was the percentage change in visceral adipose tissue measured by computed tomography.

Visceral adipose tissue decreased by 15.2% in the tesamorelin group and increased by 5.0% in the placebo group. Triglycerides fell by 50 mg/dL in the treated group and rose by 9 mg/dL with placebo. The total-cholesterol-to-HDL ratio decreased by 0.31 in the treated group and increased by 0.21 in the placebo group. IGF-1 rose by 81.0% in the treated group and fell by 5.0% with placebo. Glycemic measures did not differ significantly between groups.

Those numbers are frequently misquoted. A common error on vendor pages is to attribute the 10.9% figure from the later 404-patient study to this 412-patient trial, or to merge the two into a single "404 patients, 10.9%" statement. The studies are related but distinct, and their numbers should not be blended.

Falutz et al. published a second trial in the *Journal of Acquired Immune Deficiency Syndromes* in 2010. This one enrolled 404 adults with HIV and excess abdominal fat and ran for 12 months in two phases.

In the first six months, participants were randomized 2:1 to tesamorelin or placebo. Visceral adipose tissue decreased by 10.9%, or 21 cm², in the tesamorelin group, against 0.6%, or 1 cm², with placebo. Trunk fat, waist circumference, and waist-to-hip ratio improved, while limb and abdominal subcutaneous fat did not change. IGF-1 increased, and glucose parameters showed no significant change.

In the extension phase, participants originally assigned to tesamorelin were re-randomized to continue treatment or switch to placebo. Those who continued had a visceral fat reduction of approximately 18% at 12 months. Those who switched lost the improvement rapidly.

That reversal is the most instructive part of the study. It says the effect depends on continued exposure, which is consistent with a drug acting on visceral fat turnover rather than a one-time structural change. It also means that a short course cannot be assumed to produce a durable result.

| Study | Population | Duration | Visceral fat result | Other findings |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Falutz 2007 (NEJM) | 412 adults with HIV and abdominal fat | 26 weeks | −15.2% vs +5.0% placebo | Triglycerides −50 vs +9 mg/dL; IGF-1 +81% |
| Falutz 2010 (JAIDS) | 404 adults with HIV and abdominal fat | 6 months, extended to 12 | −10.9% vs −0.6% at 6 months; ≈−18% at 12 months | Rapid loss of effect after switch to placebo |
| Stanley 2014 (JAMA) | 50 adults with HIV and abdominal fat | 6 months | −34 cm² vs +8 cm² | Liver fat −2.0% vs +0.9%; fasting glucose +9 mg/dL at 2 weeks |

The table makes the pattern visible: the effect is consistent across trials, the population is narrow, and the endpoint is imaging-based fat measurement rather than a symptom or a clinical outcome.

## The Liver-Fat Study: 50 Patients, 6 Months

Stanley et al. published a smaller trial in *JAMA* in 2014. Fifty antiretroviral-treated adults with HIV and abdominal fat accumulation were randomized to tesamorelin or placebo for six months. The primary endpoints were changes in visceral adipose tissue and liver fat, the latter measured as the lipid-to-water percentage by magnetic resonance spectroscopy.

Visceral adipose tissue changed by −34 cm² with tesamorelin versus +8 cm² with placebo, a treatment effect of −42 cm². Liver fat changed by a median of −2.0% versus +0.9%, a net treatment effect of −2.9 percentage points. Fasting glucose rose by 9 mg/dL at two weeks in the treated group and then returned toward baseline; the six-month difference was not significant.

The authors described the study as preliminary and called for further work to determine the clinical importance and long-term consequences. That is the correct reading. A 2.9-point change in a liver-fat measure is a signal worth following up, not a demonstrated treatment for steatosis.

## IGF-1, Label Boundaries, and Where the Evidence Stops

Tesamorelin works through the GH/IGF-1 axis. The label reports that among patients who received tesamorelin for 26 weeks, 47% had IGF-1 levels above 2 standard deviation scores and 23% had levels above 3 SDS. The prescribing information instructs clinicians to monitor IGF-1 and to consider discontinuing in patients with persistent elevations.

For a research buyer, this is a reminder that the mechanism is not selective for visceral fat. The same axis drives the measured IGF-1 increase, and the label treats that increase as a safety parameter to manage, not as a benefit to advertise.

A supplier page that presents "IGF-1 increase" as a selling point has inverted the clinical framing. In the approved label, it is a monitored risk.

The prescribing information is unusually direct about limits. It states that long-term cardiovascular safety has not been established, that the product is not indicated for weight-loss management, and that there are no data to support improved compliance with antiretroviral therapy.

The label also lists contraindications: disruption of the hypothalamic-pituitary axis, active malignancy, known hypersensitivity to tesamorelin or excipients, and pregnancy. Warnings cover increased risk of neoplasms, elevated IGF-1, glucose intolerance and diabetes, fluid retention, and hypersensitivity reactions.

These are regulatory boundaries for a prescription product. They also describe the populations in which a research material has no established use. A buyer planning cell work or analytical work does not need a clinical indication, but a buyer planning anything else should understand that the label does not support it.

The trial record does not establish:

- weight-loss management in the general population;
- a cosmetic, bodybuilding, or anti-aging benefit;
- cardiovascular benefit or long-term cardiovascular safety;
- a benefit in people without HIV-associated lipodystrophy;
- equivalence between a research vial and EGRIFTA WR or EGRIFTA SV;
- a protocol for self-administration;
- long-term safety beyond the studied exposure periods.

The evidence is also bounded by its endpoints. Visceral fat measured by CT or MRI is not the same as a patient-reported outcome, a cardiovascular event, or survival. The trials were designed to show a fat-distribution effect, and they did.

## Identity Testing and Certificate Review

A 44-residue peptide is a different analytical problem from a tripeptide. The molecule is large enough that sequence coverage, disulfide status, and aggregation matter, and small enough that it is still made by solid-phase synthesis rather than recombinant expression.

| Attribute | Method | What it establishes | What it can miss |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Identity | LC-MS with deconvolution, or MALDI-TOF | Molecular mass near 5,135.9 Da | Isobaric modifications and sequence variants |
| Sequence | Peptide mapping with LC-MS/MS after enzymatic digestion | Residue-level coverage | Regions poorly ionized or poorly recovered |
| Purity | RP-HPLC-UV at 214 nm | Main-peak area percentage | Low-abundance impurities below detection |
| Counterion | Ion chromatography | Acetate or trifluoroacetate content | Counterions outside the method |
| Water | Karl Fischer | Moisture content | Residual solvents |
| Endotoxin | LAL per USP <85> | Endotoxin burden | Non-endotoxin pyrogens |
| Aggregation | Size-exclusion chromatography or dynamic light scattering | Soluble aggregates | Insoluble particles |
| Content | Amino acid analysis or nitrogen determination | Net peptide mass | Salt and water contribution |

The mass alone is weaker evidence for a 44-residue peptide than for a small molecule, because the same nominal mass can hide a sequence variant or a modified residue. Peptide mapping is the method that addresses sequence, and it is the method most often missing from a supplier certificate.

Endotoxin deserves separate attention. A peptide delivered as a lyophilized powder for cell work can carry lipopolysaccharide from water, buffers, or equipment. The LAL result should be reported in EU/mg with the method stated. The [endotoxin testing explainer](/blog/endotoxin-testing-peptide-manufacturing/) covers the difference between endotoxin and sterility.

| Field | What to look for | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sequence and mass | 44 residues; 5,135.9 Da calculated; observed mass with deconvolution | Confirms the correct molecule |
| Peptide mapping | Coverage percentage and identified modifications | Confirms sequence, not just mass |
| Purity | HPLC method, wavelength, chromatogram | Reproducible purity assessment |
| Net peptide content | Amino acid analysis or nitrogen, % by weight | Converts vial weight to peptide mass |
| Counterion | Identity and percentage | Mass balance and solubility |
| Water content | Karl Fischer result | Stability and weighing |
| Endotoxin | Method and limit, e.g. ≤10 EU/mg | Cell-work compatibility |
| Aggregation | SEC or DLS result | Handling and formulation risk |
| Storage and retest | Temperature and date | Defines usable window |

The content field is where many certificates fail. A 5 mg vial label is a nominal fill; the actual peptide mass depends on the fill weight, the water content, and the counterion. A certificate that reports only "5 mg" without a content assay leaves the buyer unable to calculate molarity accurately.

## Research Material Versus Approved Drug

The gap between a research vial and an approved product goes beyond regulation. It is a set of control decisions.

An approved product has a defined manufacturing process, validated cleaning and change control, release testing against a specification, stability data supporting the labeled shelf life, and a pharmacovigilance system. A research supplier may have some of those elements, but the buyer cannot assume them from the word "tesamorelin" on a label.

Three questions separate the two categories.

**Is the sequence confirmed, or only the mass?** A 5,135 Da signal is consistent with many 44-residue sequences. Peptide mapping is the discriminating test.

**Is the content measured, or only the fill weight?** A net peptide content assay tells the buyer what is in the vial. A fill weight tells them what the filling machine dispensed.

**Is the counterion specified?** Acetate and trifluoroacetate salts behave differently in solution and in cell assays. The certificate should say which one is present.

A supplier that answers all three is operating at a documented standard. A supplier that answers none is selling a label.

## Supplier Verification Questions

1. **What is the exact sequence, and is it confirmed by peptide mapping?**
2. **What is the observed mass after deconvolution, and how does it compare with the calculated 5,135.9 Da?**
3. **Which HPLC method was used, and can you provide the chromatogram?**
4. **What is the net peptide content, and how was it measured?**
5. **Which counterion is present, and at what percentage?**
6. **What are the endotoxin and bioburden results?**
7. **Is aggregation measured, and by which method?**
8. **Who manufactures the batch, and what is the retest date?**

## FAQ

### What is tesamorelin approved for?

Prescription tesamorelin is indicated for reducing excess abdominal fat in HIV-infected adults with lipodystrophy. The label states it is not indicated for weight-loss management.

### Is tesamorelin a general weight-loss drug?

No. The approved indication is narrow, and the label excludes weight-loss management. The trials enrolled adults with HIV and abdominal fat accumulation.

### What did the pivotal trial measure?

The 2007 NEJM trial measured percentage change in visceral adipose tissue by CT over 26 weeks, with lipid and IGF-1 endpoints as secondary measures.

### Did the effect persist after stopping?

No. In the 12-month study, participants who switched from tesamorelin to placebo lost the visceral fat improvement rapidly.

### Can a research vial be substituted for the approved drug?

No. Approval applies to a specific finished product, formulation, manufacturing control system, and labeled indication. A research vial is not that product.

### Does this page provide a treatment protocol?

No. It summarizes trial evidence and regulatory boundaries without giving administration instructions.

## The Author's Take

**Position.** Tesamorelin has a real, replicated effect on visceral fat in one narrow population, and almost nothing else. The clinical record is stronger than most peptide pages suggest and narrower than most marketing implies.

**Reasoning.** Three randomized trials, including one with 412 participants and one with 404, show consistent reductions in CT- or MRI-measured visceral fat, with the effect reversing after withdrawal. That is a solid pharmacodynamic story. It is also a story about HIV-associated lipodystrophy, not about general adiposity, and the label's own warnings treat the IGF-1 rise as a risk to monitor. On the supply side, a 44-residue peptide cannot be identified by mass alone; peptide mapping, net content, counterion, and aggregation data are what separate a characterized batch from a labelled vial. Most certificates I see for tesamorelin stop at mass and purity, which is the minimum, not the standard.
