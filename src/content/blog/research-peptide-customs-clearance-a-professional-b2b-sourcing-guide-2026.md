Customs clearance for research peptides rests on four controls: a description that matches the material, a tariff classification that matches the description, a document set that agrees line by line, and the destination's current import rules. None of these is settled by the supplier, and none is guaranteed by a shipping agent. The importer of record owns the declaration, the duty, and any permit or restriction attached to the goods.

Requirements vary by jurisdiction, and they change. The buyer is responsible for confirming the applicable rules with the destination customs authority or a licensed broker before dispatch. This article is a procurement guide, not legal or customs advice, and it does not describe how to avoid any rule.

## What Customs Reviews Before Release

A customs officer does not open a vial to check purity. Release decisions come from the entry data, the supporting documents, and risk rules applied to both. The practical sequence is short: the manifest arrives, the entry is filed, the system screens the parties and the commodity, and any agency flag routes the file to a second reviewer. A peptide entry can be flagged for health, environment, or drug-control reasons even when the invoice is internally consistent.

Three consequences follow.

First, documentation quality is the variable a buyer can control. A clean set does not guarantee release, but an inconsistent set reliably creates delay.

Second, a clearance event says nothing about identity, purity, or sterility. Customs release is an administrative decision, not an analytical result. A released shipment can still fail a buyer's own incoming test, and a held shipment can be analytically perfect.

Third, the declared use matters. A shipment described as a therapeutic or consumer product invites a different regulatory path than a lot described as a laboratory reagent with a research-use-only restriction. The description has to be true. Changing the words without changing the facts is misdeclaration, and the importer signs the entry.

The broker is an agent, not a shield. A customs broker files what the importer supplies and keeps its own records, but the importer remains liable for the accuracy of the declaration and for the duty owed. In practice that means reading the entry summary before it is filed, not after a claim arrives. A power of attorney authorizes the broker to act; it does not transfer responsibility. The same logic applies in the EU and the UK, where an EORI number identifies the trader but does not make the customs agent the party that owes the duty.

## The Document Pack: Field by Field

The commercial invoice anchors the entry. Every other document is checked against it, which is why a single mismatch — a quantity, a lot number, a name — can stop an otherwise compliant shipment.

| Document | Who issues it | What the broker checks | Common defect |
|---|---|---|---|
| Commercial invoice | Exporter | Description, quantity, value, currency, origin, terms | Generic description such as "chemical samples" |
| Packing list | Exporter | Carton count, vial count, net and gross weight | Vial count does not match the invoice line |
| Certificate of origin | Chamber of commerce or authorized body | Origin statement and consignee details | Supplier letter presented as a certificate |
| Safety data sheet | Manufacturer | Hazard classification and transport information | SDS covers a different salt form |
| Batch certificate of analysis | Manufacturer | Lot number, methods, specifications | Lot on the COA differs from the label |
| Import permit or licence | Destination authority, where required | Scope, quantity, validity dates | Permit expires before arrival |
| Air waybill or bill of lading | Carrier | Piece count, weight, consignee | House and master documents disagree |
| Label artwork | Exporter | Product name, quantity, storage, research-use statement | Label carries a therapeutic claim |

The invoice has to reflect an actual sale, not a pro forma estimate, when goods move under a purchase. Where no sale occurred — a sample, a replacement, a transfer — the valuation basis changes and the invoice has to explain the transaction rather than present a price.

## Commercial Invoice Fields That Cause Delays

United States rules give a concrete checklist. Under 19 CFR 141.86, an import invoice must state:

1. The port of entry to which the merchandise is destined.
2. The time and place of the sale, the seller, and the buyer.
3. A detailed description, including the name the item is known by, its grade or quality, and the marks and numbers on the packages.
4. Quantities in the exporter's units or in United States units.
5. The purchase price per item, in the currency of purchase.
6. All charges itemized by name and amount: freight, insurance, commission, containers, and packing.
7. The country of origin.
8. The name of a responsible employee of the exporter who can answer questions about the transaction.

Two details catch buyers repeatedly. The invoice and all attachments must be in English or carry an accurate translation, per 141.86(d). And the invoice must state in adequate detail what merchandise sits in each individual package, per 141.86(e) — which is why a packing list that merely repeats "10 boxes" is not enough.

Valuation is a separate exercise from the price on the page. Assists, packing, and selling commissions can enter the customs value; post-importation costs generally do not. A broker should see the full commercial terms before deciding what belongs in the declared value. Marking a commercial shipment as a gift, a sample, or a document set is a common shortcut that fails on the first inspection.

## Packing List Details that Matter

A packing list is an operational document, and customs reads it as evidence. The useful version maps every carton to a line item: carton number, product, lot, vial count per kit, net weight of peptide, gross weight including packaging, dimensions, and package marks. The totals must reconcile with the invoice at both the line level and the shipment level.

For a bulk order built from kits, state the kit configuration explicitly. "One kit, ten vials" is a different declaration from "ten vials" when the kit includes diluent, inserts, or a carton. If a carton contains mixed lots, list each lot and its quantity rather than averaging them into one line.

Ask for the packing list before the goods leave the factory. Reconstructing it after a hold is possible, but the delay is measured in days, and storage charges accrue at the destination, not at the origin.

## HS Classification: One Code Does Not Fit Every Peptide

The Harmonized System places peptides in several headings depending on structure, function, and presentation. The most cited heading is 2937, which covers hormones, prostaglandins, thromboxanes, and leukotrienes, natural or reproduced by synthesis, and derivatives "including chain modified polypeptides, used primarily as hormones." That qualifier does real work. A peptide that is not used primarily as a hormone does not automatically belong in 2937.

Other headings that appear in peptide entries include 2934 for other heterocyclic compounds and nucleic acids, 3504 for peptones and their derivatives, and 3822 for prepared laboratory reagents. Chapter 29 has its own legal notes, including the requirement that a separate chemically defined organic compound be exactly that. A mixture, a kit, or a formulated reagent changes the analysis.

The rate attached to a heading is not a property of the peptide. On the current United States tariff schedule, 2937.19.00 carries a free Column 1 General rate and a 25 percent Column 2 rate. The same material, from a different origin, can land under a different column or a trade program. Reading only the first number produces a wrong budget.

Two controls reduce classification risk. First, ask the supplier for the technical basis of the code in writing: structure, salt form, function, and packaging. Second, for a recurring import, request a binding ruling from the destination authority. In the United States, CBP issues prospective rulings under 19 CFR Part 177, and the CROSS database publishes them. A ruling takes time; a misclassification penalty takes longer.

A code selected because it carries a lower rate, without a technical basis, is a misdeclaration. The duty saved is smaller than the cost of a post-entry audit.

## Incoterms: Who Owns the Customs Step

Incoterms 2020 allocate cost, risk, and some obligations, but they do not determine who is the importer of record. That status comes from the law and the declaration, not from a three-letter rule printed on the invoice.

- **EXW** places the goods at the seller's premises. The buyer arranges export and import clearance, a poor fit for a first-time importer.
- **FCA** has the seller clear export and hand the goods to the carrier. The buyer handles import.
- **DAP** has the seller deliver to a named place, ready for unloading, with the buyer clearing import and paying duty and tax.
- **DDP** has the seller clear import and pay duty. Even here, some jurisdictions require a local importer of record, so the buyer can remain legally exposed.

Name the place precisely and cite the version: "DAP Rotterdam, Incoterms 2020." An invoice that says only "DDP" invites a dispute about which charges were included. If the buyer is not the importer of record, the buyer may still need to authorize the broker, supply an importer number, and accept the entry record.

Freight terms also affect the declared value and the insurance claim path. A shipment damaged in transit is a different problem when the buyer held risk from the factory gate than when the seller held it to the destination.

Write the named place into the sales contract, not just the invoice. A DAP term that names only a city leaves the final delivery point ambiguous, and the ambiguity surfaces when a carrier charges for a liftgate, an appointment, or a second attempt. For temperature-sensitive cargo, the contract should also state who pays for cold-chain handling if the shipment is held, because customs storage is rarely refrigerated.

## Certificate of Origin and Origin Claims

A certificate of origin is issued by a chamber of commerce or another authorized body. A supplier's own letter is a statement, not a certificate, and a broker will read it accordingly.

Origin is not the same as the shipping route. A product consolidated in Singapore can still be of Chinese origin if it was wholly obtained or substantially transformed there. Preferential treatment under a trade agreement requires the specific proof that agreement recognizes — often a statement on origin with a prescribed data set, not a generic chamber certificate. Non-preferential certificates serve a different purpose and do not create a duty preference.

The invoice must state the country of origin as a field, not as an inference from the address. If the supplier cannot state origin in writing, the buyer cannot declare it accurately.

## Destination Checks and a Stop-Shipment Playbook

Requirements differ by jurisdiction, and the differences are not cosmetic. The table below lists the first checks, not a complete compliance program. Each entry still depends on the material, the salt form, the quantity, and the end use.

| Jurisdiction | Classification reference | Entry system | First check for a peptide shipment |
|---|---|---|---|
| United States | HTSUS, with Chapter 29 legal notes | CBP entry; CROSS for rulings | PGA flags, TSCA certification where applicable, importer number |
| European Union | Combined Nomenclature and TARIC | Member State customs system under the UCC | TARIC measures, import licence, VAT treatment |
| United Kingdom | UK Trade Tariff | HMRC Customs Declaration Service | Commodity code, preference claim, GB EORI |

When a shipment is stopped, the sequence that works is unglamorous. Get the exact request in writing. Identify which document the officer is questioning. Answer that question with a corrected document, not a narrative about the supplier's quality system. Keep the broker in the loop, because the broker filed the entry and can amend it. Log the incident against the lot number so the same defect does not repeat on the next order.

Retain the entry file. In the United States, records relating to an entry must be kept for five years from the date of entry under 19 CFR 163.4. A buyer who cannot produce the invoice, packing list, and ruling relied on at entry has no defense in an audit, even if the original declaration was correct.

Do not instruct a supplier to re-label, under-declare, or split a shipment to avoid a threshold. Those actions convert a documentation problem into an enforcement problem, and the importer carries the exposure. A neutral outer carton is not concealment by itself; a false description inside it is.

## The Author's Take

**Position:** The lowest-risk customs strategy is boring, consistent documentation — one description, one code, one origin, one value, repeated across every document.

**Reasoning:** Most holds described to me are not caused by a prohibited compound. They are caused by an invoice that says "peptide sample," a COA with a different lot, or a packing list that does not reconcile. Each of those is fixable before dispatch and expensive after arrival. The duty difference between two plausible codes is usually smaller than a week of storage, a broker fee, and a reshipment. Paying for a binding ruling on a recurring import is cheaper than treating each entry as a fresh guess.
