Vendor documentation supports a grant in one practical way: it lets a reviewer, a purchasing office, or an auditor trace a specific vial back to a specific batch and a specific test result. It does not earn points for being glossy, and it does not substitute for a sound experimental design. What it does is remove a class of questions that can stall a purchase, weaken a methods section, or turn a routine audit into a week of reconstruction.

The distinction matters because peptide suppliers often treat documentation as marketing collateral. A certificate with a logo, a purity badge, and no lot number is a brochure. A grant file needs records that connect the material to the claim. This guide explains which documents carry weight, how they map to common grant requirements, and where the chain usually breaks.

## What grant reviewers actually ask

Reviewers evaluate the science, not the supplier's certificate. The documentation question enters through three doors.

The first is procurement review. A sponsored project usually has to follow the funder's procurement standards and the institution's purchasing policy. The file has to show what was bought, from whom, at what price, and why that supplier was selected. For a sole-source purchase, the justification has to be more than "the lab has used them before." A specification and a comparison of what other suppliers could document is a stronger record.

The second is the methods section. A peptide is a reagent, and a reagent needs an identity, a source, and a purity specification. Journals increasingly ask for the supplier, the catalog number, the lot number, and the characterization data. A methods section that says "peptide was obtained commercially" is incomplete when the result depends on which peptide arrived.

The third is audit. Federal awards are subject to record retention and internal-control requirements. The exact rules depend on the funder and the institution, but the pattern is consistent: the organization has to keep records that support the expenditure, and it has to be able to produce them on request. A vendor file that cannot be tied to an invoice is a finding waiting to happen. The [documentation package guide](/blog/peptide-documentation-package/) describes the records a supplier should be able to assemble.

## The four documents that carry the most weight

Most of the value sits in four documents. A supplier that can produce these four, consistently across lots, is easier to defend than a supplier with a longer but less connected file.

| Document | What it should contain | Grant or audit use |
|---|---|---|
| Certificate of analysis | Product name, lot number, sequence or specification reference, test methods, acceptance limits, results, release date, signatory | Ties the purchased material to a release decision |
| Analytical raw data | Chromatogram with method header and integration; mass spectrum with observed and calculated mass | Lets a reviewer or auditor check the result rather than accept the conclusion |
| Batch record extract | Lot number, manufacturing site, scale, production date, deviation summary | Shows that the lot has a manufacturing history and a site of origin |
| Commercial documents | Quotation, purchase order, invoice, packing list, and shipping record with matching values | Ties the payment to the material and the shipment |

The order matters. A certificate without a lot number cannot be matched to the invoice. A chromatogram without a method header cannot be reproduced. A batch record without a site name cannot be audited. Each document is useful only when the identifier fields agree.

The [quality testing hub](/peptide-quality-testing/) explains how these documents fit into a supplier qualification. A useful exercise is to reconcile four identifiers before filing the purchase: the invoice entity, the certificate issuer, the lot number, and the vial label. If any two disagree, the file has a gap. The [COA authentication guide](/blog/coa-verified-peptides-supplier-how-to-authenticate-research-purity-in-2026) walks through that reconciliation in detail.

## How a COA maps to a grant file

A certificate is not a single document; it is a set of fields. The fields that matter to a grant file are the ones that answer who, what, when, and how.

| COA field | Example entry | Why a reviewer or auditor cares |
|---|---|---|
| Product and lot | Retatrutide, lot RTT-241018 | Connects the certificate to the vial and the invoice |
| Sequence or reference | Sequence on file; specification revision 2.1 | Defines what "the product" means beyond a trade name |
| Purity method | RP-HPLC, C18, 214 nm, gradient table attached | Makes the purity figure reproducible |
| Purity result | 98.6% by area normalization | States the result and the calculation basis |
| Identity method | LC-ESI-MS, monoisotopic mass, ±50 ppm | Confirms the main component |
| Water content | 3.2% by Karl Fischer | Supports weighing and stability assumptions |
| Counterion | Trifluoroacetate, 6.8% by ion chromatography | Explains net peptide content |
| Endotoxin | &lt;0.5 EU/mg by chromogenic LAL, spike recovery 92% | Supports the pyrogen claim where required |
| Release date and signatory | 2026-10-21; QA manager | Establishes the release decision and accountability |

Two fields are often missing and both are easy to request. The first is the method reference. A result without a method is a number without a context. The second is the signatory. A certificate that ends with a generic "QC department" stamp is harder to authenticate than one with a named release function and a date.

For a grant file, the certificate should be stored with the purchase order and the receiving record, not in a separate folder organized by product. The point of the file is to answer a question about a transaction, and the transaction is the unit that gets audited.

## Record retention and the three-year baseline

In the United States, 2 CFR 200.334 sets a baseline retention period of three years from the date the final expenditure report is submitted for federal awards, with exceptions that can extend the period. The rule is a floor, not a ceiling: institutional policy, sponsor terms, litigation holds, and patent or publication requirements can require longer retention.

For peptide purchases, the practical implication is that the vendor file should be treated as part of the award record. That means the certificate, the invoice, the receiving inspection, and any correspondence about a nonconformance should be kept together for at least the applicable period. A PDF in a personal inbox is not a record. A shared drive folder with a naming convention and a backup is.

Electronic records add a second layer. If the file relies on electronic signatures or an electronic quality system, 21 CFR Part 11 sets out the requirements for validated systems, audit trails, and access controls. A supplier that sends a scanned certificate with a typed signature is not automatically noncompliant; the buyer's own recordkeeping system may still be fine. But a buyer who intends to rely on the supplier's electronic record should ask how the record is controlled and whether an audit trail exists.

The FDA's 2018 guidance on data integrity and CGMP is a useful reference for the questions to ask, even when the peptide is a research reagent rather than a drug. It describes the expectation that records be attributable, legible, contemporaneous, original, and accurate. Those attributes are a good test for any vendor file, regardless of the regulatory status of the material.

## Documentation for the methods section

A publications committee or a journal editor will rarely ask for a full vendor audit file. They will ask for enough information to repeat the experiment. The minimum is the supplier, the product name, the catalog number, the lot number, the purity specification, and the method used to determine it.

The [three-stage verification process](/blog/how-we-verify-product-quality-our-3-step-approach/) describes how a lab can confirm the material independently. A practical sentence for a methods section looks like this: "Peptide X was obtained from Supplier Y (catalog number, lot number) at ≥98% purity by RP-HPLC; identity was confirmed by LC-MS." That sentence is short, but every clause depends on a document the supplier provided. If the lot number is missing, the sentence cannot be written. If the purity method is unknown, the specification is not reproducible.

For studies that compare batches, the methods section should also state how the lots were selected and whether any lot was excluded. A supplementary table with lot numbers, purity results, and testing dates is more useful than a paragraph of reassurance. The [characterization reporting checklist](/blog/peptide-characterization-reporting-checklist/) lists the fields that belong in that table.

A grant application can use the same logic in the "materials and methods" or "preliminary data" section. The point is not to impress the reviewer with documentation. The point is to show that the material is defined well enough for the result to mean something.

## Where vendor files fail

Most failures are mundane. They cluster into four patterns.

**Lot mismatch.** The certificate names one lot, the vial label names another, and the invoice names a third. This can happen when a supplier substitutes a batch to meet a shipping date. It is also the pattern that a buyer is least likely to notice until an audit. The fix is to reconcile the three identifiers at receiving, before the vials are put into inventory.

**Undated or undated-method certificates.** A certificate with a result but no release date, or a purity result with no method, is difficult to defend. The buyer should request a corrected certificate rather than annotate the file with an explanation. An annotation is not a record.

**A certificate that describes a product family, not a lot.** Some suppliers issue a generic certificate for a product and attach it to every shipment. A generic certificate can be genuine and still be useless for a grant file, because it does not identify the batch that was tested. Ask for a lot-specific certificate, even if the format is less polished.

**Missing nonconformance records.** A supplier that has never had a deviation is either very good or not recording deviations. The buyer should ask what happens when a lot fails a specification, who makes the disposition decision, and what the customer receives. A written procedure is more informative than a promise. ## A purchase-order checklist

The checklist below is designed to be attached to a purchase requisition. It is short enough to use on every order and specific enough to create a file that can survive a review.

- Define the product by sequence or specification reference, not by trade name alone.
- State the lot or batch requirement in the purchase order, with a right to reject a substituted lot.
- Require a lot-specific certificate with methods, limits, results, release date, and signatory.
- Require the chromatogram and mass spectrum, or state that they will be provided on request.
- Require water content and counterion results where they affect weighing or net peptide content.
- Require a statement of the manufacturing site and the legal entity that issued the certificate.
- State the Incoterm, the importer of record, and the documents that must accompany the shipment.
- State the nonconformance remedy: replacement, credit, or refund, and who pays for return shipping.
- Record the receiving inspection, including vial count, label lot, appearance, and storage location.
- File the certificate, invoice, packing list, and receiving record under the award or project number.

The purchase order is the right place for these requirements because it is the document the supplier accepts. A requirement that exists only in an email is easy to lose. A requirement in the purchase order is part of the contract.

## What documentation cannot prove

A complete file does not prove that the peptide will work in a given assay. It does not prove that the batch is free of every impurity, that the sequence is correct at every residue, or that the material is stable under the lab's storage conditions. Those are separate questions, answered by separate tests.

A certificate also does not transfer responsibility to the supplier for the buyer's use. The research-use boundary remains with the buyer. Documentation is evidence about identity, purity, and release; it is not a warranty of experimental outcome.

What the file does is narrow the uncertainty. It lets the lab distinguish a reagent problem from a protocol problem, and it gives the institution a defensible record if the purchase is questioned. That is a modest benefit, but it is the one that matters in a grant-funded environment.

## The Author's Take

**Position.** A grant file does not need a beautiful certificate. It needs a chain: invoice to purchase order to lot to certificate to analytical raw data. If any link is missing, the rest of the file is decoration.

**Reasoning.** Auditors and reviewers ask transaction-level questions, not brand-level ones. They want to know what was bought, when, from whom, and how the result was verified. A supplier who can produce a lot-specific certificate, a chromatogram with a method header, and a batch record extract answers those questions without a follow-up. A supplier who can only produce a product brochure creates work for the lab. The cost of assembling a proper file is small at the point of purchase and large after an audit begins, which is why the request belongs in the purchase order rather than in a later email.
