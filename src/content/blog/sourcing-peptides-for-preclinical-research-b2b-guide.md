Technical Verification: Beyond the Certificate of Analysis (COA) In the context of sourcing peptides for preclinical research , a Certificate of Analysis (COA) is frequently treated as a definitive guarantee of quality. This is a mistake. A COA is a manufacturer's self-issued claim, and in the wholesale market, it represents a baseline technical reference rather than an objective truth. Fraudulent "dry-labbing"—the practice of generating analytical reports without performing the actual tests—remains a persistent risk. For professional procurement, verification must move beyond static paperwork and into the realm of raw data analysis. High-Performance Liquid Chromatography (HPLC) remains the primary method for determining chemical purity, as it separates the target peptide from residual solvents and synthesis byproducts. While HPLC measures how much of the substance is the target compound, Mass Spectrometry (MS) confirms the identity of that compound by measuring its mass-to-charge ratio. Without both sets of data, there's no way to confirm that a batch of Tirzepatide or Semaglutide is chemically accurate. Relying on a reseller's word is a liability; relying on verifiable, batch-specific raw data is a necessity. Interpreting HPLC and Mass Spec Reports Reading a chromatogram requires looking for a single, sharp peak with minimal "noise" or secondary peaks. Secondary peaks often indicate contaminants like trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) or truncated sequences where the amino acid chain failed to complete. Mass Spec reports must align with the theoretical molecular weight of the peptide. If the mass-to-charge ratio is off by even a small margin, the sequence is likely incorrect. Because synthesis errors are common in high-volume production, professional institutions should conduct independent peptide testing upon receipt.
