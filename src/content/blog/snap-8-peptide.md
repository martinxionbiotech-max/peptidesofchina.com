SNAP-8 is the cosmetic ingredient name for acetyl octapeptide-3, an eight-residue peptide modelled on the N-terminal region of SNAP-25. Its sequence is Ac-Glu-Glu-Met-Gln-Arg-Arg-Ala-Asp-NH₂, its molecular weight is about 1,075 Da, and its CAS number is 868844-74-0.

The name describes a sequence analogy, not a proven clinical effect. SNAP-25 is part of the SNARE complex that mediates vesicle fusion, and the peptide was designed to interfere with that assembly. The hypothesis is coherent. The direct human evidence for the isolated octapeptide is thin, and most published cosmetic studies tested finished formulations with several actives.

That gap matters for sourcing. A buyer can purchase SNAP-8 as a research-grade lyophilized powder or as a cosmetic raw material in solution. The two are not the same product, even when the INCI name matches. This guide explains what to specify, how to test it, and where the marketing claims outrun the data.

## What SNAP-8 Is

SNAP-8 is a synthetic octapeptide with an acetylated N-terminus and an amidated C-terminus. The terminal groups are part of the molecule, not processing artefacts. The acetyl group adds about 42 Da to the mass; the amide adds about 1 Da relative to the free acid.

| Property | Value | Consequence for buyers |
| --- | --- | --- |
| INCI name | Acetyl Octapeptide-3 | The label name used in cosmetic formulations |
| Sequence | Ac-Glu-Glu-Met-Gln-Arg-Arg-Ala-Asp-NH₂ | Identity must be confirmed against this sequence |
| Residues | 8 | Short enough for routine solid-phase synthesis |
| Molecular weight | About 1,075 Da | Intact mass is diagnostic for terminal groups |
| CAS number | 868844-74-0 | Useful for catalog matching, not a purity claim |
| Appearance | White to off-white powder | Visual check only; not an identity test |
| Solubility | Freely soluble in water | Solution stability is a separate question |

The sequence is the core specification. A supplier that lists “SNAP-8” without the sequence is asking the buyer to trust a trade name. The [molecular weight verification guide](/blog/peptide-molecular-weight-verification-steps/) explains how intact mass and sequence coverage work together.

## The SNARE Hypothesis, and Where the Evidence Stops

SNAP-25 is one of three proteins that form the SNARE complex. The complex brings a synaptic vesicle into contact with the plasma membrane and drives fusion. Söllner and colleagues identified the SNAP receptors in 1993, and Sutton and colleagues resolved the crystal structure of the core complex in 1998 [1][2]. That work established the structural basis for the hypothesis.

SNAP-8 copies eight residues from the N-terminal region of SNAP-25. The proposed mechanism is competitive: the peptide occupies a site that the native protein would use, slowing assembly and reducing vesicle fusion. Because the interaction is reversible, the effect would be titratable, unlike botulinum toxin, which cleaves SNAP-25 irreversibly.

The step from “binds a site” to “reduces exocytosis in human skin” is not automatic. It requires evidence of skin penetration, local concentration at the relevant site, target engagement, and a measurable functional effect. A plausible pathway is not the same as a demonstrated outcome. The formulation and penetration issues that sit between a sequence and a result are covered in the [solubility and handling guide](/blog/peptide-solubility-troubleshooting/).

## What the Published Studies Actually Tested

The published cosmetic literature on acetyl octapeptide-3 is small. Much of the peer-reviewed work on SNAP-25-derived peptides concerns acetyl hexapeptide-8, a shorter sequence with a similar proposed mechanism. Blanes-Mira and colleagues described the hexapeptide as an antiwrinkle peptide in a 2002 study, and that work is often cited as the template for the octapeptide [3].

The studies that do include acetyl octapeptide-3 tend to test finished formulations: a cream, a serum, or a microneedle patch that contains several actives and a delivery system. Those designs can show that the formulation changed a measured skin parameter. They cannot show that the peptide alone caused the change. Gorouhi and Maibach reviewed topical peptides and noted that the evidence for many cosmetic peptides is limited by formulation complexity and small study sizes [4]. Schagen reached a similar conclusion in a review of topical peptide treatments [5].

That is not a reason to reject the ingredient. It is a reason to describe it accurately. A supplier page that calls SNAP-8 “Botox-like” is making a drug claim, not a cosmetic claim. The regulatory boundary is discussed below.

## Two Product Grades, Two Different Specifications

SNAP-8 is sold in two broad forms: a research-grade lyophilized powder and a cosmetic-grade raw material, often as a solution or a powder blended with a carrier. The grade determines the documentation, the tests, and the permissible use.

| Attribute | Research grade | Cosmetic ingredient grade |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Form | Lyophilized powder, often 10 mg | Powder or solution, sometimes with preservative |
| Identity | LC-MS mass and sequence | Often certificate of analysis plus supplier spec |
| Purity | HPLC area %, method stated | Assay and impurity profile, method stated |
| Microbial limits | Endotoxin and bioburden if claimed | ISO 17516 limits, preservative system if solution |
| Documentation | Batch certificate, MSDS, storage sheet | Specification, SDS, INCI statement, allergen statement |
| Intended use | Laboratory research | Formulation development and finished cosmetics |
| Regulatory status | Research use only | Cosmetic ingredient; finished product compliance rests with the brand |

A cosmetic-grade ingredient is not automatically lower quality. It is a different specification. A cosmetic supplier may not provide an LC-MS spectrum for every lot because the finished product does not require it. A research buyer who needs sequence-level identity should not assume that a cosmetic certificate covers it.

The reverse is also true. A research-grade certificate with a 98% HPLC area result does not establish that the material meets cosmetic microbial limits or that it is free of a preservative that would be unacceptable in the intended formulation.

## Identity Testing: Mass, Acetylation, and Amidation

Intact mass is the first identity check. The calculated mass of the acetylated, amidated octapeptide is about 1,075 Da, so the protonated ion should appear near m/z 1076 in positive-ion mode. The certificate should show the spectrum and the observed mass, not just a calculated value.

Three mass differences are diagnostic:

- **About 42 Da lower:** consistent with a missing acetyl group.
- **About 1 Da higher:** consistent with the free acid instead of the C-terminal amide.
- **Larger shifts:** consistent with a truncated sequence, a different salt, or a modification.

A single nominal mass is not enough. The report should include the observed mass, the expected mass, the mass error, and the ion mode. For a peptide of this size, a modern LC-MS can achieve mass accuracy in the low parts-per-million range, but the report still needs to show the spectrum.

Sequence confirmation is a separate layer. MS/MS fragmentation or amino-acid analysis can confirm the order of residues. For an eight-residue peptide, MS/MS coverage is usually straightforward, but the terminal groups may not be fully resolved by fragmentation alone. The mass shift is often the clearer evidence for acetylation and amidation.

## Purity and Assay Are Not the Same Number

Purity by HPLC area percentage describes the proportion of the UV signal that belongs to the main peak. Assay describes the amount of the target peptide in the sample, usually against a reference standard. A product can be 98% pure by area and 85% peptide by mass once water, counterion, and residual solvents are counted. The [purity versus concentration guide](/blog/why-purity-and-concentration-tests-are-not-enough/) explains the calculation.

| Test | Method | What it answers |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Purity | RP-HPLC, 214 or 220 nm | Main peak as a percentage of total peak area |
| Assay | HPLC against a reference standard, or nitrogen determination | Mass fraction of the target peptide |
| Water | Karl Fischer | Residual moisture |
| Counterion | Ion chromatography or titration | Acetate, TFA, or chloride content |
| Residual solvents | GC, if claimed | Process solvent carryover |
| Heavy metals | ICP-MS | Elemental impurities |
| Microbial limits | ISO 17516 methods | Aerobic count and yeast/mold count |

The HPLC method matters. A shallow gradient may not resolve the acetylated peptide from the unacetylated precursor or from truncated sequences. The certificate should state the column, mobile phase, gradient, wavelength, and integration method. A chromatogram without those details cannot be reproduced. The [COA reading guide](/blog/how-to-read-a-peptide-coa-a-researchers-guide-to-hplc-and-ms-reports/) walks through the fields, and the [HPLC versus LC-MS guide](/blog/hplc-vs-lc-ms-peptides/) explains why the two methods answer different questions.

## Solubility, Adsorption, and Formulation Behaviour

SNAP-8 is freely soluble in water, but solubility is not the same as stability. A clear solution can still lose peptide to adsorption, aggregation, or hydrolysis. Three practical points matter for formulation work:

1. **Adsorption.** Short peptides can bind to plastic, glass, and filter membranes. At low concentrations, the loss can be a meaningful fraction of the amount delivered. Use low-binding containers and avoid unnecessary filtration.
2. **pH and buffer.** The peptide contains acidic and basic residues, so its charge state changes with pH. The formulation pH should be set from a stability study, not from a generic “pH 5–7” assumption.
3. **Preservation.** Aqueous cosmetic formulations need a preservative system or a single-use format. The preservative can interact with the peptide, and the interaction should be tested rather than assumed.

Skin penetration is a separate constraint. The peptide is about 1,075 Da, above the 500 Da rule of thumb for passive diffusion across intact stratum corneum. Formulations often use penetration enhancers, microneedles, or delivery systems to overcome that limit. The presence of such a system in a study is a clue that the peptide alone may not penetrate effectively.

## Microbial and Heavy-Metal Limits

A cosmetic raw material needs a microbiological specification. ISO 17516 provides a framework for cosmetic microbiological limits, with category-based limits for aerobic mesophilic count and yeast/mold count [8]. A common general specification is ≤1,000 CFU/g aerobic mesophilic count and ≤100 CFU/g yeast and mold, but the correct limit depends on the product category and the market.

Heavy metals are a separate specification. ICP-MS can quantify lead, cadmium, arsenic, and mercury at low parts-per-billion levels. The acceptable limit depends on the market and the finished product. A supplier should state the method and the result, not just “passed.”

For a research-grade powder, endotoxin and bioburden may be relevant if the material is used in cell culture. For a cosmetic ingredient, microbial limits and preservative efficacy are the relevant controls. The two specifications should not be mixed.

## Regulatory Boundaries: INCI, Claims, and Market Access

In the European Union, cosmetic products are governed by Regulation (EC) No 1223/2009. The regulation requires an INCI ingredient declaration, a cosmetic product safety report, and substantiation for claims [6]. A peptide can be used as a cosmetic ingredient, but the finished product must comply with the regulation. The ingredient supplier’s certificate is part of the evidence, not a substitute for the brand’s safety assessment.

In the United States, FDA regulates cosmetics but does not approve cosmetic products or ingredients before marketing. The FDA’s authority page explains the distinction between cosmetic and drug claims [7]. A product that claims to treat or prevent disease, or to alter body structure or function, may be regulated as a drug. “Botox-like” is a drug-style claim, and it is the kind of language that creates regulatory risk for a brand.

The practical rule for suppliers and buyers is to keep claims at the formulation level: what the ingredient is, what it was tested for, and what the finished product is intended to do. Do not import clinical language from a different product or a different study.

## A Buyer's Specification Checklist

A SNAP-8 purchase order should state the following, in writing:

1. **Identity.** INCI name, sequence, CAS number, and terminal modifications.
2. **Grade.** Research-grade powder or cosmetic-grade raw material.
3. **Assay.** Minimum peptide content by a named method.
4. **Purity.** HPLC area percentage with the method and limit.
5. **Water.** Karl Fischer limit and sample point.
6. **Counterion.** Identity and limit.
7. **Microbial limits.** ISO 17516 category and results, for cosmetic grade.
8. **Heavy metals.** Method and limits, for cosmetic grade.
9. **Documentation.** Batch certificate, SDS, storage sheet, and INCI statement.
10. **Storage and retest.** Temperature, light protection, and date.

## The Author's Take

**Position.** Buy SNAP-8 as a specified ingredient, not as a “Botox alternative.” The sequence, the terminal groups, the assay, and the grade are the product. The marketing story is not.

**Reasoning.** The SNARE hypothesis is biologically plausible, and the sequence is a reasonable probe for it. The direct evidence for the isolated octapeptide is thin, and the cosmetic studies that exist test multi-ingredient formulations. That does not make the ingredient useless; it makes the claims discipline important. On the supply side, the two grades have different specifications, and a cosmetic certificate may not answer a research buyer’s identity question. A buyer who states the sequence, the assay, the water limit, and the microbial specification gets a product that can be compared across suppliers. A buyer who orders “SNAP-8” by name gets a label.
