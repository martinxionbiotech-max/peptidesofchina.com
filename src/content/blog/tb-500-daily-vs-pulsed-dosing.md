There is no human trial that compares daily and pulsed TB-500 schedules. The comparison comes from preclinical studies that used different species, different injury models, different routes, and different endpoints. None of those studies was designed to rank the two approaches against each other, and none of them establishes a human protocol.

The more practical problem sits one step earlier. "TB-500" is not a single defined molecule in the research market. Some suppliers sell the full 43-residue thymosin β4 sequence; others sell a truncated actin-binding fragment; some do not state which. A schedule comparison is meaningless until the vial's sequence and mass are known.

This page covers the published animal work, explains why the daily-versus-pulsed framing overstates what the data can support, and sets out the identity checks that should come before any protocol design.

## What TB-500 Is, and Why the Label Is Ambiguous

Thymosin β4 is a 43-amino-acid protein with a molecular mass of about 4,963 Da. It is N-terminally acetylated in its native form, binds actin monomers, and is released by platelets at wound sites. It is not a classical hormone and does not act through a single G-protein-coupled receptor.

TB-500 is a commercial name for synthetic material related to thymosin β4. The ambiguity is in the word "related." Some listings describe the full-length 43-residue sequence. Others describe a short fragment containing the actin-binding motif, sometimes identified as residues 17–23 or 17–24. The fragment is roughly one-sixth the mass of the full protein and has different solubility, different aggregation behavior, and a different analytical signature.

The catalog itself shows the split: Peptides of China lists both a TB-500 product and a separate TB-500 fragment 17–24 entry. That is the honest way to handle the ambiguity. A listing that says only "TB-500" and gives no sequence leaves the buyer to guess which molecule will arrive.

The practical consequence is that two researchers can compare notes about "TB-500" and be describing different compounds. Any discussion of schedule, stability, or effect inherits that confusion.

## Why the Schedule Question Exists

Thymosin β4 has a short plasma residence time. In the phase I study by Ruff et al., the pharmacokinetic profile was dose-proportional, and the apparent half-life increased with dose — a pattern consistent with saturable tissue distribution or binding rather than simple first-order elimination.

At the same time, the biological effects in animal models persist well beyond the period when the peptide is detectable in plasma. That mismatch is the origin of the pulsed-schedule argument: if the effect outlasts the plasma concentration, continuous exposure may not be necessary.

The argument is plausible. It is not the same as demonstrated superiority. A plausible pharmacokinetic rationale can support a study design; it cannot substitute for a head-to-head comparison.

There is a second strand to the argument that deserves scrutiny. Thymosin β4 is not a receptor ligand in the classical sense, so the familiar language of "receptor desensitization" does not map onto it cleanly. The protein sequesters actin monomers and interacts with other intracellular partners. Whether repeated exposure produces tolerance is a question about pathway adaptation, not about receptor downregulation, and the published animal work does not settle it.

## What the Animal Studies Actually Tested

The table below summarizes the studies most often cited in TB-500 discussions. Read the "schedule" column as a description of what the investigators did, not as a recommendation.

| Study | Model | Administration | Schedule pattern | Main endpoint reported |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Malinda 1999 | Rat full-thickness dermal wound | Topical or intraperitoneal | Repeated dosing over the healing period | Reepithelialization +42% at day 4, +61% at day 7; wound contraction +11% |
| Ruff 2010 | Healthy human volunteers, phase I | Intravenous | Single dose, then daily for 14 days | Safety, tolerability, dose-proportional pharmacokinetics |
| Treadwell 2012 | Animal wound models plus two phase 2 trials | Various | Various | Faster dermal healing in animal models; accelerated healing in trial participants who healed |
| Evans 2013 | Zebrafish and mouse cardiac injury | Local or systemic, depending on model | Not a TB-500 schedule | Thymosin β4-sulfoxide reduced macrophage infiltration and scarring |
| Biçer 2026 | Rat Achilles tendon transection and repair | Intraperitoneal | Daily for four weeks | Higher maximum load to failure; lower Bonar and Movin scores; no additive effect with BPC-157 |

Three observations follow from the table.

First, the models are not comparable. A dermal wound, a myocardial injury, and a tendon transection heal through different processes on different timelines. A schedule that fits one says little about another.

Second, the endpoints are not comparable. Reepithelialization percentage, load to failure, histology scores, and macrophage counts measure different things. Ranking schedules across those endpoints is not a statistical exercise; it is an analogy.

Third, only one study in the table is a human trial, and it was a safety and pharmacokinetic study, not an efficacy comparison. The Ruff study establishes that single and repeated intravenous administration was tolerated in healthy volunteers and that exposure was dose-proportional. It does not compare daily with pulsed dosing, and it does not establish a therapeutic schedule.

The Biçer study is the most recent and the most specific about its animal protocol: 32 male Sprague-Dawley rats, four groups of eight, standardized Achilles transection and repair, four weeks of daily intraperitoneal administration, and biomechanical testing at four weeks. The TB-500 group showed a statistically significant advantage in maximum load to failure and lower histological degeneration scores. The combination of TB-500 with BPC-157 did not outperform either agent alone.

That last finding is worth pausing on. Combination use is common in the market, and this study found no additive benefit in its model. One study is not definitive, but it is a data point against assuming synergy.

## What the Comparison Does and Does Not Show

The animal literature supports a modest statement: intermittent and daily schedules have both produced measurable effects in preclinical models, and no study has directly compared them with sufficient power to rank one above the other.

It does not support the stronger claims that appear on vendor pages:

- that pulsed dosing is "more biologically aligned" in a way that has been demonstrated;
- that daily dosing is inferior for tissue repair;
- that either schedule produces a given outcome in humans;
- that a schedule validated in one species transfers to another;
- that a schedule validated in one injury model transfers to a different tissue.

The honest summary is that the schedule question is unresolved, and that the published studies were designed to test whether thymosin β4-related material affects healing at all, not to optimize administration frequency.

## The Translation Problem

Animal schedules do not translate directly to humans for four reasons.

**Route.** Intraperitoneal administration in a rodent produces a different exposure profile from subcutaneous or intravenous administration. The peritoneal cavity has a large absorptive surface and drains into the portal circulation, so first-pass hepatic exposure differs.

**Scale.** Dose is usually expressed per kilogram of body weight. Metabolic rate scales with body surface area rather than mass, so a milligram-per-kilogram dose in a rat does not correspond to the same milligram-per-kilogram dose in a human. Allometric scaling gives an estimate, not a guarantee.

**Formulation.** A saline solution of a 43-residue protein behaves differently from a lyophilized powder reconstituted at high concentration. Aggregation, adsorption to tubing, and stability over the dosing period all change the delivered dose.

**Endpoint timing.** A four-week biomechanical endpoint in a rat is not a four-week endpoint in a human. Tendon healing phases differ in duration, and the measurement itself — load to failure in a small tendon — has no direct human equivalent.

None of this means animal data are useless. They are the right basis for generating hypotheses and for designing early-phase studies. They are the wrong basis for a self-directed schedule.

## Identity Testing and Certificate Review

The analytical package for TB-500 depends on which molecule the supplier is selling. For the full-length protein, the key measurements are mass, sequence coverage, N-terminal modification, and aggregation. For a short fragment, mass and sequence are simpler, but the risk of confusing one fragment with another is higher.

| Attribute | Method | What it establishes | What it can miss |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Identity | LC-MS with deconvolution, or MALDI-TOF | Mass near 4,963 Da for full-length; lower mass for a fragment | Sequence variants with similar mass |
| N-terminal status | Mass shift of +42 Da, or peptide mapping | Acetylation of the N-terminus | Incomplete acetylation |
| Sequence | Peptide mapping with LC-MS/MS after digestion | Coverage of the sequence | Poorly ionized or poorly recovered regions |
| Purity | RP-HPLC-UV at 214 nm | Main-peak area percentage | Low-abundance impurities |
| Aggregation | Size-exclusion chromatography | Soluble aggregates and oligomers | Insoluble particles |
| Counterion | Ion chromatography | Acetate or trifluoroacetate content | Counterions outside the method |
| Water | Karl Fischer | Moisture content | Residual solvents |
| Endotoxin | LAL per USP <85> | Endotoxin burden | Non-endotoxin pyrogens |

The N-terminal acetylation check is specific to this molecule and easy to omit. Native thymosin β4 is acetylated; a synthetic batch may be partially or fully unacetylated. The mass difference is 42 Da, which is small enough to be missed if the reported mass is rounded to the nearest hundred. A certificate that reports "4,963 Da" without stating whether the observed mass matches the acetylated theoretical value has not resolved the question.

The identity line is equally important in the other direction. A certificate reporting a mass near 4,963 Da is describing the full-length protein. A mass near 850 Da is describing a fragment. Both can be legitimate products; they are not interchangeable. The buyer should confirm which one the listing intended before comparing prices or planning work.

| Field | What to look for | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sequence declaration | Full-length 43 residues or a named fragment | Ends the ambiguity |
| Calculated mass | 4,963 Da for full-length; fragment mass if applicable | Matches the declared sequence |
| Observed mass | Deconvoluted value with method | Confirms identity |
| N-terminal modification | Acetylated or not, with evidence | Affects activity and stability |
| Purity | HPLC method, 214 nm, chromatogram | Reproducible purity |
| Net peptide content | Amino acid analysis or nitrogen, % by weight | Converts weight to peptide mass |
| Aggregation | SEC result | Handling and formulation risk |
| Endotoxin | Method and limit | Cell-work compatibility |
| Storage and retest | Temperature and date | Defines usable window |

A certificate that states "TB-500, purity 99%" and nothing else has answered none of the identity questions. For a molecule with two common forms and a modification that shifts mass by 42 Da, that is not a minor omission.

## FAQ

### Is TB-500 the same as thymosin β4?

TB-500 is a commercial name for synthetic material related to thymosin β4. Some suppliers sell the full 43-residue protein; others sell a truncated fragment. The sequence and mass on the certificate determine which one a buyer has.

### Is there a human trial comparing daily and pulsed TB-500?

No. The comparison comes from preclinical studies with different models, routes, and endpoints. No head-to-head human trial establishes a superior schedule.

### What did the phase I study show?

Ruff et al. reported that single and repeated intravenous administration of synthetic thymosin β4 was tolerated in healthy volunteers, with dose-proportional pharmacokinetics. It was a safety and pharmacokinetic study, not an efficacy comparison.

### Why does the schedule debate exist?

Because the peptide clears from plasma faster than the biological effects observed in animal models persist. That mismatch suggests continuous exposure may not be required, but it does not prove that intermittent dosing is superior.

### Can animal doses be converted to human doses?

Not directly. Route, metabolic scaling, formulation, and endpoint timing all differ. Allometric scaling gives an estimate for study design, not a human schedule.

### What should a TB-500 certificate of analysis include?

A declared sequence, calculated and observed mass, N-terminal acetylation status, HPLC method and purity, net peptide content, aggregation data, endotoxin result, storage condition, and retest date.

## The Author's Take

**Position.** The daily-versus-pulsed debate is downstream of a more basic problem: much of the market does not define which TB-500 molecule it is selling. Schedule comparisons are noise until sequence and mass are settled.

**Reasoning.** The animal literature is consistent in showing that thymosin β4-related material can affect wound and tendon healing in specific models. It is not consistent in a way that ranks schedules, because no study was built to do that. The Ruff phase I work establishes tolerability and dose-proportional exposure, not superiority of any frequency. Meanwhile, the commercial name covers at least two molecules with a five-fold mass difference, and the acetylation status adds another 42 Da of ambiguity. A buyer who compares two suppliers on price without comparing their sequences is not comparing the same product. On the analytical side, deconvoluted mass, N-terminal acetylation, peptide mapping, and aggregation are the four fields that actually characterize this material.
