MK-677 has no established 24-hour human plasma half-life. The 24-hour figure that circulates on vendor pages describes the duration of growth hormone and IGF-1 effects after once-daily oral dosing, not the terminal elimination of the parent compound. The two measurements answer different questions, and conflating them produces washout and steady-state calculations that the underlying data cannot support.

A second correction belongs at the start. MK-677, also called ibutamoren, is not a peptide. It is a small-molecule ghrelin-receptor agonist with the formula C27H36N4O5S and a free-base mass of 528.7 Da. Buyers who order it from a peptide catalog should expect a small-molecule certificate, not a peptide certificate.

## What Half-Life Means, and Why the 24-Hour Claim Persists

Terminal half-life is derived from the slope of the log-concentration versus time curve during the elimination phase. It requires a validated bioanalytical method, serial plasma samples, and a sampling window long enough to characterize the terminal phase — generally at least three half-lives. Non-compartmental analysis then reports t½, clearance, volume of distribution, and AUC.

The 24-hour claim likely began as a description of dosing interval and biomarker duration. Once-daily oral administration produces a GH pulse and a sustained IGF-1 change over 24 hours. A reader can see "24 hours" in a trial description, assume it refers to half-life, and repeat it. The error propagates because it is plausible: a drug taken once daily is often assumed to have a daily half-life.

Plausibility is not measurement.

## The Human Studies: What They Actually Measured

The most detailed early human paper is Murphy et al., published in the *Journal of Clinical Endocrinology and Metabolism* in 1998. It was a double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled, two-period crossover study in eight healthy volunteers. Subjects were calorie-restricted for two 14-day periods and received either MK-677 or placebo during the last seven days of each period, with a 14- to 21-day washout between periods.

The endpoints were nitrogen balance, GH response, IGF-1, IGFBP-3, cortisol, and prolactin. During the treatment week, mean daily nitrogen balance was 0.31 ± 0.21 g/day in the MK-677 group versus −1.48 ± 0.21 g/day with placebo. Peak GH was 55.9 ± 31.7 µg/L after the first dose and 22.6 ± 9.3 µg/L after a week, against roughly 9 and 7 µg/L with placebo. Mean IGF-1 rose to 264 ± 31 ng/mL versus 188 ± 19 ng/mL.

Those are pharmacodynamic measurements. The paper reports a GH peak and a week-long IGF-1 change; it does not report a terminal plasma half-life from serial parent-drug sampling. That is the central distinction the old version of this page missed.

A second paper, Murphy et al. in the *Journal of Bone and Mineral Research* in 1999, pooled three randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled studies in 187 adults aged 65 or older, lasting 2 to 9 weeks. IGF-1 increased by 55–94%, urinary N-telopeptide cross-links rose 10–17% in the dose-ranging phase, and osteocalcin rose 8% in the four-week study and 29.4% in the nine-week study. Again, the measurements are biomarkers and turnover markers, not a concentration-time curve for the parent molecule.

Svensson et al., also in the *Journal of Bone and Mineral Research* in 1998, examined bone-formation and resorption markers in obese young males. The finding fits the same pattern: oral administration changes GH-dependent markers, and the paper does not establish a human terminal half-life.

| Study | Population | Design | Primary measurements | Half-life reported? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Murphy 1998 | 8 healthy volunteers | Randomized, placebo-controlled, two-period crossover | Nitrogen balance, GH, IGF-1, IGFBP-3 | No |
| Murphy 1999 | 187 adults aged 65+ | Pooled randomized, placebo-controlled trials, 2–9 weeks | IGF-1, osteocalcin, BSAP, urinary NTX | No |
| Svensson 1998 | Obese young males | Randomized controlled trial | Bone formation and resorption markers | No |
| Smith 2005 | Review | Narrative review of GH secretagogues | Mechanism and clinical development | No primary data |

The table is short because the human literature is short. MK-677 was developed in an era when the commercial program ended before a full pharmacokinetic package was published in the open literature. That absence is the reason vendor pages fill the gap with borrowed numbers.

## The Animal Data and the Translation Problem

Preclinical pharmacokinetic work exists, and some of it reports short terminal half-lives in animals. Those values are legitimate for the species studied. They become misleading when a vendor removes the species and presents the number as human.

Species differences in clearance are not a rounding error. Hepatic metabolism, plasma protein binding, and renal clearance differ enough that a half-life from dogs or rats cannot be transferred to humans without an allometric or physiologically based model, and even then the estimate carries wide uncertainty.

The correct statement is that human terminal half-life is not established in the cited literature. The incorrect statement is that it is 24 hours, or 4 to 6 hours, or any other precise figure presented without a human concentration-time study behind it.

## Why a Biomarker Timeline Is Not a Half-Life

Three mechanisms can produce a 24-hour effect from a compound with a much shorter plasma residence.

The first is indirect action. MK-677 stimulates GH release, and GH then raises IGF-1 production in the liver. IGF-1 has its own half-life and its own feedback regulation. The biomarker persists because the downstream hormone persists, not because the drug does.

The second is receptor kinetics. A ghrelin-receptor agonist can trigger a signaling cascade that outlasts occupancy. The effect duration then reflects the cascade, not the plasma concentration.

The third is sampling. A trial that measures IGF-1 once daily cannot distinguish a drug with a 2-hour half-life from one with a 20-hour half-life if both produce the same integrated daily GH output.

A washout calculation built on the wrong premise will be wrong in a predictable direction: it will overestimate the time needed for the parent drug to clear, and it may understate accumulation if the true half-life is longer than assumed. Neither error is acceptable in a study design.

A defensible human pharmacokinetic study would need four elements.

**A validated LC-MS/MS method.** Plasma concentrations of ibutamoren are low, and the assay needs a lower limit of quantification in the sub-nanogram-per-milliliter range, an internal standard, and selectivity against metabolites.

**Serial sampling across the terminal phase.** Dense early time points for absorption, then sampling out to at least three estimated half-lives. A 24-hour profile is not enough if the half-life is longer than eight hours.

**Single-dose and multiple-dose arms.** Single-dose data give clearance and volume of distribution; multiple-dose data give accumulation ratio and time to steady state. The two cannot be inferred from each other without assumptions.

**Non-compartmental or population analysis.** The report should state the terminal slope, the adjusted R², the sampling window used, and the fraction of AUC extrapolated. An extrapolated fraction above roughly 20% weakens the half-life estimate.

Until such a study is published, the honest answer to "what is the half-life of MK-677?" is that the human value is not established. The pharmacokinetic question remains open.

## Identity Testing and Certificate Review

Ibutamoren is a small molecule, so the analytical package looks different from a peptide COA. Identity is established by mass spectrometry and chromatographic retention against a reference standard, not by amino acid analysis. Purity is measured by HPLC-UV, usually near 220 nm, where the aromatic and amide chromophores absorb.

| Attribute | Method | What it establishes | Common failure |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Identity | ESI-LC-MS, [M+H]+ near m/z 529.3 | Molecular mass consistent with ibutamoren | A salt or degradant with a different mass |
| Purity | RP-HPLC-UV, 220 nm | Chromatographic purity | Co-eluting process impurities |
| Assay | HPLC against a reference standard | Content as a percentage of label | Using area percentage as assay |
| Salt form | Ion chromatography, titration | Mesylate stoichiometry | A free base sold as a salt, or the reverse |
| Water | Karl Fischer | Moisture content | Hygroscopic powder gaining weight |
| Residual solvents | GC headspace per ICH Q3C | Solvent limits | Methanol or acetonitrile above limit |
| Sulfonate esters | LC-MS or GC-MS per ICH M7 | Genotoxic impurity control | A mesylate salt without ester testing |
| Polymorph | X-ray diffraction | Solid form | A form change affecting dissolution |

The salt-form line deserves emphasis. Ibutamoren is commonly supplied as the mesylate. The free base is 528.7 Da; the mesylate salt is roughly 624.8 Da. A certificate that reports the free-base mass while the vial contains the salt is not necessarily wrong — many certificates report the free-base equivalent — but the field must say so. A buyer calculating molarity from the salt mass while the certificate reports free-base content will be off by about 18%.

The sulfonate-ester line is specific to mesylate salts. Methanesulfonic acid can form methyl methanesulfonate and related esters under certain conditions, and those are classified as genotoxic impurities. ICH M7 provides the control framework. A supplier that sells a mesylate salt without a sulfonate-ester statement has left a known class of impurity unaddressed.

A small-molecule certificate should look like a pharmaceutical release record, not a peptide certificate with the fields renamed.

| Field | Example specification | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Batch and manufacture date | Unique lot, traceable | Connects vial to data |
| Appearance | White to off-white powder | Visual signal of process or storage |
| Identity by MS | m/z 529.3 ± 0.5 | Confirms molecular mass |
| Purity by HPLC | ≥ 98.0% area | Separates main peak from impurities |
| Assay | 98.0–102.0% on dried basis | Confirms actual content |
| Water content | ≤ 0.5% w/w | Prevents weight-based dosing errors |
| Residual solvents | Within ICH Q3C limits | Patient and operator safety |
| Sulfonate esters | Below ICH M7 threshold | Genotoxic impurity control |
| Heavy metals | ≤ 20 ppm or ICH Q3D class limits | Catalytic residue control |
| Retest date | With storage condition | Defines usable window |

The difference between purity and assay is the most common source of confusion. Purity is the area percentage of the main peak in a chromatogram; it says nothing about how much material is present relative to the label. Assay compares the sample to a reference standard and reports content. A product can be 99% pure by area and 85% of label content if the powder contains water or salts. The certificate needs both numbers.

## Salt Form, Polymorphs, and Impurities

Three formulation variables change what a buyer receives even when the label says "MK-677."

**Salt stoichiometry.** A mesylate can be 1:1 or another ratio. The certificate should state the ratio and the free-base equivalent. A product sold as "ibutamoren mesylate" with no ratio is underspecified.

**Polymorph.** Small molecules can crystallize in more than one solid form, and the forms can differ in solubility and stability. X-ray powder diffraction is the usual identification method. A supplier that changes the crystallization solvent without reporting a form change has changed the product.

**Chiral purity.** Ibutamoren contains stereocenters, and the enantiomer or diastereomer can carry different activity. Chiral HPLC or SFC with a specified column and mobile phase is the control. A certificate that reports only achiral purity has not addressed this.

None of these points is exotic. They are standard for a small-molecule API. Their absence from a peptide-style certificate is a signal that the supplier is using a template rather than a product-specific method.

## Supplier Verification Questions

1. **Is the product the free base or the mesylate salt, and what is the stoichiometry?**
2. **What is the observed mass, and does the certificate report free-base equivalent?**
3. **Which HPLC method was used, and what is the assay against a reference standard?**
4. **Were sulfonate esters tested, and against which limit?**
5. **Which residual solvents were tested, and by which method?**
6. **Is the polymorph specified, and was XRD performed?**

## FAQ

### Is MK-677 a peptide?

No. MK-677, or ibutamoren, is a non-peptide small-molecule ghrelin-receptor agonist with the formula C27H36N4O5S and a free-base mass of 528.7 Da.

### Is the human half-life of MK-677 24 hours?

No published human study cited here establishes a 24-hour terminal plasma half-life. The 24-hour figure describes the dosing interval and the duration of GH and IGF-1 effects.

### Do the human trials report a half-life?

The trials cited here measured nitrogen balance, GH, IGF-1, and bone-turnover markers. They did not report a terminal half-life from parent-drug concentration data.

### Can an animal half-life be used for human planning?

No. Species differences in clearance and protein binding are large enough that an animal value cannot be relabeled as human without a bridging study.

### What would a valid human PK study require?

A validated LC-MS/MS assay, serial sampling across at least three half-lives, single- and multiple-dose arms, and a non-compartmental or population analysis with the extrapolated AUC fraction reported.

### What should an ibutamoren COA include?

Identity by MS, HPLC purity and assay, salt form and stoichiometry, water content, residual solvents, sulfonate esters for the mesylate, heavy metals, polymorph identification, storage condition, and retest date.

## The Author's Take

**Position.** The 24-hour half-life claim for MK-677 is a category error that has been repeated often enough to look like a fact. The human literature shows a daily biomarker effect; it does not show a daily elimination half-life. Buyers should treat any precise human half-life quoted by a vendor as unsupported until a concentration-time study is cited.

**Reasoning.** The three main human papers are pharmacodynamic studies with biomarker endpoints. They are worth reading, and their results are consistent: oral ibutamoren raises GH and IGF-1 and shifts bone-turnover markers. None of them reports the terminal slope of a plasma concentration curve. The animal values that do exist cannot be transferred across species without a model. On the supply side, ibutamoren is a small molecule with a mesylate salt, stereocenters, and a polymorph risk, so a peptide-template COA is the wrong document. The four fields that matter most — assay versus purity, salt stoichiometry, sulfonate esters, and polymorph — are usually the four missing from a generic certificate.
