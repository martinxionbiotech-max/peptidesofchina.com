Most buyers who ask about China peptide sourcing are asking three questions at once: is this company real, will the batch match the paperwork, and will the shipment arrive without a customs surprise. Price comes fourth. That order matters. A quote that is 30% below the next one is not a saving if the batch cannot be traced, the COA belongs to a different lot, or the parcel is stopped because the importer of record was never properly established.

This guide is for laboratory buyers. It covers supplier structure, factory versus trader, batch files, MOQ and pricing, payment risk, shipping, and customs. It does not cover human use, dosing, injection, or therapeutic applications. Products referenced here are research compounds for laboratory work; nothing here is medical advice.

## Direct answer

China peptide sourcing works when the buyer treats it as supplier qualification rather than product shopping. The country of origin tells you little. The legal entity, the production site, the batch record, and the import route tell you almost everything. The [sourcing guide](/blog/how-to-source-peptides-from-china-a-2026-b2b-procurement-guide/) covers the sequence from a procurement angle.

1. **Match the supplier tier to the order.** A 10-vial research order and a 10-kilogram campaign need different suppliers. A ton-scale CDMO is slow and expensive at small scale; a vial-filling shop cannot run a kilogram campaign with change control.
2. **Verify the legal entity and the site.** The company that invoices you, the company that owns the production licence, and the company that signs the batch record should be the same, or the relationship between them should be documented.
3. **Tie every document to the lot.** A COA without a lot number is a brochure. A chromatogram without a method is a picture. A batch record without a release decision is a draft.
4. **Own the import.** The supplier can arrange freight. It cannot be your importer of record, your customs broker, or your compliance officer unless it is licensed and contracted to do that work in your jurisdiction.

The common failure is a real vial with a paperwork chain that cannot survive a question: no site, no method revision, no named importer.

## Who this is for

This page is written for people who buy research materials for laboratories and research-adjacent businesses:

- **Academic core facilities and university labs** that need a documented batch for a study and cannot accept a generic COA.
- **Biotech procurement and operations teams** comparing Chinese API or research-grade suppliers for preclinical work.
- **Research peptide resellers and distributors** that need to qualify a manufacturer before putting a label on a vial.
- **Formulation and cosmetic research teams** buying research-grade peptides for in-vitro work, where identity and purity matter but human-use claims are out of scope.
- **Founders building a peptide brand** who need to understand what a supplier can and cannot legally support.

It is not for patients, clinicians, or consumers, and it does not cover dosing, injection, or therapeutic use. Human-use imagery or outcome claims from a supplier are a compliance signal, not a quality signal. The [research-use disclaimer](/research-use-disclaimer/) sets out the boundary.

## Key facts

- **China has several peptide supply tiers, not one.** Ton-scale API and CDMO capacity sits alongside gram-scale synthesis shops and traders. Sinopep reports 20–30 metric tons of annual capacity and FDA-inspected facilities [8]. WuXi AppTec's SPPS reactor volume was 41,000 liters at end-2024, with a plan for 100,000 liters by end-2025 [6].
- **Regulators are watching the API channel.** FDA evaluated 48 GLP-1 API sites and found 21% noncompliant under the FD&C Act, including firms that refused records requests or deregistered quickly [2].
- **US de minimis rules changed.** Duty-free treatment under 19 U.S.C. § 1321(a)(2)(C) is suspended for shipments of $800 or less, across all modes. Informal entry is generally available up to $2,500; formal entry is required above that [1].
- **HS classification is structural.** The WCO applies Chapter 29 Note 3 (last heading in numerical order) and Subheading Note 1; polypeptide hormones fall under 29.37, while non-hormonal polypeptides follow chemical structure [4].
- **"Research use only" is not a legal shield.** FDA's import alert on unapproved new drugs turns on intended use, not the label a seller prints [3].
- **Lunar New Year 2027 falls on 6 February.** Hong Kong's gazetted holidays put Lunar New Year's Day on 6 February, with the third and fourth days on 8 and 9 February [7]. Mainland factory calendars cluster around that week.
- **The public catalog minimum is $600 in goods per supplier order**, with one first-order trial below that; a 5% goods discount applies at $3,500 per supplier. Shipping is separate. See the [FAQ](/faq/) and the [MOQ guide](/blog/bulk-peptide-order-minimums-2026-research-lab-guide/).
- **The cheapest quote is rarely the lowest landed cost.** Freight, duty, broker fees, retesting, storage, and the cost of a failed batch sit outside the unit price.

## The supplier landscape: four layers, not one "China"

The word "manufacturer" covers a 20-tonne GMP API plant and a two-fume-hood synthesis lab. Buyers get into trouble when they compare quotes from different layers as if they were one market.

**Layer 1: large API and CDMO producers.** These firms run automated solid-phase synthesis at ton scale, own purification and lyophilization capacity, and maintain quality systems for regulated customers. WuXi AppTec, Asymchem, and Sinopep are the usual names [6]. Sinopep describes 20–30 metric tons of annual capacity and FDA-inspected facilities [8]. They suit a commercial API program, a tech transfer, or a multi-kilogram campaign, not a 20-vial research order: minimums are high and small buyers are not priority accounts.

**Layer 2: mid-sized peptide producers.** These companies run kilogram-scale synthesis, offer catalog compounds and custom sequences, and may hold a drug production licence for selected products. Documentation is better than at the small-shop layer, but licence scope needs checking product by product. Many serious research-supply relationships sit here.

**Layer 3: research synthesis shops.** Gram to low-kilogram capacity, fast turnaround, broad catalogs, and a business model built on small orders. Quality varies widely. Some run proper HPLC and MS release testing; others send a purity number and a chromatogram with no method. This layer supplies much of the research vial market.

**Layer 4: traders and consolidators.** They do not synthesize. They buy from layers 1–3, consolidate orders, handle export paperwork, and sell under their own brand. A good trader can be the best partner for a lab that needs ten compounds from five sites. A bad trader adds markup and obscures the chain.

Geography matters less than tier. Production clusters in Zhejiang, Jiangsu, Shanghai, and Guangdong, with capacity in Sichuan and Hubei. A Shanghai sales office is not a Shanghai factory; ask for the production address and check what is there.

The rule: match the tier to the order, then verify within the tier. A ton-scale CDMO that will not take your order is not a better supplier for your study. The [manufacturing partners](/manufacturing-partners/) page explains this site's supplier relationships.

## Factory vs trader: what changes for the buyer

"Factory-direct" is a supply-chain claim, not a quality grade. A factory can produce a bad batch; a trader can source a good one. What changes is who owns the synthesis record, who can answer a technical question, and who is still there when a batch fails.

A real peptide factory has reactors, resin and amino acid inventory, cleavage and purification equipment, lyophilizers, analytical instruments, and a quality unit that releases batches. It also has environmental permits and a batch numbering system that ties raw material lots to finished vials. You need someone who can answer questions about that without transferring you to sales.

A trader has supplier relationships, an export desk, and consolidation. That can be legitimate. The problem starts when it presents itself as a factory, refuses to name the site, or supplies another company's COA.

Five questions separate the models:

1. **Who polymerizes the chain?** Ask for the site name and city. A factory gives a production address; a trader gives a brand name.
2. **What is the reactor size and batch number?** A real batch has a number, synthesis date, and scale. "Standard batch" is not an answer.
3. **Who signs the batch record?** Ask for the role: production manager, QC lead, QA release. A trader will not have one.
4. **Which laboratory issued the COA?** In-house QC, a contract lab, or a third party. Each has different evidentiary weight.
5. **Can we audit the site?** A factory can say yes, no, or later. A trader has to ask someone else.

The honest-trader model is simple: "We source from two sites in Zhejiang. Here are the licences, batch records, and audit reports. Our name is on the invoice; the manufacturer's name is on the COA." That works. The opaque model does not. The [direct sourcing versus reseller guide](/blog/direct-sourcing-vs-peptide-resellers/) compares the trade-offs.

Do not pay a factory premium without factory evidence. Do not reject a trader without checking the chain. The question is not "factory or trader?" It is "who can prove the batch?"

## A verification workflow that survives first contact

Verification is a sequence, not a questionnaire. Run it before the first payment, then repeat the document checks on every batch.

**Step 1: Define the specification before you ask for a price.** Write the sequence, counterion, purity target, appearance, fill size, test methods, and acceptance criteria. If you cannot state the specification, the supplier will define it for you. A quote for "[BPC-157](/catalog/bpc-157/) 99%" is a slogan, not a spec.

**Step 2: Check the legal entity.** Ask for the business licence, unified social credit code, registered address, legal representative, and business scope. The invoicing company, bank beneficiary, and licence holder should match. If the licence is in one name and the bank account in another, stop. China's enterprise credit system is searchable, but foreign access can be limited; a Chinese agent or forwarder can pull the record.

**Step 3: Check the production licence and the site.** For pharmaceutical APIs, the site needs a drug production licence and a current inspection history with the provincial drug administration [10]. For research-grade chemicals, the licence may be a chemical production or trading scope, not a drug licence. A quality certificate that does not name the site, product, and scope is decoration. The [GMP compliance guide](/blog/how-to-assess-peptide-supplier-gmp-compliance-in-2026/) explains the scope.

**Step 4: Have a technical call.** Ask how the sequence is assembled, which coupling reagents are used, how difficult sequences are handled, what purification method is used, how lyophilization is controlled, and which instruments run identity and purity tests. A salesperson reads a script; a production or QC person answers in specifics.

**Step 5: Buy a sample and split it.** Keep one vial sealed as a retain, test one in-house, and send one to an accredited third-party lab. Test identity and purity at minimum; add endotoxin, sterility, or residual solvents if needed. Compare the third-party result with the supplier's COA. A small gap is normal; a different peak profile is not. The [COA verification guide](/blog/coa-verified-peptides-wholesale-2026-sourcing-verification-guide/) covers the comparison.

**Step 6: Put the quality terms in the contract.** Write down the specification, test methods, acceptance criteria, batch documentation, change-control notice period, audit rights, and remedy for a failed batch. If the supplier will not sign a quality annex, the relationship is a spot purchase, not a supply agreement. The [contract guide](/blog/peptide-supplier-contract-negotiation-tips-for-procurement/) covers the clauses.

**Step 7: Audit before scale, not after.** A video walkthrough is better than nothing: ask to see the synthesis hall, purification skids, lyophilizer, QC lab, and warehouse. An on-site audit is better. If the order justifies travel, go; if not, keep the order small enough that a failure is survivable.

Record every answer in one supplier file. Inconsistent answers, a change of contact, or a moving site address are signals. The goal is a chain of evidence that still makes sense six months later.

## The document package: COA, batch record, MSDS and customs papers

A complete batch package lets a stranger reconstruct what happened. Most suppliers send a COA and call it documentation. A COA is one page of a larger file.

| Document | What it supports | What it does not prove |
|---|---|---|
| Certificate of Analysis | The lot's tested values against a specification | That the test was independent, or that the method was suitable |
| Raw HPLC chromatogram and method | Purity profile and retention behavior | Identity, or that the column and gradient match the claimed method |
| Mass spectrometry report | Molecular identity against theoretical mass | Purity, or the absence of isomers |
| Batch manufacturing record | Sequence, scale, reagents, yield, deviations | That the record was reviewed or released |
| MSDS/SDS | Hazards, handling, storage, transport classification | Quality, purity, or legal import status |
| Certificate of origin | Country of manufacture for customs and trade measures | That the goods match the invoice |
| Commercial invoice and packing list | Value, quantity, weight, consignee | The chemical identity of the contents |
| Import permit or end-use statement | The buyer's declared purpose and eligibility | That the declaration is true |

**COA fields that matter.** Look for the lot number, manufacturing date, test date, specification, method, result, reviewer, and the laboratory's name and address. For HPLC, look for column type, mobile phase, gradient, flow rate, wavelength, and injection volume. For MS, look for observed mass, theoretical mass, ionization mode, and the spectrum. A COA that says "99.5% by HPLC" with no method is a number, not a measurement. The [COA reading guide](/blog/how-to-read-a-peptide-coa-a-researchers-guide-to-hplc-and-ms-reports/) walks through the fields.

**Batch linkage.** The lot number on the COA must match the vial label and packing list. If the supplier sends a COA for "[TB-500](/catalog/tb-500/) 10mg" without a lot number, ask for the batch-specific version. A lot number that changes between quote, invoice, and COA is an unverified shipment until explained in writing.

**Manufacturer COA vs third-party COA.** A manufacturer's COA is a release document: what the factory tested and released. A third-party COA is what an independent lab found in the sample it received. Neither is a guarantee. The third-party report is stronger evidence when the sample chain is intact and the lab is accredited for the method.

**MSDS and customs papers.** The safety data sheet tells the carrier and receiving lab how to handle the material, what PPE to use, and whether the shipment needs a dangerous-goods declaration. The invoice, packing list, certificate of origin, and any import permit must describe the same material; mismatches are the most common reason a parcel sits in a bonded warehouse. The [documentation guide](/blog/peptide-documentation-package/), [transparency checklist](/blog/peptide-supply-chain-transparency-buyer-checklist/), and [customs guide](/blog/research-peptide-customs-clearance-a-professional-b2b-sourcing-guide-2026/) go deeper.

## MOQ and pricing logic: what the quote is really built from

A peptide quote is a bundle of costs, not a market price. The drivers are sequence length, synthesis scale, sequence difficulty, purification loss, lyophilization, vial filling, release testing, documentation, and freight. A 10-amino-acid peptide at 100 mg and a 40-amino-acid modified peptide at 10 g do not share a cost curve.

**Normalize the unit.** Suppliers quote per vial, per kit, per gram, or per batch. Convert to price per gram of net peptide, then add the tests you will run and the risk of a failed batch. A $90 vial with 5 mg is $18/mg; a $600 vial with 50 mg is $12/mg. The larger vial is cheaper per milligram but may be the wrong fill for a study that needs fresh material.

**Understand step pricing.** The jump from 1 g to 10 g is usually less than ten times the price, because synthesis and QC have fixed costs. The jump from 100 g to 1 kg can be more than ten times, because purification yield and reactor scheduling become constraints. Ask for prices at three scales and what changes at each step.

**Match the MOQ to the supplier tier.** The public Peptides of China catalog uses a $600 goods minimum per supplier order, with one first-order trial below that, and a 5% goods discount at $3,500 per supplier. Shipping is excluded from both thresholds. Those rules are in the [FAQ](/faq/) and the [bulk order guide](/blog/bulk-peptide-order-minimums-2026-research-lab-guide/). A CDMO may quote in kilograms; a synthesis shop may charge a setup fee instead.

**Why a low quote can be real.** A smaller fill, a lower purity specification, a different counterion, a cheaper purification route, or an in-stock batch can all lower the price without fraud. Ask which variable changed. If the supplier cannot explain the gap, assume the specification changed. The [bulk sourcing economics](/blog/the-economic-and-operational-logic-of-bulk-peptide-sourcing/) piece covers scale effects.

**Build a landed-cost model.** Landed cost = goods + freight + insurance + duty + broker fees + port charges + retesting + internal handling + the expected cost of a failed batch. For a first order, add a retain sample and a third-party test. The [cost-reduction guide](/blog/peptide-procurement-cost-reduction-strategies-2026-guide/) has a worked framework; a 10% unit-price saving can disappear in one customs delay.

**Do not negotiate purity.** Negotiate fill size, packaging, payment terms, and lead time. A supplier that will drop the purity specification to hit a price is telling you what the specification was worth.

## Payment and risk: deposits, escrow, L/C and the limits of each

Payment is where the supplier's legal identity becomes operational. A company can have a polished website and a convincing COA; the bank account is the point where you find out whether the entity exists as stated.

**T/T deposit and balance.** The common structure is a 30–50% deposit, balance before shipment or against shipping documents. The deposit should reflect the supplier's sunk cost, not its cash-flow need. A custom sequence justifies a larger deposit because synthesis starts on receipt; a catalog item does not.

**Escrow and platform payment.** Escrow can protect a first order, but it usually protects the buyer only until delivery, not until the batch is tested. Read the release conditions. A platform that releases funds on delivery receipt does not help if the vial arrives with a mismatched COA. The [platform versus agent guide](/blog/peptide-sourcing-agent-vs-platform-a-2026-procurement-guide-for-research-labs/) compares the models.

**Documentary credit.** A letter of credit shifts risk to banks, but only if the documents are exact. A discrepancy in a description, quantity, or document title can delay payment. For a first order under $50,000, bank fees and document risk often outweigh the protection; for a large repeat order, a properly structured credit can help.

**Export credit insurance.** Export credit insurance protects the Chinese seller against buyer nonpayment. It does not make your deposit refundable.

**Bank details.** The beneficiary name must match the contracting entity. The account should be in the same jurisdiction, and the account name should not be personal. If the bank details change mid-order, verify by a callback to a known number, not by email. Email compromise is a common fraud pattern in cross-border trade.

**Payment methods to refuse.** Personal accounts, cryptocurrency, third-party accounts in another company's name, and payment to a different country from the supplier's registration. Each breaks the audit trail and makes a dispute harder to pursue.

**Contract terms.** Put the governing law, arbitration venue, inspection rights, remedy for a failed batch, and force majeure definition in the contract. A supplier that will not accept any remedy language is selling on take-it-or-leave-it terms. That may be acceptable for a $600 trial; it is not acceptable for a scale-up.

**Sanctions and AML.** Check the entity and its beneficial owner against applicable sanctions lists. A supplier that cannot name its beneficial owner is a counterparty risk.

## Shipping, Incoterms and who owns the risk

Incoterms are eleven three-letter rules that allocate costs, tasks, and risk between buyer and seller [5]. They do not allocate title or decide which law applies. They are a delivery vocabulary, not a contract. The [logistics guide](/blog/peptide-sourcing-logistics-a-professional-guide-to-direct-to-manufacturer-procurement/) covers the sequence.

- **EXW.** Buyer collects at the supplier's premises and handles export, freight, and import. A poor choice for a first-time importer, who may not be able to clear export in China.
- **FOB.** Seller delivers to the port and clears export; risk transfers on board. Buyer arranges ocean freight and insurance. Common for sea shipments.
- **CIF.** Seller pays freight and insurance to the destination port, but risk transfers on board. Buyer still owns import and the inland leg. CIF insurance is usually minimum-cover.
- **DAP.** Seller delivers to the named place, ready for import; buyer clears import and pays duty. Often the cleanest term for air freight to a lab.
- **DDP.** Seller handles everything, including duty. Convenient, but a compliance problem when the seller is not the lawful importer of record. Use it only when the seller is a licensed importer and the paperwork names the real importer.

The importer of record is the buyer unless a licensed importer is contracted. The importer is legally responsible for the declaration, duty, and accuracy of documents. A supplier can recommend a broker; it cannot transfer that responsibility by writing "DDP" on an invoice.

Peptides are not one HS code. The WCO applies Chapter 29 Note 3 and places polypeptide hormones under 29.37, while non-hormonal polypeptides follow chemical structure [4]. A wrong code can trigger reclassification, delay, or penalty. For a repeated import, request a binding ruling; the EU maintains TARIC for tariff measures [9].

In the US, duty-free de minimis treatment for shipments of $800 or less is suspended across all modes, and entry depends on value and merchandise type [1]. A parcel that once arrived with no entry now needs an entry, a classification, and often a bond.

Freight is a trade-off: express is fast and expensive, air is cheaper at volume, sea is cheapest but adds weeks. Packing and temperature control matter more than speed, but a week in a hot warehouse can still compromise a batch. The [shipping policy](/shipping-and-reshipment-policy/) covers delivery exceptions.

No supplier can guarantee customs. A promise of "guaranteed clearance" is inexperience or a plan to misdeclare.

## Holiday delays: planning around Lunar New Year and October

Chinese factory calendars are not continuous. Lunar New Year is the largest disruption and it is predictable. In 2027, Lunar New Year's Day falls on 6 February, with Hong Kong gazetting holidays on 6, 8, and 9 February [7]. Mainland arrangements are published separately and can add days. Factories in Zhejiang, Jiangsu, and Guangdong slow down before the holiday and ramp up after it.

The effect starts earlier than the holiday. Freight bookings tighten two to three weeks before the break, suppliers push to clear orders, and QC queues lengthen. The shipment that leaves the week before Lunar New Year is the one most likely to sit. The first two weeks after the holiday are a restart period.

**A planning calendar that works:**

- **Eight to ten weeks before Lunar New Year:** confirm the production slot, not just the price. Ask for a written ship-by date.
- **Six weeks before:** approve the batch record and the labels; book freight.
- **Four weeks before:** complete QC and documentation; avoid scheduling a study start against a pre-holiday ETA.
- **Two weeks before:** confirm the booking, the pick-up, and the export documents. Have a fallback supplier or a safety stock buffer.
- **After the holiday:** expect a two-week ramp. Do not treat the first quote as a commitment.

**National Day Golden Week (1–7 October)** is the second major window. It is shorter than Lunar New Year but affects ports, trucking, and government offices. May Day and Dragon Boat Festival cause smaller disruptions. The [lead-time guide](/blog/how-to-evaluate-peptide-supplier-lead-times-in-2026/) and the [safety-stock note](/blog/role-of-safety-stock-in-peptide-procurement/) explain how to turn these windows into inventory policy.

The mistake is to treat a holiday as a shipping delay. It is a production and documentation delay that shows up later as one. Plan the batch, not the parcel.

## Supplier audit checklist

Use this as an evidence checklist, not a scorecard. Each item needs a document or a named person behind it; a verbal "yes" is not evidence.

**Legal entity**

- [ ] Business licence with the company name, unified social credit code, registered address, legal representative, and business scope.
- [ ] The invoicing entity, the licence holder, and the bank beneficiary are the same, or the relationship is documented.
- [ ] Beneficial owner identified for AML and sanctions screening.
- [ ] Export licence or registration covers the products being shipped.

**Facility and capability**

- [ ] Production address is a real industrial site, not a residential or virtual office.
- [ ] Reactor count and volume match the claimed scale.
- [ ] Purification equipment (preparative HPLC or equivalent) is on site.
- [ ] Lyophilizer capacity matches the fill format.
- [ ] QC lab has HPLC, MS, and the instruments needed for release tests.

**Quality system**

- [ ] Written specification for the product, including counterion and appearance.
- [ ] Batch record with synthesis, cleavage, purification, and fill steps.
- [ ] HPLC method with column, mobile phase, gradient, flow, and wavelength.
- [ ] MS identity check against theoretical mass.
- [ ] Deviation and out-of-specification procedure.
- [ ] Change-control notification clause.
- [ ] Retain sample policy and stability data on request.

**Documentation**

- [ ] Lot-specific COA matches the vial label and packing list.
- [ ] Raw chromatogram and spectrum available, not just the summary.
- [ ] MSDS/SDS in the destination language if required.
- [ ] Certificate of origin and commercial invoice consistent with the goods.
- [ ] Import permit or end-use statement where required.

**Commercial**

- [ ] MOQ, step pricing, and lead time in writing.
- [ ] Payment terms and beneficiary details verified.
- [ ] Remedy for a failed batch or a customs rejection.
- [ ] Audit rights and notice period.

A supplier that cannot produce five of these in writing is not qualified. One that produces all of them and refuses an audit is a calculated risk. The checklist tells you which conversation comes next.

## Red flags that should stop an order

Some warnings are negotiable. These are not.

1. **The supplier will not name the legal entity or the production site.** A brand name and a WhatsApp number are not a counterparty.
2. **The bank account is in a personal name or a different company's name.** This is the most common fraud signal in peptide trade.
3. **The COA has no lot number, no method, or no laboratory name.** A purity percentage without a method is marketing.
4. **The same COA appears across multiple batches.** COAs are lot-specific. Reuse means either the batch did not change or the document is decorative.
5. **The MS data does not match the theoretical mass.** Identity is not a purity question. A clean HPLC peak can still be the wrong molecule.
6. **The supplier promises "guaranteed customs clearance."** No one can guarantee a customs outcome. The promise usually means a misdeclaration.
7. **The supplier offers "stealth" or "discreet" shipping that misdescribes the goods.** This shifts legal risk to the buyer and destroys the traceability chain.
8. **The website markets human use, dosing, or therapeutic outcomes.** That is a different regulatory category, and it tells you who the intended customer is.
9. **The price is far below the market with no process explanation.** Ask which variable changed: fill, purity, counterion, or batch status. No answer means no deal.
10. **The supplier cannot answer a technical question without a script.** A real manufacturer has a production or QC person who can discuss coupling and purification.
11. **The lead time is vague and the ship-by date is verbal.** "About three weeks" is not a commitment. A date, a batch number, and a booking reference are.
12. **The supplier asks for crypto, a third-party account, or payment outside the contract.** Each removes your ability to dispute the charge.

A red flag is not proof of fraud. It is a reason to slow down. The combination of two or three is a reason to stop. The [low-quality market analysis](/blog/why-so-many-peptides-on-the-market-are-low-quality/) explains why these signals cluster.

## Decision framework: a 30-minute supplier scorecard

Use a weighted score to make the comparison explicit, then override it with judgment. The score is a conversation tool, not evidence.

| Criterion | Weight | What a 5 looks like |
|---|---:|---|
| Legal identity and ownership | 15% | Licence, credit record, and bank beneficiary all match; beneficial owner disclosed |
| Site and technical capability | 20% | Production address verified; reactors, purification, lyophilization, and QC match the order |
| Batch documentation | 25% | Lot-specific COA, raw HPLC/MS, batch record, MSDS, and customs papers all consistent |
| Quality system | 15% | Written specification, deviation procedure, change control, retain samples |
| Commercial terms | 15% | Clear MOQ, step pricing, lead time, remedy, and audit rights |
| Logistics and customs | 10% | Named broker, correct HS code, entry plan, insurance, and a realistic transit window |

Score each criterion 1–5, multiply by the weight, and add the results. A score of 4.0 or higher, with no disqualifying red flag, supports a first order. A score of 3.0–3.9 supports a sample order with a third-party test and a written quality annex. Below 3.0, keep looking.

Two rules sit above the score:

- **A disqualifier is not averaged away.** A personal bank account, a missing legal entity, or a refused audit is a zero for the whole supplier, not a low score in one category.
- **Score the evidence, not the salesperson.** A supplier with a polished deck and no batch record scores lower than a plain-spoken trader with a complete file.

Run the scorecard once before the first order and again before the first scale-up. The second run is where problems surface, because order size changes supplier behavior: a small order can be handled by a salesperson, while a large order exposes scheduling, documentation, and quality systems.

## FAQ

### 1. Can I buy research peptides from China?

Yes, in many jurisdictions, for laboratory research, if the compound is not controlled, the supplier is a legitimate entity, and the import meets local customs and research-use rules. The legal question is local, not Chinese. Check your own import rules, your institution's procurement policy, and whether the compound is scheduled.

### 2. How do I verify a Chinese peptide manufacturer?

Start with the business licence and the unified social credit code. Match the invoicing entity, bank beneficiary, and production address. Ask for the drug production licence or the relevant chemical production scope, then check the site with the provincial drug administration [10]. Ask who polymerizes the chain, the reactor size, and who signs the batch record. The [verification protocol](/blog/china-peptide-manufacturer-verification-a-2026-b2b-sourcing-protocol/) walks through the sequence.

### 3. What is a COA, and what does it not prove?

A Certificate of Analysis reports test results on a specific lot against a specification. It can show purity by HPLC, identity by MS, appearance, water content, and other release tests. It does not prove that the test was independent, that the method was suitable, or that the sample came from the vials you received. A COA is a claim about a lot; the raw chromatogram, method, and batch linkage make it checkable.

### 4. What MOQ should I expect?

It depends on the tier. The public Peptides of China catalog uses a $600 goods minimum per supplier order, with one first-order trial below that; shipping is separate. A synthesis shop may charge a setup fee instead. A CDMO may quote in kilograms. Do not compare MOQs without comparing what is included: filling, QC, documentation, and freight can sit outside the headline number.

### 5. What payment method is safest?

For a first order, a small T/T deposit with the balance against shipping documents is common. Escrow can help if release conditions are clear. A letter of credit suits large orders but adds document risk and bank fees. The safest method keeps the beneficiary name matched to the legal entity and gives you a written remedy if the batch fails. Never pay a personal account, a third-party account, or crypto.

### 6. Who is the importer of record?

The buyer, unless a licensed importer is explicitly contracted. The importer of record is legally responsible for the declaration, duty, and accuracy of the documents. A supplier can arrange freight and recommend a broker; it cannot transfer that responsibility by using DDP on an invoice. If you do not have an importer of record, talk to a customs broker before ordering.

### 7. How long does delivery take?

It depends on batch status, freight mode, and entry. An in-stock item can ship in days; a custom sequence can take weeks before it reaches the shipping desk. Air express is fastest and most expensive; sea freight is cheaper at volume and slower. Add time for customs entry, especially where de minimis has changed [1]. Ask for a stage-by-stage timeline.

### 8. What happens if the shipment is held or seized?

Contact the carrier and broker first; the notice usually names the agency and the reason. Provide the invoice, packing list, COA, MSDS, certificate of origin, and any permit. If the goods are detained for classification or admissibility, a broker or trade lawyer can respond. If they are seized or destroyed, the contract's remedy clause decides who bears the loss.

## The Author's Take

**Position.** I would rather buy from a trader that discloses its factory than from a "factory" that cannot show a batch record. The label is not the evidence. The chain is.

**Reasoning.** Most sourcing failures start with a buyer accepting a document that looks official and skipping the question underneath it. A COA with a 99% purity line and no method feels reassuring until the third-party lab returns a different profile. A "factory" with a polished website feels safer until the bank details do not match the business licence. The buyer optimizes for the story and underinvests in the chain of custody.

The suppliers that survive are boring in the right way. They answer technical questions with specifics. They send lot-specific documents without being asked twice. They tell you when a batch is delayed instead of inventing a ship date. They accept a quality annex. They do not promise customs clearance. Those behaviors are harder to fake than a website, and they predict the next batch better than a price list.

I also think buyers overpay for "factory-direct" as a concept. A good trader with audit rights, a named manufacturer, and a complete batch file can serve a small lab better than a large plant that treats the order as a nuisance. The goal is not to eliminate intermediaries; it is to make the chain legible.
