Most compliance pages open with a disclaimer and end with a shrug. This one opens with a warning letter.

In March 2026, the FDA wrote to Gram Peptides, a California seller offering retatrutide and tirzepatide. The product labeling carried "Research Use Only" statements and a disclaimer that the products were "not intended for human consumption, medical use, or veterinary use." The agency still classified them as unapproved new drugs. The evidence came from the website, which described "reducing body weight," "improving glucose handling," and "slowing gastric emptying," and referred to benefits seen in "both animal and human studies." The label said research. The marketing said medicine.

That gap is the subject of this page. A research-use label is a statement of purpose, and it does not settle purpose on its own. Regulators, customs officers, and institutional reviewers read the label alongside the product page, the invoice, the images, and the buyer's own records.

What follows is editorial analysis, not legal advice. It stays inside a research-use frame: no human use, no dosing, no medical or therapeutic claims. The goal is to show whether a supplier's compliance story survives contact with an entry file, a certificate database, and a skeptical reviewer.

## Direct Answer

Research-use-only is not a customs category, an import license, or a legal shield. It is a claim about intended use, and it holds only when the entire commercial record supports that claim. A supplier can print RUO on a vial and still fail the test if the surrounding marketing describes human benefit, a consumer outcome, or a schedule of use.

Three rules matter more than any label.

**Documents decide entry.** Customs authorities classify and admit goods on an entry file: invoice, packing list, transport document, and any agency paperwork the shipment triggers. A slogan has no line on that file.

**Claims decide intent.** Under 21 CFR 201.128, intended use is inferred from labeling, advertising, oral statements, and the circumstances of sale. "Labeling" is broader than the sticker on the vial. It includes the website, the catalog, the email that quotes a price, and the testimonial on the landing page.

**The importer of record owns the risk.** Whoever is named importer receives the detention notice, the duty bill, and the seizure letter. A sales page that implies "we handle everything" does not change that fact.

The useful question is not "is this legal?" but "can I prove what this is, where it was made, and what it is for?" The rest of this page is about building that proof.

## What Research-Use-Only Means in Practice

The phrase entered regulated trade through diagnostic reagents, not through the peptide market. FDA's 2013 guidance on in vitro diagnostic products says RUO labeling is meant to serve as a warning, to prevent a product from being used in clinical diagnosis, patient management, or a clinical investigation that is not exempt from 21 CFR Part 812. The same guidance says a product should not carry an RUO label if it is intended for clinical diagnostic use; doing so would make the labeling false or misleading.

Commercial research chemicals borrowed the convention. In that setting, RUO does four jobs:

- It states that the product is supplied for laboratory investigation.
- It disclaims clinical, diagnostic, and human-use applications.
- It signals that the product has not gone through the testing a medicine would require.
- It places the responsibility for appropriate use on the buyer.

None of those functions is a customs exemption, and none overrides the destination country's definition of a medicine.

What RUO does not do is equally important. It does not change the HS classification. It does not make an unapproved drug admissible. It does not transfer the obligation to make truthful claims if you resell the product. It does not convert a consumer sale into a laboratory sale. And it does not prevent a regulator from reading your website.

The intended-use test is the hinge. FDA does not need a confession. It can infer purpose from product names, category pages, blog posts, images of people, before-and-after photos, calculators, and the way a product is bundled with other items. In the Gram Peptides letter, "Bacteriostatic Water for Injection" sold alongside the peptides was treated as evidence that the peptides were intended for preparation and human use. One product can define the purpose of another.

A supplier's compliance posture is measured by the content it publishes and the documents it will sign, not by the label it prints. A supplier that cannot keep its own marketing inside the research frame will not keep your transaction inside it.

## The Claims Boundary: Where Marketing Becomes Intended Use

Most suppliers do not set out to make drug claims. They drift there because the market rewards it. The table shows the common drifts and a research-frame alternative.

| Wording that causes trouble | Why it reads as human use | Research-frame alternative |
|---|---|---|
| "Healing," "repair," "recovery" | Implies treatment of injury or disease | "Studied in in vitro wound-model assays" |
| "Weight loss," "fat loss," "slimming" | Disease or therapeutic outcome | "Incretin receptor agonist used in metabolic research" |
| "Dose," "protocol," "cycle," "stack" | Instructions for human administration | "Working concentration is set by the laboratory's own protocol" |
| Before-and-after photos, body scans | Evidence of human subjects | Published cell or animal data, cited |
| "Pharmaceutical grade," "clinical grade" | Implies an approved medicine | "Research grade; identity by MS; purity by HPLC" |
| "Sterile" on an untested product | Implies suitability for parenteral use | "Endotoxin tested by LAL; not sterile" |
| Testimonials describing personal results | Direct evidence of human use | Laboratory citations only |

In the United States, the FTC requires health-related claims to be supported by "competent and reliable scientific evidence," and its guidance says this generally means randomized controlled human trials for health benefit claims. A certificate of analysis proving 98% purity is not that evidence. It says nothing about whether a product does what the copy implies.

FDA's intended-use rule is the second layer. The agency can treat a product as a drug if the labeling or advertising suggests it will affect the structure or function of the body, or will be used to diagnose, cure, mitigate, treat, or prevent disease. The label "Research Use Only" is one data point. It is not a firewall.

The Gram Peptides letter is worth reading in full. The disputed phrases are not extravagant; they are the kind of copy that appears on hundreds of peptide sites: "supports balanced insulin levels," "associated with significant decreases in body weight," "improving glucose handling." Each sounds like a summary of published research. Together they describe a medicine. The agency did not need a dosing schedule to reach that conclusion.

A ten-minute claims audit catches most of this. Search your own catalog and content for: weight loss, fat loss, healing, repair, recovery, dose, dosage, cycle, stack, inject, sterile, anti-aging, libido, testosterone, before and after, clinically proven, doctor recommended. Then read the sentence around each hit. If the sentence promises an outcome in a person, rewrite it or remove it. If it describes a laboratory observation, keep it and cite the study.

Resellers need the same audit. The supplier's label does not control your page. Market a research reagent with human-benefit language and your page becomes the evidence.

## The Import Dossier: Documents That Should Travel With the Shipment

US entry documentation is not mysterious. 19 CFR 142.3 lists what CBP needs to release merchandise: an entry (CBP Form 3461 or its electronic equivalent), evidence of the right to make entry, a commercial invoice, a packing list where appropriate, and any other documents required by CBP or another federal agency for that shipment. The entry summary, CBP Form 7501, carries the value, classification, and origin data used to assess duty.

| Document | Who issues it | What it must contain | Common failure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Commercial invoice | Seller | Exact product name, sequence or CAS, quantity, unit value, Incoterm, origin, HS code, importer number | "Research peptide, 10 vials" with no chemistry |
| Packing list | Seller | Vial count, pack size, net and gross weight, batch-to-carton mapping | Quantities that disagree with the invoice |
| Air waybill or bill of lading | Carrier | Shipper, consignee, pieces, weight, service level | Consignee is an individual, not the lab |
| Certificate of analysis | Manufacturer | Batch number, specification, method, result, release date, signature | Generic COA reused across lots |
| Safety data sheet | Manufacturer | Section 1 identity, section 14 transport information | Section 14 blank or copied from another product |
| Certificate of origin | Seller or chamber | Country of manufacture, exporter, consignee | Origin stated as "China" with no site |
| GMP certificate | Regulator | Site, scope, validity, inspecting authority | Scope covers trading, not manufacturing |

The invoice is the first compliance document, not the last. It is what a customs officer reads to see whether the shipment matches its description. If the invoice says 5 mg vials, the COA says 10 mg, and the label says 2 mg, the shipment has already failed the consistency test. The file supplies the reason to examine it.

Agency admissibility is separate from tariff duty. FDA can refuse admission of articles that appear adulterated or misbranded under 21 U.S.C. 381. Import Alert 66-41, current version dated November 2, 2026, authorizes detention without physical examination of unapproved new drugs promoted in the United States. It reaches any product marketed with drug-like claims, whatever the label says.

Build the file before the order ships, not after it is held. Ask for a draft commercial invoice and packing list with the quotation. Compare the product name across invoice, COA, label, and SDS. Confirm who will be named importer and whether that entity has a bond and a broker. Failures caught here are cheap; failures at the port carry storage fees, brokerage fees, and a clock.

## HS Classification: Why the Code Is a Technical Claim

There is no single HS code for peptides. Classification depends on molecular structure, purity, presentation, and principal use. Most peptide trade lands in Chapter 29 organic chemicals or heading 3822 for prepared laboratory reagents.

| Heading | Typical content | Why it applies, or does not |
|---|---|---|
| 2937 | Polypeptide hormones and derivatives | Only when the molecule has hormonal structure and hormonal principal use |
| 2930 | Organo-sulfur compounds | Peptides with defining sulfur features, such as disulfide-rich sequences |
| 2933 | Nitrogen heterocycles | Synthetic peptides whose structure includes a heterocyclic ring |
| 2934 | Other heterocyclic compounds | Structures that fall outside 2933 |
| 2942 | Other organic compounds | Basket heading for compounds not covered elsewhere in Chapter 29 |
| 3822 | Prepared diagnostic or laboratory reagents | Kits and formulated products rather than bare compounds |

The General Rules for the Interpretation of the Harmonized System are applied in sequence. GRI 1 looks at heading text and chapter or section notes. Later rules handle incomplete goods, mixtures, and cases where two headings compete. For a pure, structurally defined peptide, GRI 1 usually decides once the structure is known.

The code is a technical claim, not a formality. The importer is asserting a structure and a use. A code chosen because "everyone uses 2937" is a guess with a duty rate attached. Different headings carry different duty rates, agency flags, and documentation expectations. A misclassification can produce an assessment, a penalty, or a release delay.

Two habits reduce the risk. Put the sequence or CAS number and structural description on the invoice, not just the trade name. Ask the supplier for its classification rationale in writing: heading, GRI applied, and the structural feature behind the choice. A supplier that cannot explain the code is guessing.

For repeated shipments of the same product, a binding ruling from CBP is worth the effort. It fixes the classification for that product and gives the broker something firmer than an opinion. The [HS code guide](/blog/hs-code-for-peptides/) covers the headings and ruling process in more detail; the [customs clearance guide](/blog/research-peptide-customs-clearance-a-professional-b2b-sourcing-guide-2026/) covers the entry sequence.

## Low-Value Shipments: The De Minimis Assumption Has Changed

For years, small peptide orders moved under the US Section 321 de minimis exemption, which allowed goods valued at or below $800 to enter without duty. Suppliers built pricing and courier choices around that threshold. Buyers treated a $600 order as a parcel, not an import.

That assumption is out of date. The exemption was suspended for China, Hong Kong, and Macau in 2025, then suspended for all countries effective August 29, 2025 under Executive Order 14324. In June 2026, CBP published an interim final rule making the suspension indefinite for merchandise arriving through all modes other than the international postal network. The postal channel has its own rules, but it is not a duty-free route.

A low-value shipment now needs entry data, may need a broker, and can attract duty, fees, and examination. Splitting an order into several $700 parcels does not restore the exemption; it creates several entries, several brokerage charges, and a paper trail that looks like structuring. Describing a commercial shipment as a gift to avoid duty is a false statement on an entry document.

For budget purposes, treat freight as one line and landed cost as another. Duty, brokerage, storage, and a possible second shipment after a seizure belong in the second. A quote that stops at the air waybill is not a landed cost.

## Supplier Representations: What to Ask For and How to Test It

A representation is a statement the supplier is willing to stand behind. The useful ones are specific enough to be checked. "We are a professional manufacturer" is not a representation. "We synthesize at this address, under this business license, and release each lot against this specification" is.

Ask for six things before the first order:

1. The exact legal entity name and the manufacturing site address, not the sales office.
2. The business license or company registration document.
3. The product specification sheet, including sequence, molecular weight, purity method, and acceptance criteria.
4. A lot-specific certificate of analysis with a batch number that matches the vials.
5. The safety data sheet, including section 14 transport information.
6. A written statement of who is responsible for export clearance and who is responsible for import clearance.

Then cross-check. The invoice entity should match the bank account. The GMP certificate site should match the business license. The air waybill shipper should match the site that made the product. The email domain should match the company name. None of these checks requires special access, only the willingness to look.

The most common failure is not a forged certificate but a real certificate for a different legal entity. A trading company holds a certificate issued to a factory it buys from, sends that PDF to the buyer, and signs the invoice under its own name. The certificate is genuine; the representation is not.

That distinction matters when something goes wrong. If the shipment is detained, the importer needs a supplier who can produce records, answer questions, and correct documents. A trader who cannot reach the factory's quality team cannot do any of that. Trader-sourced supply is not automatically bad; undisclosed trader-sourced supply is a documentation problem waiting for a trigger.

Contract terms deserve the same specificity. The [sourcing contract guide](/blog/how-peptide-sourcing-contracts-work-a-procurement-guide/) and the [supplier negotiation notes](/blog/peptide-supplier-contract-negotiation-tips-for-procurement/) cover the clauses that matter: specification, lot acceptance, deviation notice, recall cooperation, and allocation of seizure risk. A compliance page that ignores the contract is half a compliance page.

## GMP and ISO Certificates: Read the Scope, Not the Logo

Certificates are evidence only when the scope matches the product and the issuer can be checked.

A GMP certificate is issued by a regulatory authority after an inspection. It names a site, a scope, an inspecting authority, and a validity period. The scope is the part buyers skip. A certificate for non-sterile cosmetic ingredients does not establish GMP for an API, and one for a contract packager does not establish that the peptide was synthesized there. An expired certificate is a historical document.

In the EU, the EudraGMDP database is the public register for GMP certificates and non-compliance statements. It is maintained by EMA under the falsified medicines framework, and it lets a buyer search by site or certificate number. If a supplier claims an EU GMP certificate, the claim should be findable there. If it is not, ask why before you ask for a discount.

ISO certificates need the same treatment. ISO 9001 certifies a quality management system. It says the organization has documented processes, not that a specific lot meets a specific specification. A company can hold ISO 9001 and still ship the wrong peptide. ISO/IEC 17025 is different: it certifies the competence of a testing laboratory for defined methods.

The verification path is straightforward. Ask for the certificate number, certification body, scope, and expiry date. Check the body against the relevant accreditation register, check the number with the issuer, and compare the site address to the invoice. Look for "trading," "distribution," or "sales office" in the scope.

Certificate fraud has patterns: a number that returns no record, a scope for a different product category, an address that differs by a floor or city, visible editing artifacts, an unaccredited certificate body, and "GMP compliant" used where "GMP certified" is implied. None is proof alone; together they justify a pause.

The [GMP assessment guide](/blog/how-to-assess-peptide-supplier-gmp-compliance-in-2026/) goes deeper on inspection scope, and [research grade versus GMP](/blog/research-grade-vs-gmp-peptides/) explains why the two labels are not interchangeable.

## Third-Party Testing Claims: Accreditation, Scope, and Sample Identity

"Third-party tested" is one of the least standardized phrases in the peptide trade. It can mean a serious lab, or a column run by someone's cousin.

Four questions separate the useful claims from the noise.

**Which laboratory?** A named lab with a verifiable address and accreditation number. "An independent US lab" is not a name.

**Which accreditation and scope?** ISO/IEC 17025 accreditation covers specific methods. A lab accredited for water analysis is not thereby accredited for peptide purity by HPLC. The ILAC mutual recognition arrangement helps verify the body and the lab, but the scope still has to include the method.

**Which method?** Purity by HPLC needs the column, mobile phase, gradient, wavelength, and integration method. Identity by mass spectrometry needs the ionization mode and the observed mass. Endotoxin by LAL needs the assay format and the limit. A result without a method is a number without a meaning.

**Which sample?** This decides most disputes. A lab report is evidence about the sample it received. If the supplier selected a "golden batch," the report says nothing about your lot. The report's lot number must match the vials, and sampling should be traceable to the shipment. Buyer-side sampling or a third-party inspection closes that gap.

A legitimate report carries the lab's name and accreditation mark, a unique number, the sample description and lot, the receipt date, the method, raw data or chromatogram, the result against specification, and an authorized signature. A report with only a percentage and a logo is a marketing asset, not a test.

External testing is a per-lot cost. A quote that undercuts the market by a third while promising full external testing for every lot has to be explained. Either the volume is enormous, the testing is selective, or the claim is decorative.

Our own position on third-party certificates is disclosed in [this note](/blog/why-we-do-not-provide-third-party-coas-and-what-that-actually-means/). Read it alongside [how to authenticate a COA](/blog/coa-verified-peptides-supplier-how-to-authenticate-research-purity-in-2026/) and [how to read an HPLC and MS report](/blog/how-to-read-a-peptide-coa-a-researchers-guide-to-hplc-and-ms-reports/). The point is not who issues the paper. The point is whether the paper is traceable to the vial in your hand.

## Jurisdiction: One Vial, Several Regulators

The same vial can be a research reagent in one country, a medicine in another, and a controlled substance in a third. Jurisdiction is fixed at the destination, not at the supplier's warehouse.

In the United States, the FD&C Act supplies the drug definition, FDA supplies the intended-use test, and CBP supplies the entry process. A product labeled RUO can still be an unapproved new drug if the marketing establishes human use. State-level rules can add requirements for consumer sales.

In the European Union, the medicinal product definition is set at Union level and applied by member states. Imported active substances need a written confirmation from the exporting country's competent authority attesting that manufacturing and control standards are equivalent to EU GMP, unless the country is on the Commission's equivalence list. The requirement sits with the importer. A supplier that cannot obtain the confirmation cannot open that lane.

The United Kingdom applies its own medicines regime after Brexit. China regulates exports through customs declaration and exporter registration, and maintains export controls for sensitive goods. None of these regimes uses "research use only" as a harmonized legal category. Each asks its own question about the product's presentation and intended use.

Write the jurisdictional answer down before ordering: destination country, legal category, import route, documents required, and the person who will handle a hold. If the answer is "the supplier said it is fine," there is no answer. Our [research-use disclaimer](/research-use-disclaimer/) and [terms and conditions](/terms-and-conditions/) describe how we frame the boundary; they do not replace destination-country advice.

## Shipping Restrictions: Carrier Rules, Air Cargo, and Temperature

Carriers are not regulators, but they can stop a shipment faster. DHL's restricted commodities guidance lists biological substances and UN-classified dangerous goods among items requiring special handling or refused outright, and points to IATA rules for air transport. Lyophilized, non-infectious peptides usually do not meet the Category B biological substance definition, but that determination belongs in the SDS and on the air waybill.

Dry ice changes the paperwork. It is a Class 9 dangerous good, with quantity limits per package and specific marking requirements. A shipment packed with dry ice without a declaration is a compliance problem for the shipper and a delay for the consignee. Gel packs avoid the dangerous goods declaration but do not guarantee a temperature range.

Carrier policy varies by lane. Some forwarders refuse "research chemicals" as a category, some accept peptides but reject anything described as injectable, and some route through a third country to avoid a direct lane. That creates new import rules and a new chance for a misdescription. A forwarder who offers to "handle the customs description" is offering to make the importer responsible for someone else's wording.

Temperature is the quieter risk. A lyophilized vial is more stable than a reconstituted solution, but not indestructible. A parcel that sits for a week in an August customs warehouse has been through a stability experiment nobody designed. Ask for a data logger, define the acceptable excursion in the purchase order, and inspect the logger on arrival. The [shipping documentation note](/blog/why-legitimate-documentation-matters-in-peptide-shipping/) covers the paperwork side; the [stability guide](/blog/peptide-stability-why-lyophilized-peptides-are-far-more-stable-than-you-think/) covers the product side.

## Refund, Reshipment, and Who Owns the Seizure Risk

Three different risks get mixed together in peptide sales: loss or damage in transit, customs detention, and regulatory refusal. They have different causes, different evidence, and different remedies. A policy that treats them as one thing is usually a policy written to sound generous.

Incoterms decide who clears the goods. Under DDP, the seller is responsible for import clearance and duty. Under DAP, the buyer clears. Under FOB or CIF, the buyer is the importer once the goods are on board. The importer of record is the party that receives the detention notice and the seizure letter, regardless of who paid the freight.

That is why "we reship seized orders" is not the same as "you are protected." A reshipment is a second entry file and a second chance of seizure. If the first was seized because the product was inadmissible, the second meets the same rule. A supplier that reships automatically without asking why the first parcel was stopped is repeating the problem, not solving it.

Ask four questions before relying on a reshipment promise:

- What evidence is required to trigger a reship: a seizure notice, a detention notice, or a tracking status?
- Is the reship free, discounted, or at cost?
- Does the policy exclude products that were misdescribed or ordered without required documents?
- What is the time limit for a claim, and who pays storage or return freight?

The answers should be in the policy, not a sales email. Our [shipping and reshipment policy](/shipping-and-reshipment-policy/) and [refund and cancellation policy](/refund-and-cancellation-policy/) are public for comparison.

Seizure risk is a cost. If a supplier does not price it, the buyer pays in a lost order; if the supplier prices it, the buyer pays in the quote. Either way, someone pays. A quote that appears to ignore the risk has usually not met it yet.

## A Compliance Audit Checklist for a New Supplier

This is the checklist I would use before a first order. Each item has a pass condition; "the supplier said yes" is not one.

| # | Item | Evidence | Pass condition |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Legal entity | Business license or registration | Name matches invoice, bank account, and contract |
| 2 | Manufacturing site | Site address on license and certificate | Address is a production site, not a sales office |
| 3 | Product specification | Specification sheet with sequence, MW, purity method | Acceptance criteria are numeric and testable |
| 4 | Lot COA | Batch-specific report | Batch number matches vials and packing list |
| 5 | Independent testing | Lab report with accreditation number | Lab scope covers the method; sample traceable to lot |
| 6 | GMP certificate | Certificate number and scope | Site and product scope match; certificate current |
| 7 | ISO certificate | Certificate number, body, scope | Scope covers the activity claimed; body accredited |
| 8 | SDS | Sections 1 and 14 complete | Identity and transport information match the product |
| 9 | Invoice draft | Commercial invoice | HS code, origin, Incoterm, and importer number present |
| 10 | Export responsibility | Written allocation | Named party responsible for export clearance |
| 11 | Import responsibility | Written allocation | Named importer of record with a customs bond |
| 12 | Deviation and recall | Contract clause | Supplier must notify before or after release, with timeline |
| 13 | Record retention | Written policy | Batch records retained for the agreed period |
| 14 | Claims review | Website and catalog sample | No human-use, dosing, or therapeutic claims |

Twelve of fourteen is not a guarantee, and two failures do not disqualify a supplier for a low-risk order. The purpose is to find the items that will matter if the shipment is held: entity, lot COA, independent testing, and importer of record.

Run the checklist on a sample order first. A sample reveals how the supplier handles documents, not just how the product looks. If it arrives with a generic COA and an invoice that says "peptide sample," the bulk order will arrive the same way, with more zeros.

## Red Flags That Predict Trouble

Most bad suppliers announce themselves before the first payment.

- A COA with the same lot number across shipments that span months.
- A certificate whose scope says "trading" or "distribution" while the sales email says "our factory."
- A website calculator that converts milligrams into units for preparation, or sells bacteriostatic water alongside every peptide.
- Before-and-after photos, body-composition charts, or testimonials describing personal results.
- An invoice description of "gift," "sample," or "supplement" with a value below the actual price.
- A bank beneficiary in a different country from the contracting entity.
- No SDS, or an SDS with section 14 left blank.
- "FDA registered" presented as "FDA approved," or "GMP certified" with no certificate number.
- A refusal to name the manufacturing site, or a site that changes when you ask twice.
- Pressure to pay through a personal account, a payment app, or an irreversible channel.

None is proof on its own; a single flag can have an innocent explanation. Three together describe a supplier whose paperwork will not survive review. The list moves the burden of proof: a supplier with a real factory answers quickly, and one without explains why each question is difficult.

## Decision Framework: Proceed, Pause, or Walk Away

Compliance decisions are easier when they are separated into gates. I use three.

| Gate | Question | Evidence needed | Stop rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| Legality | Can this product enter my jurisdiction for research use? | Destination rule, legal category, import route | Any unresolved "no" or "unknown" stops the order |
| Documentation | Can I prove identity, purity, and origin? | Lot COA, specification, invoice, SDS, certificates | Missing lot linkage pauses the order |
| Commercial | Can I absorb a seizure, a delay, or a reship? | Landed cost, contract terms, insurance | No named importer or no buffer pauses the order |

Score each gate from 0 to 2. A 2 means the evidence is in hand and consistent; a 1 means plausible but undocumented; a 0 means unknown or contradicted by the file.

A total of 6 is a proceed. A 4 or 5 is a proceed only with the missing evidence supplied before payment. A 3 or less is a walk. Any 0 on legality is an automatic stop, even if the total is high; that rule keeps a good price from overruling an import problem.

A simpler test catches most of what the scoring misses. Would you forward the entire file, including the supplier's website link, invoice, COA, and shipping plan, to your compliance officer or customs broker? If no, the file is not ready. If yes, you have probably done the work.

## FAQ

**Does an RUO label make a peptide legal to import?**

No. Import legality depends on destination rules, product classification, and entry documents. An RUO label describes intended use; it creates no exemption, license, or admissible status. Customs can still detain a shipment that appears to be an unapproved drug or a misdescribed article.

**Can a supplier ship peptides to my lab without an import license?**

That depends on the destination and the product. Many jurisdictions allow research reagents to be imported by registered institutions without a product-specific license, but the importer may still need customs registration, a bond, or agency notification. The supplier's willingness to ship is not evidence that the route is legal. Confirm with a broker or the destination authority before ordering.

**Which documents should arrive before I pay?**

At minimum: a pro forma invoice with HS code and Incoterm, a product specification, a lot-specific COA, an SDS, and a written statement of who handles export and import clearance. For regulated destinations, add the GMP certificate or written confirmation the route requires. A supplier that cannot produce a draft invoice before payment will not produce a clean entry file after it.

**Is a manufacturer COA enough to prove purity?**

It is evidence, not proof. A COA is only as good as the method behind it and the sample it describes. Check that the batch number matches the vials, the method is stated, and the result is compared with a numeric specification. For high-value orders, have a sample tested independently and keep the chain of custody.

**What does an ISO 9001 certificate prove about a peptide?**

It proves the company has a certified quality management system. It does not certify the product, the purity, or the site's GMP status. Compare the scope and site address with the entity you are buying from. If the scope covers trading rather than manufacturing, the certificate says nothing about who made the peptide.

**Who is responsible if customs seizes the shipment?**

The importer of record receives the legal notice and generally bears the loss, unless the contract says otherwise. Incoterms and the purchase agreement decide who pays for seizure, storage, and return. A reshipment promise is a commercial remedy, not a legal transfer of responsibility. Put the allocation in writing before shipment.

**Can I resell peptides labeled for research use?**

Resale is possible in many markets, but the reseller inherits the claims problem. Your product page, catalog, and customer communications become labeling evidence. If they describe human benefits or dosing, the RUO label on the vial will not protect you. Check destination rules for consumer sales and keep resale content inside the research frame.

**What if the supplier's HS code looks wrong?**

Ask for the classification rationale: heading, GRI applied, and the structural feature that supports it. Compare it with the product's sequence, purity, and presentation. If the answer is vague, get a second opinion from a customs broker or request a binding ruling for repeated shipments. Do not accept a code because it cleared once; release is not a classification determination.

## The Author's Take

**Position.** I treat research-use-only as a discipline of restraint, not a permission slip. The suppliers I trust are the ones whose marketing is boring: a sequence, a specification, a lot number, a method, and no story about what the product will do for a person.

**Reasoning.** Three patterns drive that position. Enforcement follows marketing, not labels; the Gram Peptides letter shows how ordinary product copy establishes intended use even when the vial says research. Documentation is the only part of the transaction a buyer can audit before money moves; a lot-linked COA and a clean invoice predict more than any certificate logo. And seizure risk is routinely mispriced; a reshipment promise sounds like insurance until the second parcel meets the same rule as the first.

It also shows me how often a certificate belongs to a different entity than the one answering the email. I have tried to make the checklist and red flags useful to buyers who never contact us; the policy links are there so you can compare our answers with anyone else's.
