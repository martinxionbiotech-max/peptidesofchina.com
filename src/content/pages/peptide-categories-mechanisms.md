Peptide class names are the most common shorthand in this market and the least reliable one. A vial labeled "GHRP" tells you which receptor family the sequence is supposed to engage. It does not tell you whether the sequence is correct, whether the C-terminus is amidated, whether the batch contains truncated fragments, or whether the assay the supplier ran can detect any of those problems. This page maps the major research peptide classes and the sourcing questions each makes urgent.

This page is written for laboratory and procurement readers and describes research compounds and preclinical evidence categories. It does not describe human use, dosing, injection schedules, or therapeutic outcomes, and nothing on this page should be read as a recommendation to administer any compound to a person.

## Direct answer: a class is a hypothesis, not a specification

A peptide class describes a shared mechanism or structural family. It is a starting point for reading literature, not a certificate of identity, purity, potency, safety, or regulatory status. Two vials can carry the same class label and contain different molecules, different counterions, different impurity profiles, and different amounts of active peptide.

The consequence is simple: classify the compound to decide what evidence to read and what analytical methods to request. Then verify the specific batch against a specification. Buyers who reverse that order — choosing a supplier because the label says "GLP-1" or "GHRP" and assuming the rest — are buying a category, not a product.

## How to classify a peptide without trusting the label

Classification works best when you separate five questions that marketing language tends to collapse into one.

**Mechanistic class.** What receptor, enzyme, ion channel, or binding partner does the sequence engage? GHRH analogs and GHRPs both raise growth hormone readouts in some assays, but they act on different receptors. That difference changes the downstream hormone profile, the feedback response, and the off-target panel a lab should run.

**Structural family.** Is the molecule linear or cyclic? Does it contain disulfides, D-amino acids, non-natural residues, a lipid chain, or a PEG? Structural features predict protease resistance, aggregation, and the analytical methods that will separate the product from its impurities.

**Origin and reference status.** Is the sequence an endogenous hormone fragment, a synthetic analog, a fragment of a larger protein, or a research-only design with no clinical reference? Endogenous sequences often have well-characterized receptors but short half-lives. Synthetic analogs may have longer circulation and less historical safety data.

**Evidence stage.** Approved drug, investigational candidate, preclinical tool, or cosmetic ingredient. These are different evidence categories, and a page that mixes them is not giving you a class definition. It is giving you a marketing position.

**Formulation role.** Is the peptide the active ingredient, a stabilizer, a counterion, or a preservative? Some catalog items — bacteriostatic water, benzyl alcohol, acetic acid — are handling materials, not peptides.

A short worksheet keeps the classification honest:

1. Write the full sequence and any terminal modifications.
2. Name the primary target and the evidence stage.
3. Identify the structural features that affect stability.
4. List the assays that would distinguish the intended molecule from its likely impurities.
5. Only then compare suppliers.

For the binding side of the question, [peptide selectivity explained](/blog/peptide-selectivity-explained-for-researchers/) walks through affinity ratios and receptor panels. Neither replaces a batch specification.

## Sequence and modification basics that change behavior

A peptide is a short chain of amino acids linked by amide bonds. The usual working range is two to fifty residues. Small changes in the chain can produce large changes in behavior.

**Length and composition.** A four-residue peptide and a thirty-nine-residue peptide are not managed the same way. Short sequences are cheaper per gram, often harder to quantify by UV absorbance when they lack aromatic residues, and more likely to co-elute with buffer salts in a simple HPLC method. Longer sequences have more opportunities for truncation, deamidation, and aggregation.

**Termini.** The N-terminus and C-terminus are chemical handles. A free amine at the N-terminus can cyclize with a nearby residue, be acetylated, or carry a pyroglutamate after storage. A C-terminal amide removes a negative charge and often improves receptor binding for neuropeptides and GHRH analogs. A supplier that lists "GHRP-6" without stating the amidated C-terminus is leaving a structural question open.

**Charge and hydrophobicity.** These two properties predict solubility, membrane interaction, and HPLC retention. They also predict which counterions will be needed to make a stable salt. A highly cationic peptide such as LL-37 behaves differently from a neutral, lipophilic peptide such as SS-31, and the analytical methods that resolve one may fail on the other.

**Disulfide bonds and cyclization.** Disulfides lock a peptide into a conformation and protect it from some proteases. They also create scrambling and reduced/oxidized variants that a purity method must resolve. Cyclic peptides can have better enzymatic stability, but the closure chemistry adds another identity check.

**Non-natural residues and D-amino acids.** D-amino acids are common in GHRP sequences because they slow proteolysis. They also mean the peptide cannot be verified by a simple amino acid analysis that assumes L-stereochemistry. Mass spectrometry confirms mass, not stereochemistry. A chiral method or an enzymatic digestion may be needed.

**Lipidation, PEGylation, and albumin binding.** These modifications extend circulation by increasing size or binding to albumin. CJC-1295 with DAC uses a maleimidopropionic acid linker that reacts with serum albumin. Semaglutide uses a fatty diacid linker. The modification is part of the molecule, not an accessory. A batch that lacks the lipid chain is a different compound, even if the peptide backbone is correct.

**Salt form and counterion.** Peptides are usually isolated as acetate, trifluoroacetate, or chloride salts. The counterion affects solubility, pH, and the mass balance of the lyophilized powder. A certificate that reports 98% "purity" by HPLC may not account for the counterion and water content, which can be a meaningful fraction of the powder. This is why [counterion choice](/blog/role-of-counterions-in-peptide-stability/) belongs in the specification, not in a footnote.

The [modification overview](/blog/how-peptide-modifications-affect-activity/) covers how these changes alter activity. At purchase, every modification you rely on should appear in the sequence description and, where possible, the batch documentation.

## GHRH analogs versus GHRP secretagogues

The two families are often discussed together because both can increase growth hormone release in research models, but they are separate systems.

GHRH is a hypothalamic hormone that binds the pituitary GHRH receptor, a class B G-protein-coupled receptor that signals through Gs and the cAMP/PKA pathway. The full peptide is 44 residues, but the 1–29 fragment retains receptor activity and is the backbone of several analogs. Sermorelin is an amidated GHRH(1–29). Tesamorelin is a stabilized 44-residue analog. CJC-1295 is a GHRH(1–29) analog with substitutions that resist degradation, optionally coupled to a drug-affinity complex for albumin binding.

GHRPs were first synthesized in 1977 as enkephalin analogues that stimulated growth hormone release from rat pituitary cells. GHRP-6, the first hexapeptide with clear in vivo activity, does not bind the GHRH receptor. It binds the growth hormone secretagogue receptor, now known as the ghrelin receptor or GHS-R1a, which couples through Gq/11 and Gi proteins and activates phospholipase C. GHRPs act at both the pituitary and the hypothalamus, and they stimulate growth hormone release while preserving the feedback mechanisms that constrain exogenous growth hormone.

The two systems interact. In clinical pharmacology, GHRH and GHRP given together produce a larger growth hormone response than either alone. That synergy is one reason combination products exist. It is also one reason a buyer should not treat a GHRH analog and a GHRP as interchangeable "growth hormone peptides."

|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sermorelin | GHRH analog | GHRH-R | Minutes | GHRH(1–29), C-terminal amide |
| Tesamorelin | GHRH analog | GHRH-R | Longer than native GHRH | 44 residues, N-terminal stabilization |
| CJC-1295 without DAC | GHRH analog | GHRH-R | Short | Four substitutions, no albumin binder |
| CJC-1295 with DAC | GHRH analog | GHRH-R | Days | Maleimidopropionic acid linker |

Two sourcing consequences follow. First, GHRH analogs are more sensitive to truncation and terminal modification than short GHRPs, because the receptor interaction depends on a longer N-terminal region. Second, GHRP batches need stereochemical and amidation checks that a generic purity report may not include. A 98% HPLC peak can hide a mixture of diastereomers.

## The GLP-1 receptor agonist class and its expanding edges

GLP-1 is a 30-amino-acid incretin hormone produced by cleavage of proglucagon in intestinal L-cells, pancreatic alpha cells, and neurons of the nucleus of the solitary tract. Its receptor is a class B GPCR. Activation increases glucose-dependent insulin secretion, reduces glucagon release, slows gastric emptying, and reduces food intake through central appetite pathways. Endogenous GLP-1 is degraded rapidly by dipeptidyl peptidase-4, with a plasma half-life measured in minutes.

The drug class was built by solving that stability problem. Liraglutide attaches a fatty acid and carries one amino acid substitution. Semaglutide uses an Aib substitution at position 8, an Arg34 substitution, and a fatty diacid linker that promotes albumin binding. Dulaglutide fuses the peptide to an Fc fragment. These are different molecules with different pharmacokinetics, not different strengths of one product.

The class has also expanded beyond a single receptor. Tirzepatide is a dual GIP/GLP-1 receptor agonist. Retatrutide adds glucagon receptor activity. Mazdutide and survodutide are GLP-1/glucagon co-agonists. Cagrilintide is an amylin receptor agonist, not a GLP-1 agonist, though it is often shelved with them commercially. Orforglipron is an oral non-peptide GLP-1 receptor agonist, which makes the phrase "GLP-1 peptide" inaccurate for that molecule.

That expansion creates a classification problem. "GLP-1" is used in the market to mean a clinical area, a receptor, a peptide family, and a procurement category. A buyer who asks for "a GLP-1" without specifying the receptor portfolio may receive a compound that does not match the intended assay. A dual agonist is not a stronger GLP-1; it is a different pharmacological tool with a different off-target profile.

| Compound | Receptor profile | Structural theme | Classification note |
|---|---|---|---|
| Semaglutide | GLP-1 | Fatty diacid, Aib8, Arg34 | Long-acting single agonist |
| Tirzepatide | GIP and GLP-1 | 39 residues, C20 fatty diacid | Dual agonist, not a GLP-1-only tool |
| Retatrutide | GIP, GLP-1, glucagon | 39 residues, lipid modification | Triple agonist, investigational |
| Mazdutide | GLP-1 and glucagon | Oxyntomodulin-derived | Metabolic co-agonist |
| Cagrilintide | Amylin | Amylin analog | Not a GLP-1 receptor agonist |
| Orforglipron | GLP-1 | Small molecule | Not a peptide |

This class is analytically harder than its reputation suggests. Deamidation of asparagine and glutamine residues, oxidation of methionine, isomerization of aspartate, and aggregation all produce species that can co-elute with the parent peak in a shallow HPLC gradient. Fatty-acid conjugation adds lipophilicity and can cause carryover. A credible batch record for a long-acting GLP-1 analog should include a gradient that resolves the main peak from deamidated and oxidized forms, plus mass confirmation of the intact conjugate.

## Cosmetic and repair peptides are not one category

The phrase "cosmetic peptide" describes a use category, not a mechanism. It usually means a peptide sold to formulators for topical products: GHK-Cu, palmitoyl pentapeptide-4 (Matrixyl), acetyl hexapeptide-8 (Argireline), and acetyl octapeptide-3 (Snap-8) are familiar examples. Their in vitro stories differ. GHK-Cu is a copper-binding tripeptide studied for gene-expression changes and collagen-related endpoints in skin models. Matrixyl is a palmitoylated procollagen fragment studied for matrix protein synthesis. Argireline and Snap-8 are marketed around neurotransmitter-release themes, with in vitro data that do not establish topical efficacy.

Repair-signaling peptides are a different group. BPC-157, TB-500, KPV, and LL-37 are research compounds studied in preclinical wound, inflammation, and tissue-repair models. They are not cosmetic ingredients, and their literature is not a topical formulation literature. The overlap is commercial, not mechanistic.

Three purchase differences follow.

**Purity means different things.** A cosmetic ingredient may be sold with a specification built around appearance, color, and copper content. A research peptide specification is built around sequence identity, HPLC purity, counterion, water content, and endotoxin. A cosmetic-grade batch is not automatically a suitable research-grade batch.

**Formulation dominates the cosmetic case.** A peptide that is active on a monolayer of keratinocytes may not cross the stratum corneum. Vehicle, pH, preservative, and package all change the result. A supplier's in vitro data cannot be transferred to a finished formula without a penetration study.

**Repair claims travel poorly.** A rodent model of a specific tissue injury does not establish that a peptide repairs other tissues, that it works by a single mechanism, or that a topical product delivers it to the relevant tissue. The site's [BPC-157 mechanism review](/blog/bpc-157-mechanism-of-action-studies-a-technical-review-for-research-procurement/) and [BPC-157 versus TB-500 comparison](/blog/bpc-157-vs-tb-500/) set out the preclinical boundaries.

For GHK-Cu specifically, copper content is the specification that buyers most often skip. The peptide is a complex, not just a sequence. Copper loading affects color, solubility, and the molar ratio of copper to peptide. An ICP-MS copper assay plus a peptide-content assay gives a more useful picture than a single HPLC purity number. The [GHK-Cu sourcing guide](/blog/ghk-cu-wholesale-supplier-a-professional-guide-to-direct-to-manufacturer-sourcing/) covers that specification in detail, and the [skin-peptide review](/blog/types-of-bioactive-peptides-for-skincare-2026-guide/) covers the wider ingredient group.

## Bioregulators: short sequences, large claims, uneven evidence

Bioregulators are short peptides, typically two to seven residues, associated with tissue-specific gene-regulation claims. Epithalon (Ala-Glu-Asp-Gly, or AEDG) is the most recognizable example in the research catalog. Related compounds include Livagen, Vesugen, Cardiogen, Bronchogen, Cartalax, Pinealon, and Ovagen, each with a tissue or system theme in its naming.

The mechanistic proposition is that these short sequences enter cells and interact with chromatin or gene-regulatory machinery in a way that is tissue-specific. The published literature, reviewed by Khavinson and colleagues, argues that short peptides can regulate gene expression and that the effects depend on sequence and concentration. That is a testable proposition, and the review assembles a large body of work around it.

The evidence has a recognizable shape. Much of the literature comes from a relatively small group of research institutions, uses in vitro and animal models, and reports endpoints that are not always replicated by independent groups. That does not make the data false. It makes the evidence base narrower than the marketing language usually implies. A 2021 systematic review is a reason to read the primary studies, not a substitute for them.

Sourcing short bioregulators has its own failure modes. A four-residue peptide has a low molecular weight, so a small mass impurity can represent a large molar fraction. Residual salts and water can dominate the powder. The peptide may not retain well on a standard C18 column without ion-pairing. And because the sequences are simple, a supplier can synthesize them cheaply — which means price competition is severe and documentation is often thin.

A useful specification for this class includes the exact sequence, counterion, water content, purity by HPLC with a method that resolves the peptide from salt, and identity by mass spectrometry. For Epithalon, the [product page](/catalog/epithalon/) lists the sequence and pack format; the [wholesale sourcing guide](/blog/epithalon-wholesale-sourcing-a-b2b-guide-to-direct-manufacturer-channels-2026/) covers the commercial side.

## Mitochondrial peptides: three different mechanisms under one label

"Mitochondrial peptide" can mean a peptide that is encoded in mitochondrial DNA, a peptide that targets mitochondria, or a peptide that changes mitochondrial function. The compounds here illustrate the difference.

**SS-31 (elamipretide)** is a synthetic tetrapeptide with alternating aromatic and cationic residues. It concentrates in the inner mitochondrial membrane and binds cardiolipin, a phospholipid that is important for cristae structure and respiratory chain organization. Its mechanism is biophysical and structural, not receptor-mediated. A 2025 review summarizes the structure, mechanism, and development history.

**Humanin** is a 24-residue peptide encoded by a short open reading frame in the mitochondrial 16S ribosomal RNA region. It has been studied in cellular stress, apoptosis, and metabolic models. **MOTS-c** is a 16-residue peptide encoded in the 12S rRNA region, studied for AMPK signaling and metabolic regulation. **SHLP1–6** are additional mitochondrial-derived peptides identified in the same literature. A 2022 review in the *Journal of Clinical Investigation* counts eight published mitochondrial-derived peptides: humanin, MOTS-c, and SHLP1–6.

The distinction matters because "mitochondria-targeted" and "mitochondria-derived" predict different assays. SS-31 binding to cardiolipin can be studied with liposomes, membrane potential, or respiration assays. Humanin and MOTS-c are usually studied through receptor or signaling readouts, and their effects may depend on extracellular administration and tissue context. A supplier's claim that a peptide "targets mitochondria" does not tell you which of these models applies.

Sourcing risks also differ. SS-31 is hydrophobic and cationic, which makes it prone to adsorption and aggregation at high concentration. Its purity profile should be checked for scrambled or truncated sequences, and its counterion matters for solubility. MOTS-c and humanin are more conventional linear peptides, but their biological activity can depend on terminal modifications and on the absence of endotoxin in cell-culture work. The [SS-31 product page](/catalog/ss-31/), [MOTS-c page](/catalog/mots-c/), and [humanin page](/catalog/humanin/) list the available formats; the [mitochondrial aging review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35499074/) is the better entry point for the biology.

## Blends: when a mixture is a formulation, not a mechanism

A blend is a formulation decision. It is not a mechanistic class. The catalog includes [Wolverine Blend](/catalog/wolverine-blend/) (BPC-157 and TB-500), [GLOW](/catalog/glow/), [KLOW](/catalog/klow/), and [CJC-1295 plus ipamorelin](/catalog/cjc-1295-ipamorelin/). Each combines compounds that have separate literatures and separate analytical signatures.

The rationale for a blend is usually complementary pathways: one component acts on actin or angiogenesis, another on cell migration or matrix deposition; one stimulates growth hormone release through GHRH, another through the ghrelin receptor. That is a reasonable research hypothesis, not evidence of synergy.

Synergy has a specific meaning in pharmacology. The Chou-Talalay combination index expresses the effect of a combination relative to the expected additive effect: values below 1 indicate synergy, a value of 1 indicates additivity, and values above 1 indicate antagonism. The calculation depends on the dose-response curves of each component alone, the ratio tested, and the endpoint. A blend sold at a fixed mass ratio is rarely tested across the ratio space, and an in vitro combination index does not translate automatically to an animal model.

Blends also create analytical problems that single-peptide specifications do not solve:

- **Co-elution.** Two peptides with similar hydrophobicity can appear as one peak in a standard reverse-phase method. A purity number for the blend may describe the combined peak, not the individual components.
- **Ratio control.** A label that states "5 mg + 5 mg" is a mass ratio. The molar ratio depends on molecular weight. For components of different size, the two ratios are not the same.
- **Homogeneity.** Blended powders can segregate during filling. A sample from the top of a vial may not represent the bottom.
- **Stability mismatch.** One component may oxidize or deamidate faster than the other. A single expiry date hides that difference.
- **Sterility and endotoxin.** A blend has more surfaces and more handling steps, which increases the risk of contamination during compounding.

A credible blend specification includes per-component identity, per-component assay, the ratio by mass and by mole, a homogeneity check, and a stability protocol that monitors each component separately. Without those, "blend" is a packaging term.

The [chemical synergy review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28473769/) is a useful primer on what the word "synergy" actually requires.

## Receptor selectivity: affinity, efficacy and bias

Class labels often imply selectivity the molecule does not have. Receptor pharmacology separates three ideas that are easy to conflate.

**Affinity** is how tightly a ligand binds. It is usually expressed as a dissociation constant (Kd) or, in competition experiments, an inhibition constant (Ki). **Potency** is the concentration that produces a given functional response, often an EC50 or IC50. **Efficacy** is the maximum response a ligand can produce. A ligand can bind tightly and produce a weak response, or bind loosely and produce a full response.

**Selectivity** is a ratio, not a property. A peptide that binds the intended receptor ten times more tightly than a related subtype is moderately selective. A thousand-fold ratio is high selectivity. The number depends on the panel tested, the assay format, and the species of the receptor. A supplier's claim of "highly selective" without a panel is not a measurement.

**Biased agonism** adds another layer. A ligand can activate one downstream pathway more than another through the same receptor. That is a real pharmacological phenomenon, and it means that two compounds with similar binding affinities can produce different signaling profiles. For a research buyer, the practical question is whether the assay you plan to run measures the pathway you care about.

GHRP-6 illustrates the problem. It binds GHS-R1a, but it also stimulates ACTH and cortisol release in published human studies, which is a reminder that a single receptor label does not capture the full endocrine response. Ipamorelin was developed with a more selective profile in animal models. The difference is relevant to study design, not just to marketing.

The site's [selectivity explainer](/blog/peptide-selectivity-explained-for-researchers/) covers affinity ratios, radioligand displacement, surface plasmon resonance, and receptor panels. The sourcing lesson is to ask for the panel, not the adjective.

## Preclinical models: what a class label cannot tell you

A peptide class predicts a plausible mechanism. It does not predict whether a particular cell line expresses the relevant receptor, whether the peptide survives the assay matrix, or whether the readout is specific.

**Receptor expression.** Immortalized cell lines often express the target at levels that do not match primary tissue. A GLP-1 receptor assay in a transfected line can confirm agonist activity without telling you how a primary cell will respond. A GHRH-R assay in a pituitary-derived line is closer to the relevant tissue, but still not the whole animal.

**Matrix effects.** Serum contains proteases that degrade many peptides within hours. A peptide that looks potent in serum-free medium may be inactive in 10% serum. The reverse can also happen: serum proteins can bind a hydrophobic peptide and change its effective concentration. Assay conditions belong in the methods section, and they belong in the supplier conversation if the supplier is making activity claims.

**Adsorption and solubility.** Cationic and hydrophobic peptides adsorb to plastic and to filters. A nominal concentration prepared in a polypropylene tube may be lower than the concentration that reaches the cells. This is a common source of failed replication, and it is not visible in a purity certificate.

**Species cross-reactivity.** A peptide sequence that matches the human receptor may bind the mouse receptor with different affinity. GHRH, GLP-1, and ghrelin receptor sequences are not identical across species. A rodent study is not automatically a model of human receptor pharmacology. It is a model of rodent receptor pharmacology.

**Controls and blinding.** Vehicle controls, scrambled-sequence controls, and blinded quantification are basic requirements. A study that compares a peptide to "no treatment" rather than to a scrambled sequence cannot separate sequence-specific effects from handling effects.

## What a class does and does not imply

| A class label can imply | A class label does not imply |
|---|---|
| A plausible receptor family or mechanism | That the batch contains the intended sequence |
| A body of literature to read | That the literature applies to your model |
| A set of likely impurities to test for | That the batch meets a purity specification |
| A general formulation challenge | That the supplied salt form is suitable |
| A commercial category | That two products are interchangeable |
| A general regulatory context | That the product is approved or legal in your jurisdiction |

The last row deserves emphasis: research-use-only status says nothing about safety or legality of import. It describes intended use. A peptide that is an approved drug in one country can be a controlled or prescription-only substance in another. A cosmetic ingredient can be restricted in a finished product. The [research-use disclaimer](/research-use-disclaimer/) sets out the site's position; it is not a substitute for local legal advice.

Class membership also does not imply batch consistency; two batches of the same sequence can differ in counterion, water content, impurity profile, and endotoxin. The site's [quality page](/peptide-quality/) and the [COA reading guide](/blog/how-to-read-a-peptide-coa-a-researchers-guide-to-hplc-and-ms-reports/) explain what to check.

## Sourcing implications by class

Class determines which analytical questions matter. A generic "98% pure" certificate is not equally informative across these groups.

| Cosmetic peptides | Copper content, color, peptide content, preservative compatibility | ICP-MS, HPLC, UV/vis, microbial limits | Copper-to-peptide ratio off | What is the copper content by ICP-MS? |
| Repair peptides | Sequence, salt form, endotoxin, sterility | HPLC, LC-MS, LAL, sterility | Endotoxin or substitution | Which salt form? What is the endotoxin result? |
| Bioregulators | Exact short sequence, salt content, water content | HPLC with ion-pairing, LC-MS/MS, Karl Fischer | Salt dominates the powder | What is the peptide content, not just HPLC purity? |
| Mitochondrial peptides | Hydrophobicity, aggregation, counterion | HPLC, LC-MS, solubility, endotoxin | Aggregation or adsorption | Is there a solubility protocol? What is the counterion? |
| Blends | Per-component identity and ratio, homogeneity | Orthogonal HPLC, LC-MS/MS, homogeneity sampling | Co-elution or ratio drift | Can you assay each component separately? |

Three implications cut across the table.

**First, the assay must match the modification.** A lipidated, PEGylated, or disulfide-bonded peptide cannot be characterized by a method designed for a linear unmodified peptide. If the supplier's certificate shows only a single purity percentage and a mass that matches the backbone, ask what else was tested.

**Second, the salt form is part of the product.** Acetate and trifluoroacetate salts have different mass balances and different effects on cell assays. Residual TFA can be cytotoxic at sufficient concentration. A certificate that does not report counterion or residual solvent is incomplete for cell work.

**Third, endotoxin is class-independent but use-dependent.** Any peptide used in cell culture or animals needs an endotoxin specification; a cosmetic-grade batch may have none. The site's [endotoxin testing overview](/blog/endotoxin-testing-peptide-manufacturing/) explains the assay and the units.

The [quality page](/peptide-quality/) and [HPLC versus LC-MS comparison](/blog/hplc-vs-lc-ms-peptides/) cover the analytical vocabulary; the [custom synthesis page](/custom-peptide-services/) applies when a catalog sequence does not exist.

## A class-by-class verification workflow

1. **Write the identity block.** Full sequence, terminal modifications, non-natural residues, lipid or PEG chains, and intended salt form. If the supplier cannot provide this, stop.
2. **Classify the evidence.** Approved drug, investigational compound, preclinical tool, or cosmetic ingredient. Match the literature you read to the correct category.
3. **Choose the assays.** Identity by mass, purity by a method that resolves likely impurities, counterion and water content, endotoxin if relevant, and a functional assay if the claim is functional.
4. **Request the batch record.** Look for a lot number, a date, the method, the column and gradient, the integration method, and the specification limits. A certificate without a lot number is a template.
5. **Check the salt and solubility.** Confirm the counterion and test solubility in the intended vehicle. A peptide that precipitates at the working concentration is not usable, regardless of purity.
6. **Confirm the supplier's scope.** Does the supplier manufacture or resell, and can it retest or replace a failed batch? The [how-it-works page](/how-it-works/) explains this platform's model; the [FAQ](/faq/) covers order-level questions.

Shipping is covered by the [shipping and reshipment policy](/shipping-and-reshipment-policy/), payment by [how to pay](/how-to-pay/); neither substitutes for batch-level verification.

## FAQ

### What is the difference between a peptide class and a peptide product?

A class is a group of molecules that share a mechanism or structural theme. A product is a specific sequence, salt form, purity grade, and batch. The class helps you choose which assays and literature are relevant. The product specification tells you what is in the vial.

### Are GHRH and GHRP interchangeable?

No. GHRH analogs act on the pituitary GHRH receptor and signal through cAMP/PKA; GHRPs act on the ghrelin receptor GHS-R1a and signal through Gq/PLC. They can produce additive or synergistic growth hormone responses together, but they are different tools with different off-target profiles and analytical specifications.

### Is every GLP-1 receptor agonist the same?

No. The class includes single GLP-1 agonists, dual GIP/GLP-1 agonists, triple agonists, GLP-1/glucagon co-agonists, and amylin analogs that are often grouped commercially with GLP-1 products. Even within single agonists, fatty-acid acylation, substitutions, and formulation differ. Match the receptor profile to the assay, not the shelf label.

### Do cosmetic peptides work like research repair peptides?

They are different use categories. Cosmetic peptides are formulated for topical products and are studied for skin-cell signaling, penetration, and stability. Repair-signaling peptides such as BPC-157 and TB-500 are studied in preclinical tissue models. Evidence from one category does not transfer to the other, and a cosmetic-grade specification is not a research-grade one.

### Are bioregulator peptides supported by independent clinical evidence?

The literature is real but narrow. Much of it comes from a small number of research groups and relies on in vitro and animal models, with limited independent replication. The mechanistic hypothesis is testable, but buyers should treat claims of tissue-specific gene regulation as a research question, not an established clinical fact.

### How is SS-31 different from MOTS-c?

SS-31 is a synthetic tetrapeptide that binds cardiolipin in the inner mitochondrial membrane. MOTS-c is a 16-residue peptide encoded in mitochondrial DNA and studied through metabolic signaling pathways. One is mitochondria-targeted; the other is mitochondria-derived.

### Can a blend be tested like a single peptide?

Not with a single-peak purity method. A blend needs per-component identity, per-component assay, ratio confirmation, homogeneity testing, and a stability protocol that monitors each component. A combined HPLC peak may hide co-elution. A mass ratio on the label is not the same as a molar ratio.

### What single document should I ask for first?

The full sequence and modification record, followed by a batch-specific certificate with lot number, method, and specification limits. If a supplier cannot provide the sequence and the lot-linked certificate, the rest of the documentation is not verifiable. The [COA guide](/blog/how-to-read-a-peptide-coa-a-researchers-guide-to-hplc-and-ms-reports/) explains which fields matter.

## The Author's Take

**Position.** The fastest way to buy the wrong peptide is to buy a category. I would rather see a buyer ask for a sequence, a counterion, and a lot-linked assay than ask for "the best GLP-1" or "a reliable GHRP." The category is the beginning of due diligence, not the end of it.

**Reasoning.** The classes here are real, and the mechanistic distinctions matter. GHRH and GHRP really do act on different receptors. GLP-1, GIP, glucagon, and amylin really are different targets. SS-31 and MOTS-c really do come from different research traditions. But the commercial market uses these labels as sorting bins, and sorting bins do not preserve the information that determines whether an experiment will work: sequence fidelity, terminal modifications, salt form, impurity profile, endotoxin, and batch consistency. A buyer who knows the class but not the specification has learned the vocabulary without learning the language.
